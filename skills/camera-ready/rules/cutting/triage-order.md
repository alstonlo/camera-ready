# Triage order

Under a length constraint, cut in this order, most expendable first.

1. **Repeated restatement.** Cut repetitions of the same point in the introduction, section or chapter opening, and conclusion. Keep the instance that contributes most to the argument, usually in the section that develops the point. Preserve two kinds of repetition: one restatement of each contribution in the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, and a deliberate reminder that [refreshes a shorthand](../../references/guide.md#terminology) defined pages earlier. Even a permitted restatement must not be a copy. If a conclusion sentence repeats the abstract word for word, revise it with the specifics established by the results, or flag it.
2. **Signposting that survives the bloat pass.** This includes the two-sentence roadmap allowed by the [rule on combining signposts with content](../../references/guide.md#concision). Preserve references to a claim's support or to the location of a proof; these are not signposting.
3. **Tangential background and related work.** Cut material that does not motivate the text's question. A citation list that states no relation to the gap the text fills is expendable; the two or three works that define the gap are not.
4. **Minor results, secondary analyses, and exploratory findings.** Remove a result from the main text if it advances no central claim. Relocate material that supports rigor: sensitivity analyses, robustness checks, and the negative result a skeptical reviewer will ask about. Accounts of what the authors tried first also belong in this tier. A failed alternative stays in the main text only as a stated negative result.
5. **Exhaustive parameter and implementation detail.** Keep enough detail in the main text to make the work reproducible in outline, including its [spelled-out procedures](../../references/guide.md#plain-wording) and [protected content](../../references/guide.md#protect). Move the full sweep to the appendix.
