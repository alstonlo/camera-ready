# Compare prior work fairly and state specific differences

End each paragraph or group of paragraphs about prior work with a checkable difference in an assumption, condition, scope, metric, measured result, cost, or guarantee. A comparison that is vague on both sides is a [false concession](../deslopping/false-concession.md). Use only the draft's account of each source. Flag missing context or differences instead of supplying them. Explain both sides enough for readers to judge the comparison. State what each source contributes and assumes before criticizing it, and preserve its [credit](../cutting/protected-sentences.md). Do not write "ignores" or "fails to" for a difference in scope unless the draft shows that the source failed. Keep a source's tentative verbs. Attribute each claim, concession, and reported objection to whoever holds it, not to the paper. For surveys, see Details.

## Details

A brief summary or isolated excerpt may tell readers too little about a source to justify a detailed criticism. Do not criticize a source with words such as "ignores" or "fails to" merely because it studies a different problem or set of cases. When reporting a source's claims, keep its tentative verbs: for example, do not strengthen "suggests" to "shows". Do not turn a source's claim, concession, or reported objection into a claim that the paper itself makes.

In a survey, end the discussion of each group of related sources by stating the pattern, disagreement, or gap they establish. Authors can organize established knowledge without claiming a new discovery.

For proprietary products, distinguish what documentation says the product can do from what remains undisclosed about how it works. Do not infer an unknown mechanism or assume that a product's existence invalidates a research contribution.

## Examples

**Example 1.** Lee et al. deliberately limited their study to summer rainfall. Good describes that scope without implying that excluding winter was a failure.

> **Bad.** Lee et al. ignore winter rainfall; we examine rainfall in both summer and winter.
>
> **Good.** Lee et al. examine only summer rainfall; we examine rainfall in both summer and winter.

**Example 2.** The draft reports that Lee concedes that the coating costs too much. The authors do not endorse that judgment, so Good attributes the concession to Lee.

> **Bad.** Lee argues that the coating lasts longer. Although it costs too much, Lee favors it; we compare measured costs.
>
> **Good.** Lee argues that the coating lasts longer. Although Lee concedes that it costs too much, Lee favors it; we compare measured costs.

**Example 3.** This is a survey, and the draft confirms both studies' scope. Good closes with the gap they share without claiming a discovery by the survey's authors.

> **Bad.** Lee measures summer rainfall. Rao also measures summer rainfall.
>
> **Good.** Lee measures summer rainfall. Rao also measures summer rainfall. Both studies leave winter rainfall unmeasured.

**Example 4.** The draft states that the commercial filter removes salt and that its internal design is undisclosed.

> **Bad.** The commercial filter removes salt with the same membrane as ours; our membrane design is public.
>
> **Good.** The commercial filter removes salt, but its internal design is undisclosed; our membrane design is public.
