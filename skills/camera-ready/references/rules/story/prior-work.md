# Compare prior work fairly on named dimensions

End each paragraph or group of paragraphs about prior work with a checkable difference in an assumption, metric, cost, or guarantee. Describe each source only as the draft does; flag missing context or a missing difference rather than supplying it. Explain both sides enough to judge the comparison, stating each source's contribution and assumptions before criticizing it. Do not write "ignores" or "fails to" for a difference in scope unless the draft shows failure. Preserve [credit](../cutting/protect.md) and a source's "suggests" rather than turning it into "shows". Keep each position attributed to whoever holds it, including concessions and reported objections; a source's claim must not become the paper's assertion. For survey synthesis, see Details. A comparison that is vague on both sides is the [false concession](../deslopping/false-concession.md) pattern.

## Details

Possible comparison dimensions include assumptions, guarantees, evaluation conditions, measurements, resource costs, and supported uses. Enough explanation on each side does not mean equal word counts for every component.

A criticism needs the relevant claim, evidence, and conditions. A broad summary or an isolated fragment of a source cannot support a detailed criticism without that context.

An author can report a rival view, concede a limited point, or reject a claim without endorsing it. Preserve those roles throughout a source summary, including when changing its sentence structure.

In a survey, end a group of source discussions with the pattern, disagreement, or gap that the draft supports. A survey that orients readers can organize established knowledge without claiming a new discovery. Such an ending is the survey form of comparison; it does not demand a novel result.

If the draft compares a proprietary system, retain its stated distinction between documented capabilities and undisclosed internals. Do not fill in an explicitly unknown mechanism from memory, or infer that a product's existence invalidates a research contribution.

## Examples

The draft states that Lee et al. deliberately limited their study to summer rainfall; Good is the proposed revision for the author.

- Bad: Lee et al. ignore winter rainfall; we examine rainfall in both summer and winter.
- Good: Lee et al. examine only summer rainfall; we examine rainfall in both summer and winter.
