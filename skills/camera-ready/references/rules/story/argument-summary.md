# Test the argument with five lines and one sentence

Write one line each for the problem, the gap and its cost, the idea, the evidence, and the implication, and flag any line the draft does not supply. Combine the idea and evidence into core claims for the [spine test](../cutting/spine-test.md).

Combine the five lines into one test sentence: "A matters, and B is known, but C fails because D; therefore we propose E, which achieves F." The lines and the sentence are notes, not text for the paper. If the sentence lists facts without a problem or gap, flag what is missing. If it requires unrelated problems, flag the competing focuses and ask which one the text addresses, without inventing a connection. Judge both cases by meaning, not by counting "but": a gap need not use that word, and several "but" clauses may describe obstacles to answering one question.

## Details

The five lines are your notes for checking the argument. Do not insert them into the paper. Write each line using only what the draft states. The gap is what existing work leaves unresolved, and its cost is why that gap matters. The spine test uses the core claims to check whether each paragraph supports the argument. If the draft omits part of a line, such as the gap's cost, write the rest of the line and flag the missing part for the author.

## Examples

**Edit 1.** Suppose the draft says that the missing storm records leave peak water levels unknown.

> **Before.** Gap and cost: The tide gauge stops recording during storms.

> **After.** Gap and cost: The tide gauge stops recording during storms and leaves peak water levels unknown.

**Edit 2.**

> **Before.** Gap and cost: The tide gauge stops recording during storms, so flood warnings fail.

> **After.** Gap and cost: The tide gauge stops recording during storms. Flag for the author: The draft does not state why the missing records matter.

The failed flood warnings appear in the editor's summary, not in the draft. The correction removes that invented consequence from the notes.

**Edit 3.** Imagine the authors propose the lid and report that it halves evaporation in the tested tank.

> **Before.** Core claim: We propose and test a lid.

> **After.** Core claim: The lid halves evaporation in the tested tank.

**Edit 4.** Suppose the draft states elsewhere that existing window screens fail to keep small flies out.

> **Before.** Keeping flies out protects stored food; we propose a finer window screen that blocks all flies in our trial.

> **After.** Keeping flies out protects stored food, but existing window screens let small flies through; we propose a finer window screen that blocks all flies in our trial.

**Edit 5.** Imagine the draft gives no connection between the two problems.

> **Before.** Small flies and overheating make homes uncomfortable, so we propose a finer window screen and a reflective roof paint.

> **After.** Flag for the author: The summary can be written only by naming two unrelated problems, small flies and overheating roofs, so the draft has two competing focuses.
