# Match each section to its part of the argument

Check that each section does its part of the argument. The introduction states the problem, gap, idea, and contributions. Background gives only the context needed for the gap and comparisons. Methods explains the idea, Results gives the evidence, and Discussion interprets the evidence, limitations, and alternatives. The conclusion states the implications. Keep combined or repeated sections if the argument stays clear, and follow venue rules. Each result must follow the method description it needs (when Methods comes last, see Details). State the contributions in the first quarter of the text. If a central result first appears later or sections need reordering, flag the problem instead of moving sections.

The abstract states all five parts of the argument within the venue's word limit, using recognizable terms for the problem, system, and technique. Readers must understand it without the body or bibliography. Remove a reference to a section or figure only when the abstract already states that result. Flag a missing part or result, or needed content that exceeds the limit. For proposals, surveys, and abstract citations, see Details.

## Details

When the venue places Methods after Results, keep a brief description of the procedure with each result so that readers can interpret the result, and refer readers to Methods for the full procedure. When Methods precedes Results, flag any substantial procedure first described in Results. A small result that validates a procedure may stay beside that procedure in Methods if it needs no separate discussion. A proposal describes the evidence that the authors plan to gather, not evidence that they already have.

The five parts of the argument in the abstract are the problem, the gap, the idea, the evidence, and the implication. When readers need a cited work to understand the abstract, identify the work in the abstract itself. Use the authors and year supplied by the draft when the venue permits that format, and flag any missing information needed to identify the work. A survey abstract may state what the reviewed studies establish together.

## Examples

**Example 1.** The venue places Methods last, and that section supplies the procedure added here. Good gives enough of the procedure in Results to explain how depth was measured and points to the full account.

> **Bad.** The lake is 12 m deep at the sampling point.
>
> **Good.** We obtain depth from calibrated echo times (Methods). The lake is 12 m deep at the sampling point.

**Example 2.** The central result first appears in Results, after the paper's first quarter. Good is a proposed addition to the introduction; the editor flags the delay because readers need the contribution earlier.

> **Bad.** We test a lid to reduce water loss.
>
> **Good.** We test a lid to reduce water loss. The lid halves water loss in the tested tank.

**Example 3.** This abstract omits a result established in the body. Good proposes stating it so readers can understand the finding without following a section reference; the editor flags the missing result.

> **Bad.** The filter's result appears in Section 5.
>
> **Good.** The filter removes all visible sand from the tested samples.

**Example 4.** The drying procedure is substantial, while the calibration result only validates the procedure beside it. Good proposes moving the misplaced procedure while keeping the small validation result in Methods; the editor flags the move for the author.

> **Bad.** Methods: temperature calibration and validation. Results: drying procedure and drying results.
>
> **Good.** Methods: temperature calibration and validation; drying procedure. Results: drying results.
