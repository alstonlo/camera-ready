# Map argument stages to section functions

Check section functions: the introduction states the problem, gap, idea, and contributions; background supplies only the context that the gap and comparison need; methods explain the idea; results supply evidence; discussion interprets evidence, limits, and alternatives; the conclusion gives implications. Keep combined or repeated sections when the argument remains coherent, and follow venue requirements. Check that each result follows the method context needed to understand it; for formats that put methods last, see Details. Make the contributions clear within the first quarter of the text. If a central result first appears after that point, or if the sections should be reordered, flag the problem instead of moving sections. A proposal describes the evidence it plans to gather.

The abstract states all five stages within the venue's word limit, using recognizable terms for the task and technique. It must be understandable without the body or bibliography; remove a pointer to a body section or figure only when the abstract already states that result. Flag a missing stage or result, or necessary content that cannot fit the limit; for a citation the abstract needs, see Details.

## Details

The section map is a conventional guide: Abstract covers the whole argument; Introduction motivates the question and identifies the contribution; Background enables understanding; Method develops the idea; Results supplies evidence; Discussion interprets that evidence; Conclusion explains its implications. These functions do not require separate sections or fixed labels.

A result can motivate a later method, a theory paper can pair definitions with results and proofs, and a survey can organize evidence by questions. Put each part after the material needed to understand it, rather than following the chronology of the research.

In a methods-last format, retain the short procedure description that makes a result interpretable and point to the existing full method. In a methods-first format, flag a substantial procedure introduced only with its results. A small validation result may accompany the procedure it justifies when it needs no separate discussion.

Theses, reports, and proposals may use their own section names. Describe planned work as planned evidence rather than as a completed finding.

Read the abstract as if the body, figures, and bibliography were unavailable. Select the contribution and evidence that let readers judge relevance, retaining enough scope to understand the result. Several argument stages can share a sentence; the abstract need not fill its word limit with a list of experiments.

For a missing abstract stage or result, propose a sentence supported by the draft in a flag rather than adding it. Remove a section or figure pointer only when the abstract already states the result. If necessary content cannot fit the venue limit, identify the competing content for the author.

Use recognizable terms for the task and technique, rather than only a coined system name. Follow the [acronym rule](../conventions/acronyms.md).

If the abstract depends on a particular cited work, identify it in words that make sense without the bibliography, using authors and year supplied by the draft where venue requirements permit. Flag missing identifying information. A survey abstract may state what the surveyed studies establish collectively.
