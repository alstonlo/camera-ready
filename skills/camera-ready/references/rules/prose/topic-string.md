# Check sentence openings with the topic string

When individual sentences are clear but the paragraph is hard to follow, check its connections:

1. List each sentence's opening words (the topic string). Check that they advance the paragraph's point rather than merely repeat words. If concepts share a theme but their connection is unclear, state it from the draft or flag the gap. Consecutive sentences may keep the same topic, and a new term at a sentence's end need not recur unless the text promises to explain it. If most sentences start with new material, rewrite them to start with [old information](old-before-new.md).
2. Insert the implied connective in brackets at each sentence boundary ("[however]", "[therefore]"). If no connective fits or you cannot tell which fits, supply the missing link from the draft or flag it. If "[therefore]" fits but a reader outside the subfield could not explain why the second sentence follows, flag the missing step in the argument.
3. If two adjacent paragraphs could swap places without other edits, add a [summary naming their parallel items](direct-content.md) when they present parallel items, or state how the second depends on the first.

## Details

- Bracketed connectives: These are only a test; the edited text does not need a connecting word between every pair of sentences.
- Paragraph openings and endings: Check that they fit what the paragraph contributes to the section.
- Unclear connections: Check both sentences. The earlier one may create an expectation the later one does not meet. Before moving or deleting ideas, follow [cutting permissions](../cutting/idea-cuts.md).

## Examples

**Edit 1.** Suppose the draft says elsewhere that the lid keeps rain out.

> **Before.** The box has a lid. The tools stay dry.

> **After.** The box has a lid that prevents rain from entering. The tools stay dry.

**Edit 2.**

> **Before.** The jar holds salt. A tight seal keeps the jar dry. The jar fits on the shelf.

> **After.** The jar holds salt. The jar stays dry because of a tight seal. The jar fits on the shelf.

Mentioning salt does not promise a discussion of it. The jar can remain the paragraph's topic.

**Edit 3.** Suppose the draft describes a box.

> **Before.** The lid has a gasket.
>
> The base has a drain.

> **After.** The box has two features: a gasket in the lid and a drain in the base.
>
> The lid has a gasket.
>
> The base has a drain.

**Edit 4.** Suppose the draft requires draining to finish before drying begins.

> **Before.** We open the valve to drain the tank.
>
> We dry the empty tank with warm air.

> **After.** We open the valve to drain the tank.
>
> After draining, we dry the empty tank with warm air.
