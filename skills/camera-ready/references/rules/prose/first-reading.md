# Make every sentence clear on the first reading

Rewrite any sentence that makes readers reread it. Length alone is not a defect. Put subjects next to verbs and verbs next to their objects or complements. Shorten a long opening phrase or clause. Move a long subject clause or interruption toward the end unless it states old information, and keep short, familiar asides. Rewrite strings of three or more nouns and long modifier chains, counting an established technical name as one noun. Read from left to right for words that mislead at first, such as "and" or "or" joining the wrong items. Make clear which noun a relative clause or verb-form phrase describes. Keep or restore articles and "that" when needed to prevent misreading, except in short, predictable clauses or after "we have" (see Details). Put "only" next to what it restricts. Put a condition before its claim unless it is short and clear at the end. Give a long condition its own sentence, and list three or more steps or conditions instead of packing them into one sentence. If a sentence still packs in too many concepts, follow [definitions and procedures](definitions-procedures.md).

## Details

In a string of nouns such as "roof water drain cover", find the main noun ("cover") and use prepositions or clauses to show how the other nouns relate to it. Count an established technical name, such as "scanning electron microscope", as one noun. When either phrase order works, put the longer phrase last unless it states old information.

Restore "that" after verbs such as "assume", "show", or "find" when readers could at first take the next noun as the verb's direct object, as in "We assume the samples ...". Also check whether "that" is needed in a long relative clause whose verb takes the described noun as its object ("the seal that the technician repaired after the pump failed") or completes an adjective ("certain that ..."), but do not add optional words to short, predictable clauses or "that" after "we have". Use "in order to" when a bare "to" could look like a value rather than a purpose.

In a shortened clause such as "the samples stored in the cabinet", readers may take "stored" for the sentence's main verb. Restore "that were" or "which were" when this misreading is possible. For a nested or trailing "which" or "that" clause, identify the noun each clause describes, then repeat that noun, join parallel clauses with "and" or "or", or split the sentence. Any summary noun used to refer back must keep the earlier statement's [claim status](ambiguous-this.md); do not call a conjecture a finding.

If an aside contains its own commas and punctuation alone leaves the main clause hard to find, restructure the sentence. For a participial phrase (one beginning with a verb form such as "wearing gloves" or "stored in ice"), make clear who performs or undergoes the action, and whether the phrase states means, time, condition, or consequence. Flag the relation if the draft leaves it unclear. When you turn three or more steps or conditions into a numbered list, or when you split or repunctuate a sentence, keep each condition attached to the same claims as before. Also recheck the subject of each joined verb, the noun each modifier describes, and the [emphasis of findings and qualifications](stress-position.md).

A readability score does not measure whether readers in the field can follow a sentence. Do not replace an accurate technical term merely to improve a score.

## Examples

**Example 1.**

> **Bad.** The survey, which asked residents about their heating costs, insulation, window seals, and thermostat settings, took 10 min.
>
> **Good.** The survey took 10 min. It asked residents about their heating costs, insulation, window seals, and thermostat settings.

**Example 2.**

> **Bad.** We put, after checking each label and recording each mass, the jars on the shelf.
>
> **Good.** After checking each label and recording each mass, we put the jars on the shelf.

**Example 3.**

> **Bad.** After checking each label, which records the date and mass, and recording the lid type, we weigh the jars.
>
> **Good.** We check each label, which records the date and mass, and record the lid type. Then we weigh the jars.

**Example 4.**

> **Bad.** That the lid leaks whenever pressure exceeds the limit is clear from the test.
>
> **Good.** The test makes clear that the lid leaks whenever pressure exceeds the limit.

**Example 5.** The preceding text has already discussed whether the lid leaks and has introduced trial 2.

> **Bad.** That the lid leaks is, as trial 2 shows, certain. The tank, which holds water from the stream beside the shed, stays full.
>
> **Good.** That the lid leaks is, as trial 2 shows, certain. The tank stays full. It holds water from the stream beside the shed.

**Example 6.**

> **Bad.** We inspect the roof water drain cover.
>
> **Good.** We inspect the cover of the drain for roof water.

**Example 7.** The draft uses the established instrument name “scanning electron microscope”.

> **Bad.** We inspect the scanning electron microscope sample holder cover.
>
> **Good.** We inspect the cover of the sample holder for the scanning electron microscope.

**Example 8.**

> **Bad.** We test the newly installed, manually operated, difficult-to-reach valve.
>
> **Good.** We test the newly installed valve, which is manually operated and difficult to reach.

**Example 9.** The draft identifies the cup, not the tray, as cracked.

> **Bad.** We remove the cup from the tray that is cracked.
>
> **Good.** We remove the cracked cup from the tray.

**Example 10.** The draft says the technician, not the vial, was wearing gloves.

> **Bad.** Wearing gloves, the vial was opened by the technician.
>
> **Good.** Wearing gloves, the technician opened the vial.

**Example 11.**

> **Bad.** We rinse the jars and the lids dry on a rack.
>
> **Good.** We rinse the jars, and the lids dry on a rack.

**Example 12.**

> **Bad.** We assume the samples in the red tray contain salt.
>
> **Good.** We assume that the samples in the red tray contain salt.

**Example 13.**

> **Bad.** The samples stored in the cabinet near the door leaked.
>
> **Good.** The samples that were stored in the cabinet near the door leaked.

**Example 14.**

> **Bad.** We place sample beside scale.
>
> **Good.** We place the sample beside the scale.

**Example 15.** The draft says the technician weighs jars and no other objects.

> **Bad.** The technician only weighs jars.
>
> **Good.** The technician weighs only jars.

**Example 16.**

> **Bad.** The valve opens and the lamp flashes if pressure exceeds 20 kPa.
>
> **Good.** If pressure exceeds 20 kPa, the valve opens and the lamp flashes.

**Example 17.**

> **Bad.** Open the valve only if the lid has remained closed throughout the heating cycle and the pressure is below the marked limit.
>
> **Good.** The lid must have remained closed throughout the heating cycle, and the pressure must be below the marked limit. Open the valve only if both conditions hold.

**Example 18.** In this example, ↵ marks a line break.

> **Bad.** Open the valve only if the lid is shut, the pump is off, and the tank is cool.
>
> **Good.** Open the valve only if all three conditions hold: ↵ 1. The lid is shut. ↵ 2. The pump is off. ↵ 3. The tank is cool.

**Example 19.** The draft says the authors choose the rate with the aim of doubling the output.

> **Bad.** We set the rate to double the output.
>
> **Good.** We set the rate in order to double the output.

**Example 20.**

> **Bad.** We test the seal the technician repaired after the pump failed during the first trial.
>
> **Good.** We test the seal that the technician repaired after the pump failed during the first trial.
