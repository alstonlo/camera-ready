# Make every sentence clear on the first reading

Repair what forces rereading; length alone is not a defect. Bring subjects next to verbs and verbs next to complements; shorten an overloaded introduction. Move a long subject clause or interruption later unless it carries old information; keep short familiar asides. Unpack stacks of three or more nouns and long chains of modifiers, counting an established technical compound as one unit. Clarify which noun a trailing relative clause describes and what each participle modifies. Read left to right for false starts, including coordination that first seems to join the wrong items. Keep or restore articles and clause markers such as "that" after "assume", "show", or "find" when their absence permits a misreading; do not add optional words to short predictable clauses, or "that" after "we have". Put "only" beside what it restricts. Place conditions before a claim unless a short trailing condition is unambiguous. Give long conditions their own sentence, and enumerate three or more steps or conditions packed into one sentence; preserve their scope when splitting. For omitted markers or unclear modifier attachment, use the page's diagnostics. If grammatical prose still compresses too many concepts, unpack the explanation under [definitions and procedures](definitions-procedures.md).

## Details

An opening can overload the reader before the subject even arrives. Preserve a short backward link when moving the main clause earlier, but separate introductory qualifications that require explanations of their own. Keep a verb near the phrase it requires, as well as near its object.

For a noun stack, identify the head noun and state how the other nouns relate to it, using prepositions or clauses. Treat an established multiword technical term as one unit instead of splitting it arbitrarily. When grammar permits either phrase order, put the longer phrase last unless it carries the old information.

Restore a clause marker after a reporting or assumption verb when the following noun could initially seem to be its object. Other cases include a long relative clause modifying an object, a difficult clause completing an adjective's meaning, and a purpose phrase that could first seem to specify a value. Use "in order to" when "to" alone creates that last misreading. Keep articles; deleting them can make prose read like compressed notes.

A relative clause with its pronoun and auxiliary omitted can first look like the main predicate. Restore its relative pronoun and auxiliary when needed. For nested or trailing relatives, identify the noun each clause describes, then repeat it, coordinate equivalent clauses, or split the sentence. If a short summary noun replaces a clause, preserve its claim status under [ambiguous this](ambiguous-this.md).

Internal commas can hide the boundaries of an aside. Restructure an interruption when changing its punctuation still leaves the main clause hard to find. For a participial phrase, identify its implied subject and whether it expresses means, time, condition, or consequence. Resolve the attachment from the draft; flag an unsettled relation.

After a punctuation change, check the subject of every coordinated verb and the attachment of every modifier. Splitting a sentence must preserve logical relations and the [emphasis of findings and qualifications](stress-position.md). A readability score, short sentence, or low syllable count does not establish comprehension; do not replace an accurate technical term to improve such a score.

## Examples

- Bad: The survey, which asked residents about their heating costs, insulation, window seals, and thermostat settings, took 10 min.
- Good: The survey took 10 min. It asked residents about their heating costs, insulation, window seals, and thermostat settings.
