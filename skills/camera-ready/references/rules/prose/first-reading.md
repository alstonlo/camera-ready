# Make every sentence clear on the first reading

Length alone is not a defect. Put subjects next to verbs and verbs next to their objects or required phrases ("compare X with Y"). Shorten long opening phrases or clauses. Move a long subject clause toward the end ("It remains open whether the gain survives at larger scale") and, when grammar allows either order, put the longer of two phrases last, unless the long part states old information. Keep short, familiar asides. Move an interruption that introduces new material or a full clause to the beginning or end, make it a separate sentence, or cut it if it adds nothing, unless it states old information readers need there. Never nest one such interruption inside another before the main verb.

Rewrite strings of three or more nouns with a preposition or clause. Break up long sequences of modifiers, usually more than three words before the head noun. Count an established technical name ("large language model") as one noun.

Keep articles so that sentences read as prose, not notes. Keep optional words that mark the start of a long or hard-to-predict clause. Restore "that" after verbs such as "assume", "suppose", "show", "prove", or "find" when readers could first take the next noun as the verb's object; after other verbs, adjectives, and conjunctions when a long clause follows; and before a long relative clause. Restore "that were" in a shortened clause such as "the samples stored in the cabinet" when readers could take "stored" as the main verb. Use "in order to" when bare "to" could look like a value ("we limit the batch size in order to fit in memory"). Do not add optional words to short, predictable clauses, or "that" after "we have".

Read from left to right for words that mislead at first: "We prove that A and B imply C" first reads as proving A, so write "We prove that the two conditions A and B together imply C". Make clear which noun a relative clause or verb-form phrase describes. Put "only" immediately before the word it restricts: "We only evaluate on two datasets" restricts the verb; "We evaluate on only two datasets" restricts the count, which is usually intended. Put a condition before its claim unless it is short and clear at the end. Give a long condition its own sentence, and list three or more steps or conditions instead of packing them into one sentence. If a sentence still contains too many concepts, follow [definitions and procedures](definitions-procedures.md).

## Details

- Noun strings: Find the main noun first ("cover" in "roof water drain cover") to identify the object named.
- Relative clauses: For nested or trailing "which" or "that" clauses, identify the noun each describes. Repeat the noun, join parallel clauses with "and" or "or", or split the sentence. A summary noun that refers back must preserve [claim status](referents.md); do not call a conjecture a finding.
- Asides: Restructure the sentence if an aside contains commas and punctuation alone leaves the main clause hard to find.
- Verb-form phrases: For phrases such as "wearing gloves" or "stored in ice", make clear who performs or undergoes the action and whether the phrase states means, time, condition, or consequence. Flag a relation the draft leaves unclear.
- Restructuring: When turning three or more steps or conditions into a numbered list, or splitting or repunctuating a sentence, keep each condition attached to the same claims. Recheck the subject of each joined verb, the noun each modifier describes, and the [emphasis of findings and qualifications](stress-position.md).
- Readability scores: These do not measure whether readers in the field can follow a sentence. Do not replace an accurate technical term merely to improve a score.

## Examples

**Edit 1.** Suppose the preceding text discusses the leak and introduces trial 2.

> **Before.** That the lid leaks is, as trial 2 shows, certain. The tank, which holds water from the stream beside the shed, stays full.

> **After.** That the lid leaks is, as trial 2 shows, certain. The tank stays full. It holds water from the stream beside the shed.

The first sentence keeps the familiar leak as its subject. Its short aside refers to a familiar trial and is easy to follow.

**Edit 2.**

> **Before.** We inspect the scanning electron microscope sample holder cover.

> **After.** We inspect the cover of the sample holder for the scanning electron microscope.

The words "scanning electron microscope" stay together because they name one instrument.

**Edit 3.**

> **Before.** Wearing gloves, the vial was opened by the technician.

> **After.** Wearing gloves, the technician opened the vial.

**Edit 4.**

> **Before.** We assume the samples in the red tray contain salt.

> **After.** We assume that the samples in the red tray contain salt.

**Edit 5.**

> **Before.** Open the valve only if the lid is shut, the pump is off, and the tank is cool.

> **After.** Open the valve only if all three conditions hold:
> 1. The lid is shut.
> 2. The pump is off.
> 3. The tank is cool.
