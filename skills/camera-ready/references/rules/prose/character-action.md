# Put actors in subjects and actions in verbs

A nominalization is an action expressed as a noun. When a phrase pairs a weak verb with a nominalization ("perform an evaluation of", "lead to an improvement in"), or when several nominalizations occur in one clause, put the actor in the subject and the action in the verb. Keep a nominalization that names a technique ("quantization") or a concept the text keeps discussing, that refers back to the previous sentence ("This reduction in noise ..."), that replaces an awkward "the fact that" clause, or that appears alone in an otherwise clear sentence. Preserve every action; if the draft never states who performs an action, flag the gap. With an inanimate subject, replace a verb that requires intention, thought, perception, feeling, memory, or eating ("hopes", "forgets", "swallows") with a verb that names the literal event. Keep established technical usage, such as an enzyme recognizing its substrate, a sensor detecting light, or a function expecting input, and keep a component, instrument, or organism as the subject of the operation it performs. A result or figure may be the subject of a [reporting verb](../claims/verb-ladder.md) ("the results suggest"). Follow [voice](../conventions/active-voice.md) and [information flow](passives-fronting-inversions.md).

## Details

Nominalizations often end in -tion, -ment, -ance, or -ing. To locate obscured actions, list the main verbs in a passage; repeated "is", "has", or "leads to" invites inspection, though not automatic rewriting. In theoretical work, an abstract concept may legitimately serve as the subject.

Keep conventional computing terms such as a model learning. When the draft describes a specification as making a decision, the sentence may need the actual actor in the subject; distinguish a document, which cannot act, from a component that performs its own operation. Take the literal event from the draft without inferring new system behavior or assigning blame for a failure.

## Examples

> **Bad.** The technician performs the weighing of each sample.
>
> **Good.** The technician weighs each sample.

> **Bad.** The technician’s inspection of the seal precedes the technician’s replacement of it.
>
> **Good.** The technician inspects the seal before replacing it.

> **Bad.** We perform an evaluation of quantization.
>
> **Good.** We evaluate quantization.

The draft identifies the authors as the people detecting faint signals.

> **Bad.** Noise fell after filtering. This reduction in noise permits the detection of faint signals.
>
> **Good.** Noise fell after filtering. This reduction in noise permits us to detect faint signals.

> **Bad.** The fact that the seal leaked delayed the test.
>
> **Good.** The leak in the seal delayed the test.

The draft states that the recorder deletes readings after an hour.

> **Bad.** The recorder forgets readings after an hour.
>
> **Good.** The recorder deletes readings after an hour.

> **Bad.** The results suggest that the coating causes a reduction in heat loss.
>
> **Good.** The results suggest that the coating reduces heat loss.
