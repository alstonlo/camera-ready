# Follow the triage order to meet a length limit

To meet a length limit, cut material in this triage order, preserving [protected content](protected-sentences.md) and following [relocation safeguards](sentence-dependencies.md):

1. **Repeated points.** Cut points repeated across the introduction, section openings, and conclusion, keeping the most informative statement (usually the one that explains the point). Preserve one statement of each contribution in each of the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, along with [shorthand reminders](../conventions/shorthand-reminders.md). Keep a closing restatement when readers need the intervening explanation to understand the opening claim. If a restatement copies another passage word for word, as when a conclusion repeats the abstract, revise it to state established results or flag it. Apply the [deletion test](../prose/deletion-test.md).
2. **Remaining signposts.** Cut signposts left after removing empty wording, including the introduction's closing [roadmap](../prose/signposts.md). Keep references to evidence and proofs.
3. **Unrelated background and related work.** Cut background that does not motivate the research question and citation lists unrelated to the gap. Keep the few works and comparisons that define the gap.
4. **Minor results, secondary analyses, and exploratory findings.** Remove results from the main text if they do not support a central claim, and relocate sensitivity analyses and robustness checks needed for rigor. In the main text, keep any failure or counterevidence that alters a central claim, and state a failed approach as a negative result rather than narrating attempts.
5. **Full parameter lists and procedural details.** Keep [explicit procedures](../prose/definitions-procedures.md), essential steps, and conditions in the main text so readers can reproduce the method in outline. Relocate complete parameter lists, full parameter sweeps, and subsidiary details.

## Details

This rule has no further details.

## Examples

**Example 1.** The author asks to shorten a draft. The paragraph's opening sentence is clear on its own, making the second sentence redundant.

> **Bad.** The lid reduces evaporation in both jars. In both jars, less water evaporates with the lid.
>
> **Good.** The lid reduces evaporation in both jars.

**Example 2.** The draft establishes that the lid halves evaporation. An editor proposes cutting the conclusion's only statement of that contribution to save space.

> **Bad.** The next step is a field trial.
>
> **Good.** The lid halves evaporation. The next step is a field trial.

**Example 3.** Readers need the explanation of gas expansion to understand why the opening claim holds. An editor proposes cutting the paragraph's closing restatement.

> **Bad.** An open vent prevents pressure buildup. Heating expands the gas, which escapes through the vent.
>
> **Good.** An open vent prevents pressure buildup. Heating expands the gas, which escapes through the vent. The vent thus prevents pressure buildup.

**Example 4.** The draft still exceeds the length limit after the editor cuts redundant restatements, so the editor moves to Tier 2.

> **Bad.** Section 2 presents the design, and Section 3 presents the tests. Appendix A proves the stability bound.
>
> **Good.** Appendix A proves the stability bound.

**Example 5.** The draft still exceeds the length limit after Tiers 1 and 2. The history of ceramic pots does not motivate the paper's question about drainage.

> **Bad.** Ceramic pots have a long history. Unlike the pots tested by Lin (2020), ours have drainage holes.
>
> **Good.** Unlike the pots tested by Lin (2020), ours have drainage holes.

**Example 6.** The draft still exceeds the length limit after Tiers 1–3. Appendix B already contains the robustness checks across pressure levels.

> **Bad.** The seal holds at 10 °C and 20 °C but leaks at 30 °C. We repeat each temperature test at three pressures.
>
> **Good.** The seal holds at 10 °C and 20 °C but leaks at 30 °C. Appendix B gives the pressure checks.

**Example 7.** The author asks to shorten the draft. The failure of paper lids alters a central claim, but the draft narrates the attempts chronologically.

> **Bad.** We first tried paper lids, then tried paper lids again. Paper lids leak.
>
> **Good.** Paper lids leak.

**Example 8.** The draft still exceeds the length limit after Tiers 1–4. Appendix C already lists all fan settings.

> **Bad.** We dry the seeds at 30 °C. The fan settings are 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.
>
> **Good.** We dry the seeds at 30 °C. Appendix C lists the fan settings.

**Example 9.** The conclusion repeats the abstract word for word instead of stating the established result from the Results section.

> **Bad.** The lid reduces evaporation.
>
> **Good.** The lid reduces water loss from 8 mL to 4 mL per day.
