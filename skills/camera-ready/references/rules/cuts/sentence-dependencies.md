# Repair text that depends on a sentence you cut or move

For each sentence within [the request's scope](idea-cuts.md):

1. **Check whether the sentence is needed.** If a core claim or the reader needs it, apply "Protect sentences the argument depends on".
2. **Check what depends on it.** Look for later references, terms defined or sources cited only here, hedges or evidence for other claims, and the paragraph's topic string. Repair every dependent passage in the same edit, or keep the sentence.
3. **Choose whether to delete or move it.** Delete only content that no reader needs, and never delete details needed to reproduce the work. Move content that only some readers or a skeptical reviewer will need. Use an appendix or supplement only if the draft already has one; otherwise, keep the material in place and flag what to move. Make moved material understandable on its own, and refer to it in the main text where it becomes relevant. Keep enough of the method and the reasons for choosing it in the main text for readers to judge the results. If the venue permits footnotes, you may move secondary comments into new footnotes. Keep essential steps of a method, derivation, or algorithm, and the only definition of a term later text uses, in the main explanation, not in footnotes or parentheses.
4. **Read across the gap.** The sentence after the gap must open with a term, result, or referent supplied by the sentence before it. Restore a missing connection only from the draft, or keep the original sentence. If the connection requires an inference made by the deleted sentence, add a clause to an adjacent sentence or reuse the original framing sentence to open what follows.

## Details

- Dependencies: Search the rest of the text for passages that depend on the sentence you plan to cut or move, beyond the sentence immediately after the gap.
- Supplement references: Name the specific section, figure, or procedure in a large supplement. Never guess a section number. If the draft has no appendix or supplement, keep the material in place and flag what that section would need to contain.
- Essential steps and definitions: If the draft puts an essential step or the only definition of a term used later in a footnote or parentheses, move it into the main explanation when the author has authorized relocation; otherwise, flag where it appears. A brief definition of notation may remain in parentheses when it is clear and easy to find.

## Examples

**Edit 1.** Say an editor has cut the preceding sentence, which said that the blue dye was easiest to see.

> **Before.** We use it for all remaining experiments.

> **After.** Because the blue dye was easiest to see, we use it for all remaining experiments.

The edited text restores the reason for choosing the dye and makes clear what "it" refers to.

**Edit 2.** Suppose the protocol appears in Section S2 of the existing supplement, and the author permits moving it there.

> **Before.** We dry each sample at 30 °C using the protocol in the supplement.

> **After.** We dry each sample at 30 °C using the protocol in Supplement Section S2.

**Edit 3.** Suppose an editor has replaced the draft's calibration temperatures with an appendix reference, but the draft has no appendix.

> **Before.** The calibration settings appear in Appendix A.

> **After.** We calibrate at 0 °C and 20 °C.

The edited text restores the draft's calibration settings so that readers can repeat the procedure.

**Edit 4.** Suppose the author allows content to be moved.

> **Before.** We heat the sample (first removing the cap).

> **After.** We remove the cap before heating the sample.

**Edit 5.** Suppose an editor has cut the draft's only definition of recovery time R, which later sections still use.

> **Before.** We record R after each trial.

> **After.** Recovery time R is the time to return to 20 °C. We record R after each trial.

The edited text restores the definition needed by later sections.
