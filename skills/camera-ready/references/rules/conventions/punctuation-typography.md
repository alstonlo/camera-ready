# Use punctuation and type styles only for their standard purposes

Avoid contractions, exclamation marks, and emoji. Use title-case headings only when the venue requires them. Use italics for definitions, not decorative emphasis. When italics mark what a negation denies ("we do not heat the *lid*"), state the contrast in words if clear, or keep the italics and flag the ambiguity. Use quotation marks for quotations, words discussed as words, and unendorsed terms (the cell's "decision"); flag an unendorsed term if the draft never explains it (see Details). Do not quote ordinary words. In exact literals, such as commands and outputs, preserve every character; place sentence punctuation outside unless it belongs to the literal.

## Details

Replacing straight quotes with curly quotes can change an argument passed to a shell command or the exact output of a program. For text that must be reproduced exactly, flag a proposed correction instead of changing characters directly.

When the draft states what an unendorsed term in quotation marks refers to, keep the quotation marks and state that meaning beside the term.

## Examples

**Example 1.**

> **Bad.** The lid doesn't fit the jar.
>
> **Good.** The lid does not fit the jar.

**Example 2.**

> **Bad.** The lid fits!
>
> **Good.** The lid fits.

**Example 3.**

> **Bad.** The lid fits. ✅
>
> **Good.** The lid fits.

**Example 4.** The venue does not require title case in headings.

> **Bad.** Seed Storage Conditions
>
> **Good.** Seed storage conditions

**Example 5.** The venue requires title case in headings.

> **Bad.** Seed Storage Conditions!
>
> **Good.** Seed Storage Conditions

**Example 6.**

> **Bad.** The lid is *airtight*.
>
> **Good.** The lid is airtight.

**Example 7.**

> **Bad.** We call the gap between lid and jar the *clearance*. The clearance remains *constant*.
>
> **Good.** We call the gap between lid and jar the *clearance*. The clearance remains constant.

**Example 8.**

> **Bad.** We do not heat the *lid*; we heat the base.
>
> **Good.** We heat the base rather than the lid.

**Example 9.** The draft leaves unclear what the italicized "lid" contrasts with. The editor keeps the italics and flags the ambiguity for the author.

> **Bad.** We don't heat the *lid*.
>
> **Good.** We do not heat the *lid*.

**Example 10.**

> **Bad.** The label reads "fragile". We weigh the "sample".
>
> **Good.** The label reads "fragile". We weigh the sample.

**Example 11.**

> **Bad.** The word "cold" appears on the label. We weigh the "sample".
>
> **Good.** The word "cold" appears on the label. We weigh the sample.

**Example 12.** The draft defines the cell's "decision" as a change in swimming direction, without attributing conscious choice.

> **Bad.** We record the cell's "decision".
>
> **Good.** We record the cell's "decision", its change in swimming direction.

**Example 13.** The draft identifies the command as `pwd`, without a period.

> **Bad.** The command is `pwd.`
>
> **Good.** The command is `pwd`.

**Example 14.** The draft states that the program prints `Done.`, with the period as part of the output.

> **Bad.** The output is `Done`.
>
> **Good.** The output is `Done.`.

**Example 15.** The script in the draft uses straight quotes in `echo "ready"`. The editor flags the curly quotes for the author rather than changing characters inside the command directly.

> **Bad.** The command is `echo “ready”`.
>
> **Good.** The command is `echo "ready"`.
