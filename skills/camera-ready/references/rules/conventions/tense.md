# Match tense to the time frame

Use present tense for the paper's contents ("Section 4 shows", not "will show"), results, method, setup, established facts, and continuing prior work. Preserve a draft's consistent past tense for its experiments; use past tense for events outside the described procedure. Future tense describes uncompleted plans, not later sections. Keep tense stable within a time frame. Prefer simple tense unless perfect tense marks current relevance or an event before another past event. Keep past tense in reported findings ("Lee found that the cache was slower") and hypotheticals ("if the cache were larger"). "No work had studied X before Lee" does not establish whether the gap remains open. Do not turn a research recommendation into the authors' promise. For reported clauses, hypothetical past, and relative dates, see the page.

## Details

A past-tense report need not say whether the reported finding still holds. Changing its subordinate clause to present tense can make the paper endorse a continuing claim. Preserve the source's viewpoint when the draft does not settle that distinction. A past form in a hypothetical condition need not describe an earlier event.

Past perfect can locate an event before another past event. A statement that no work had addressed a question before a particular study does not, by its tense alone, establish whether the question remains open. Present perfect can describe a gap that extends to the present. Do not change the stated status of the gap merely to simplify tense.

A recommendation for future research does not commit the authors to performing it. Keep that distinction when replacing vague future-work wording. For a relative date such as "last year", use an event date supplied by the draft or flag the missing date. A cited publication's year does not necessarily date the event it describes.

## Examples

- Bad: Section 6 will report the simulator's memory use.
- Good: Section 6 reports the simulator's memory use.
