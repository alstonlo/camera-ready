# Write captions that stand alone and state the finding

A caption that only names its figure or table is incomplete. Complete it from the paragraph that cites it, and flag needed facts that appear nowhere in the draft. Identify quantities, units, axes, conditions, visual encodings such as colors and line styles, and [error bars](../claims/variability.md); state whether higher or lower is better when that is not obvious. State the supported finding and check that it agrees with the body text. Name compared methods or conditions instead of identifying them only by color. In tables, flag unexplained blank or dash cells and columns with mixed or missing units. When the plot source or caption gives axis ranges, a second vertical axis, or the range of a fitted curve, flag any claim read from visual distances or slopes that those scales do not support, and flag any extrapolated value described as measured. An axis zoomed to part of its range is not by itself an error.

## Details

A blank or dash in a table can mean that a value is unavailable, unsupported, or inapplicable; never assume that it means zero. Propose a table note or header repair to explain blanks, dashes, or units, without changing data or computing new columns. For formatting changes aimed at meeting a page limit, follow the [stop rules](../cutting/stop-rules.md).

For claims about a fitted relationship, follow [statistical significance](../claims/statistical-significance.md).

## Examples

The paragraph citing Figure 2 states that it shows mean plant height after four weeks, in centimeters, and that plants given fertilizer were taller than untreated plants.

> Bad: Figure 2: Plant growth.
>
> Good: Figure 2: Mean plant height after four weeks, in centimeters. Plants given fertilizer were taller than untreated plants.

The draft states that dashes in Table 3 mark missing temperature measurements; the Good line proposes a caption revision to the author.

> Bad: Table 3: Daily air temperature at each site, in degrees Celsius.
>
> Good: Table 3: Daily air temperature at each site, in degrees Celsius. A dash marks a missing measurement.

The draft supplies these axes and units.

> Bad: Figure 3: Growth over time. The plants grow throughout the study.
>
> Good: Figure 3: Plant height in centimeters (vertical axis) against time in days (horizontal axis). The plants grow throughout the study.

The draft states what each line style represents, and the axes already give the height and its units.

> Bad: Figure 4: Mean height after four weeks. Fertilized plants are taller.
>
> Good: Figure 4: Mean height after four weeks. Solid lines show fertilized plants; dotted lines show untreated plants. Fertilized plants are taller.

The draft states that the error bars are standard deviations across ten plants per condition.

> Bad: Figure 5: Mean height after four weeks, in centimeters. Fertilized plants are taller.
>
> Good: Figure 5: Mean height after four weeks, in centimeters. Error bars show standard deviations across ten plants. Fertilized plants are taller.

The draft states that lower damage scores are better, and the plot labels the conditions and visual encodings.

> Bad: Figure 6: Mean root damage score. Treatment A has the lowest score.
>
> Good: Figure 6: Mean root damage score; lower is better. Treatment A has the lowest score.

The draft identifies red as fertilized plants and blue as untreated plants.

> Bad: Figure 7: Height after four weeks, in centimeters. Red exceeds blue.
>
> Good: Figure 7: Height after four weeks, in centimeters. Fertilized plants exceed untreated plants.

The draft gives grams as the unit for every mass entry; the Good line proposes a caption repair to the author.

> Bad: Table 4: Mean seed mass by site.
>
> Good: Table 4: Mean seed mass by site, in grams.

The draft gives the units for both sets of rows; the Good line proposes adding them to the caption without changing the data.

> Bad: Table 5: Rod lengths.
>
> Good: Table 5: Rod lengths. Entries 1–4 are in centimeters; entries 5–8 are in millimeters.

The draft reports heights of 11 cm and 10 cm, and the vertical axis starts at 9 cm, which makes the bar for treated plants look twice as tall; the Good line proposes correcting the claim for the author.

> Bad: Figure 8: The treated plants are twice as tall as the controls.
>
> Good: Figure 8: The treated plants are 11 cm tall and the controls are 10 cm tall.

The plot shows two rising series on separate vertical axes with different units; the Good line proposes a supported finding to the author.

> Bad: Figure 9: Temperature rises faster than rainfall.
>
> Good: Figure 9: Temperature and rainfall both rise.

The draft reports measurements only through day 20 and states that the 18 cm value at day 30 is a prediction from a fitted curve; the Good line proposes correcting the caption for the author.

> Bad: Figure 10: The measured height at day 30 is 18 cm.
>
> Good: Figure 10: The predicted height at day 30 is 18 cm.

The draft reports a 1 cm increase in each panel, while different vertical-axis ranges make the curve in panel A look steeper; the Good line proposes correcting the comparison for the author.

> Bad: Figure 11: Height increases more in panel A than in panel B.
>
> Good: Figure 11: Height increases by 1 cm in each panel.

Both the paragraph citing Figure 12 and the plotted data show a 3 cm difference; the Good line proposes a caption repair to the author.

> Bad: Figure 12: Mean height after four weeks, in centimeters. Fertilized plants are 5 cm taller than untreated plants.
>
> Good: Figure 12: Mean height after four weeks, in centimeters. Fertilized plants are 3 cm taller than untreated plants.
