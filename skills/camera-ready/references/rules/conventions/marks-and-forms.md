# Use marks and type styles only for meaning, and keep the draft's accepted forms

Use sentence case for headings unless the venue requires title case; remove decorative bold and italics, but keep definition italics and emphasis that affects meaning. Use quotation marks only for quoted text, words mentioned as words, and words used loosely or without endorsement (the model's "reasoning"), not around a word used in its ordinary sense. Beside a word used loosely, state the draft's explanation, or flag the word if the draft never explains it. Check that commas marking a non-restrictive clause match the intended meaning ("the encoder, which has 12 layers, ..."); do not add commas merely because a restrictive clause uses "which", or around a symbol that names the preceding noun ("a directed graph G with source s"); and change restrictive "which" to "that" only when the venue requires it.

Change accepted spelling varieties and term forms only to meet a venue requirement or resolve a genuine inconsistency; if the draft mixes two acceptable forms ("dataset" and "data set") and the venue specifies neither, use the more frequent one. Keep an established field term unhyphenated when the draft consistently writes it so ("a large language model"). Put sentence punctuation outside an exact literal, such as a command or string, unless it belongs to the literal. Use "cf." only to mean "compare", never for "see".

## Details

When italics mark what a negation denies, state the contrast in words if the draft makes it clear. Otherwise, keep the italics and flag the ambiguity.

Accepted spelling varieties include US, UK, and other established varieties.

## Examples

**Edit 1.** Suppose the venue does not require title case.

> **Before.** Measuring Heat Loss Through Glass

> **After.** Measuring heat loss through glass

**Edit 2.**

> **Before.** We call the time between flashes the *interval*. The interval is *constant*, and the lamp is **blue**.

> **After.** We call the time between flashes the *interval*. The interval is constant, and the lamp is blue.

The definition keeps its italics; the other type styles add no meaning.

**Edit 3.** Suppose the draft specifies that the base is heated instead of the rim.

> **Before.** We do not heat the *rim*.

> **After.** We heat the base, not the rim.

**Edit 4.** Suppose an editor removed emphasis from a negation, and the draft never identifies what contrasts with the rim.

> **Before.** We do not heat the rim.

> **After.** We do not heat the *rim*.

After restores the emphasis that affects what is denied. Flag the unclear contrast for the author.

**Edit 5.** Suppose the draft gives no special meaning to "temperature".

> **Before.** We record the "temperature" every minute.

> **After.** We record the temperature every minute.

**Edit 6.** Suppose an editor removed distancing quotes, although the draft explains elsewhere that the robot's "choice" means selecting the brighter light without conscious intent.

> **Before.** We record the robot's choice.

> **After.** We record the robot's "choice", its selection of the brighter light.

The quotes preserve the draft's distancing. Without an explanation in the draft, flag the term instead of supplying a meaning.

**Edit 7.** Suppose an editor changed straight quotes to curly quotes and moved sentence punctuation into the draft's exact command and output.

> **Before.** The command is `echo “warm”.` Its output is `warm.`

> **After.** The command is `echo "warm"`. Its output is `warm`.

After restores both literals character for character. Flag any proposed correction to their contents for author review.

**Edit 8.**

> **Before.** For the sensor specifications, cf. Appendix A.

> **After.** For the sensor specifications, see Appendix A.

**Edit 9.** Suppose an editor changed restrictive "which" to "that", although the venue permits both forms.

> **Before.** The sensors that face the window record higher temperatures.

> **After.** The sensors which face the window record higher temperatures.

After restores the draft's accepted wording.

**Edit 10.** Suppose an editor added commas, although the draft refers only to the samples stored in glass jars.

> **Before.** The samples, which are stored in glass jars, remain dry.

> **After.** The samples which are stored in glass jars remain dry.

**Edit 11.** Suppose an editor added commas around the symbols naming the graph and its source.

> **Before.** A directed graph, $G$, with source, $s$, represents the channels.

> **After.** A directed graph $G$ with source $s$ represents the channels.

**Edit 12.** Suppose an editor changed the draft's UK spelling to US spelling, although the venue accepts either variety.

> **Before.** The colored markers identify the centers of the plots.

> **After.** The coloured markers identify the centres of the plots.

**Edit 13.** Suppose the draft otherwise uses "dataset", and the venue accepts both forms.

> **Before.** This data set contains daily wind speeds.

> **After.** This dataset contains daily wind speeds.

**Edit 14.** Suppose an editor added a hyphen to an established field term that the draft consistently writes without one, and the venue has no contrary requirement.

> **Before.** The soil-mechanics laboratory tests the clay samples.

> **After.** The soil mechanics laboratory tests the clay samples.

**Edit 15.** Suppose the venue requires "data set", although the draft consistently uses "dataset".

> **Before.** The dataset contains lake temperatures.

> **After.** The data set contains lake temperatures.
