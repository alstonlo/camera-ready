# Make each free style choice consistent, following the venue

For each choice below, use the venue's form if it requires one; otherwise use the form the draft establishes, and where the draft mixes accepted forms, use the more frequent one. Never convert one accepted form to another by preference.

- The variety of English (US, UK, Canadian, or another), judged as a whole system rather than word by word: Canadian English writes both "colour" and "analyze".
- The spelling, spacing, and hyphenation of each term ("dataset" or "data set"). Keep an established field term unhyphenated when the draft consistently writes it so ("a large language model").
- Heading case. Where the draft has no consistent scheme, use sentence case.
- The cutoff below which numbers are spelled out as words.
- The serial comma before "and" or "or" in a list of three or more items.
- "Which" or "that" to begin a clause that identifies its noun. A draft may use both, so change one to the other only when the venue requires it.

Never add or remove commas around a clause to make the style consistent: the commas decide whether the clause describes every noun mentioned ("the samples, which are dry, ...") or picks out some of them ("the samples which are dry ..."). Add no commas around a symbol that names the preceding noun ("a directed graph G with source s").

## Details

Accepted varieties include US, UK, Canadian, and other established varieties of English. Ignore quotations, code, and official names both when establishing the draft's forms and when editing.

## Examples

**Edit 1.** Suppose the venue sets no heading case, and the draft's other headings use sentence case.

> **Before.** Measuring Heat Loss Through Glass

> **After.** Measuring heat loss through glass

**Edit 2.** Suppose the venue specifies no variety of English, and the draft otherwise uses UK spelling.

> **Before.** The centre of each plot shows the color scale.

> **After.** The centre of each plot shows the colour scale.

**Edit 3.** Suppose an editor changed the draft's UK spelling to US spelling, although the venue accepts either variety.

> **Before.** The colored markers identify the centers of the plots.

> **After.** The coloured markers identify the centres of the plots.

**Edit 4.** Suppose the draft otherwise uses "dataset", and the venue accepts both forms.

> **Before.** This data set contains daily wind speeds.

> **After.** This dataset contains daily wind speeds.

**Edit 5.** Suppose an editor added a hyphen to an established field term that the draft consistently writes without one, and the venue has no contrary requirement.

> **Before.** The soil-mechanics laboratory tests the clay samples.

> **After.** The soil mechanics laboratory tests the clay samples.

**Edit 6.** Suppose the venue requires "data set", although the draft consistently uses "dataset".

> **Before.** The dataset contains lake temperatures.

> **After.** The data set contains lake temperatures.

**Edit 7.** Suppose the draft otherwise uses the serial comma.

> **Before.** We measure mass, volume and density.

> **After.** We measure mass, volume, and density.

**Edit 8.** Suppose an editor changed "which" to "that", although the venue permits both forms.

> **Before.** The sensors that face the window record higher temperatures.

> **After.** The sensors which face the window record higher temperatures.

After restores the draft's accepted wording.

**Edit 9.** Suppose an editor added commas, although the draft refers only to the samples stored in glass jars.

> **Before.** The samples, which are stored in glass jars, remain dry.

> **After.** The samples which are stored in glass jars remain dry.

**Edit 10.** Suppose an editor added commas around the symbols naming the graph and its source.

> **Before.** A directed graph, $G$, with source, $s$, represents the channels.

> **After.** A directed graph $G$ with source $s$ represents the channels.
