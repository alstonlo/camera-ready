# Repair pause punctuation between a claim and its evidence

Look for a vague claim followed by a colon or semicolon and then its evidence ("Retrieval helps: recall rises by 4 points"). Read the first clause alone. If it states no specific fact, connect the claim to its evidence in words ("as shown by ..."), or delete the vague clause if the evidence states its whole claim. Replacing the mark with a period does not fix this. Apply the test to every such colon or semicolon, not only to one that replaced an em dash. When the first clause states a specific fact, keep a semicolon before a related fact or a colon before an explanation or example. Colons introducing lists, displays, quoted definitions, or labels need no factual assertion before them; this exception does not permit vague claims followed by evidence. The colon must not imply an unsupported cause or exhaustive list. Follow [mathematical punctuation](../conventions/math-prose.md) for displays. Restore a missing verb in a trailing comma phrase ("adds 3 ms, below the limit" becomes "adds 3 ms, which is below the limit"); keep appositives, clear participial phrases, and parallel phrases sharing an earlier verb.

## Details

Identify what the first clause asserts and how the second supports it. If the specific second clause already states everything useful in the first, remove the first. Otherwise choose words that express the supported connection; do not infer a causal explanation from juxtaposition alone.

A semicolon can give each of two related clauses its own emphasis. Replacing it mechanically with a period can change how readers connect their claims.

A colon can make a following description look complete. Check that the colon does not present a partial list as an exhaustive definition, or a related finding as a restatement of the first clause.

For a trailing comma phrase, decide whether it needs a finite verb to state its relation to the preceding clause. A genuine appositive names the same thing, a clear participial phrase modifies an identifiable actor or event, and a parallel phrase may share a verb already stated. Do not expand those forms.

## Examples

- Bad: The new tap is effective: it reduces water use by 18%.
- Good: The new tap reduces water use by 18%.
