# Choose the reporting verb from the verb ladder

Choose each reporting verb from the verb ladder using the evidence for the claim, regardless of enthusiasm for the result.

| Verb | Required support |
|---|---|
| **prove** | a theorem with stated assumptions, and nothing else |
| **show / demonstrate / establish** | direct evidence that rules out plausible alternatives |
| **find / observe / report** | a directly measured empirical result |
| **suggest / indicate**; **hypothesize / conjecture** | an inference or interpretation that could reasonably be wrong |

A benchmark does not prove a mechanism. A figure or table shows only what it displays; choose an inferred claim's verb by its evidence. Replace *reveal* or *confirm* with a lower-rung verb if the evidence does not establish the stated conclusion. Flag a claimed disproof supported only by an objection or an inconclusive test. When reporting cited work, preserve whether the source presents a result as central or incidental, and whether it accepts or doubts the result. See the page for verb choices and [prior work](../story/prior-work.md) for attribution.

A claim hedged in Results keeps its hedge in the abstract, introduction, conclusion, and every other summary. Find each summary claim's supporting result, and flag a summary claim that has none. Add a missing section or table pointer when support exists, except in the abstract, which states the result itself.

## Details

The ladder distinguishes a proof, an established conclusion, a measured observation, and an uncertain inference. Choose the rung for the proposition being reported: a measurement can establish that a score increased without establishing why it increased. A theorem claim needs its proof or a reference to the proved result, together with the assumptions under which it holds.

Trace a summary claim through intermediate assertions until the draft identifies an observation, demonstrated result, or established source result. Another writer's agreement does not establish a contested proposition. An ordinary accepted result does not need a fresh derivation.

A reporting verb can change the writer's attitude toward a cited result as well as its evidential strength. Describing a central result as something the source notes in passing understates its role. Calling an established premise an assumption may introduce doubt absent from the source. Keep these distinctions faithful to the supplied account.

An objection or an unsuccessful test is not necessarily a disproof. Describe what the evidence establishes rather than treating disagreement as a settled outcome.

A figure may display a trend without supporting an explanation for it. Preserve that distinction when shortening a caption or a summary. Abstract claims need support in the paper even though the abstract states the result without directing readers to a body section or table.

## Examples

The draft reports water temperatures measured in a test of an insulated bottle and a plain bottle; it contains no mathematical proof.

- Bad: We prove that the insulated bottle kept water warmer than the plain bottle in the test.
- Good: We find that the insulated bottle kept water warmer than the plain bottle in the test.
