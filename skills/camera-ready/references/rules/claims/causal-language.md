# Reserve causal language for causal evidence

Use "causes", "leads to", "improves because", or other wording that credits a factor (such as a design choice, treatment, or exposure) with an effect only when the evidence isolates that effect, as a suitable controlled comparison can. Describe correlations with "is associated with" or "correlates with". Preserve the direction of cause and effect, and do not present a partial contribution as the sole cause. When several changes are tested only together, credit their combination, not a single component, and do not claim that each component is necessary. Flag unsupported causal attribution, and mark an untested mechanism as [speculation](speculation.md).

## Details

Causal wording also includes "produces", "results in", "results from", and "contributes to". Check whether the draft distinguishes a contributing factor from a sufficient or necessary condition, and preserve that distinction when shortening.

Experiments that can isolate one factor include a suitable ablation, gene knockout, or randomized controlled trial.

## Examples

> **Context.** The draft reports an association between the number of houseplants and the number of colds in surveyed households, with no evidence isolating a causal effect; the revision is proposed to the author.
>
> **Bad.** In the surveyed households, owning more houseplants caused fewer colds.
>
> **Good.** In the surveyed households, owning more houseplants was associated with fewer colds.

> **Context.** The draft states that cooling causes condensation, not the reverse; the revision is proposed to the author.
>
> **Bad.** Condensation results in cooling.
>
> **Good.** Condensation results from cooling.

> **Context.** The draft identifies poor drainage as one of several causes of root decay; the revision is proposed to the author.
>
> **Bad.** Poor drainage is the sole cause of root decay.
>
> **Good.** Poor drainage contributes to root decay.

> **Context.** The draft isolates the effect of adding mulch and shade together but never tests either alone; the revision is proposed to the author.
>
> **Bad.** Mulch reduces water loss.
>
> **Good.** Mulch and shade together reduce water loss.

> **Context.** The draft shows that a cover and insulation together prevent freezing but does not test whether either is necessary; the revision is proposed to the author.
>
> **Bad.** A cover and insulation are each necessary to prevent freezing.
>
> **Good.** A cover and insulation together prevent freezing.

> **Context.** The draft identifies lubrication as a contributing factor but reports failures despite lubrication; the revision is proposed to the author.
>
> **Bad.** Lubrication is sufficient to prevent wear.
>
> **Good.** Lubrication contributes to preventing wear.

> **Context.** The draft isolates the coating's effect on corrosion and proposes, without testing the mechanism, that the coating blocks oxygen.
>
> **Bad.** The coating reduces corrosion because it blocks oxygen.
>
> **Good.** The coating reduces corrosion; we conjecture that it does so by blocking oxygen.
