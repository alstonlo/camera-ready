# Match scope and confidence to the evidence

Scope can cover every case, a tendency across contexts, named settings, or one observed case. Choose the broadest scope and the most direct confidence the evidence supports, avoiding [underclaiming](underclaiming.md), but keep every claim within the conditions, datasets, and settings its evidence covers. An unqualified plural noun ("Solar cells degrade in humid air") may state a tendency; if the evidence covers only the tested cases, name them. Check whether words such as "always" or "unique" extend a claim to untested cases.

A single example can establish that a case occurs. Flag a universal claim contradicted by an example within its scope; an exception need not refute a tendency. Flag a broader claim from one example or from sampled data unless the draft explains how it was selected and, for sampled data, justifies the target population. Keep explicit labels marking examples as hypothetical or constructed, but never add them by inference. Check [independent confirmation](variability.md) under its own rule.

Also check these claims:

- Unless the draft supports the interpretation, flag a research question or conclusion that treats a score as the broader property it measures indirectly, or a ratio of scores as a proportional change in that property. Do not rewrite the question or add a caveat.
- A result without a comparison supports "X works", not "X is better" or "X is practical"; flag any stronger claim that lacks a relevant baseline.
- A claim such as "ready for clinical use" or "ready for production" also needs evidence on costs and operating conditions beyond test gains, and must address the adverse consequences and relevant alternatives the draft identifies. Flag missing support or unsupported assurances unless the draft explicitly marks the claim as tentative.
- A claimed trend over time needs observations over time, and a small sample needs both its count of cases and its total visible in the text.
- Flag words that assume an unstated fact, such as "the solution", which may assume existence or uniqueness.

## Details

- Examples and samples: Calling an example random, typical, or the best case does not by itself establish a broader claim. A larger sample does not by itself make an unrepresentative sample representative. A valid constructed example can establish mathematical possibility; do not present it as an empirical observation.
- Trends: A claim that a proportion or rate changes over time needs observations over time with comparable denominators. A claim about raw counts needs counts over time but no denominators. A few recent cases establish neither trend. Do not treat a sample percentage as an estimate of a population probability without support.
- Baselines: Outperforming a baseline the draft identifies as irrelevant or needlessly weak cannot establish general superiority. Check whether the draft's baselines reflect current methods, are competently implemented, and cover the credible simple alternatives it identifies. Flag gaps that undermine the comparison. Age alone does not make a baseline obsolete.
- Scores: Read the research question, measurement, and conclusion together. A scale may rank systems without measuring how much they differ in the underlying property. Do not infer the type of scale or change reported values to repair an unsupported interpretation.
- Implied facts: Keep "the" for an object already introduced or a chosen representative. Do not read "a solution" as implying that several solutions exist.
- Practical use: Check the requirements the draft documents, such as startup costs or guarantees about outputs. Do not impose a generic deployment checklist. Keep conditional recommendations supported by the draft's evidence.

## Examples

**Edit 1.** Suppose the draft records that 99 of the 100 tested bulbs lit within a second and calls that timing typical.

> **Before.** The tested bulbs always lit within a second.

> **After.** The tested bulbs usually lit within a second.

**Edit 2.** If the draft documents a crack in one photographed tile but gives no survey of other tiles, propose the edited text to the author as a revision.

> **Before.** Cracks are common in these tiles.

> **After.** The photographed tile has a crack.

**Edit 3.** Say the draft gives chair A a comfort score of 8 and chair B a score of 4, without establishing that the scores are proportional to comfort. Treat the edited text as a proposal to the author.

> **Before.** Chair A is twice as comfortable as chair B.

> **After.** Chair A scores twice as high as chair B on the comfort scale.

**Edit 4.** Imagine the draft reports tests only on aluminum and explicitly describes possible use on bridges as untested.

> **Before.** The coating resists corrosion on all metals and may be useful on bridges.

> **After.** The coating resists corrosion on aluminum in the tests and may be useful on bridges.

The draft already marks the bridge application as untested, so the tentative suggestion can stay.
