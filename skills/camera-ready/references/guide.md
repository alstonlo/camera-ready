# Writing guide

This guide is generated from a collection of writing rules. Each rule has its own page in the `rules/` folder next to this guide and follows this format:

```markdown
# {{ Name of the rule }}

{{ A summary of the rule: what to look for, the edit to make or problem to flag, and the exceptions that change the edit. }}

## Details

{{ Explanations, further cases, and procedures left out of the summary, or the sentence "This rule has no further details." }}

## Examples

**Edit 1.** {{ Optional context, phrased as a hypothetical: "Suppose the draft ..." }}

> **Before.** {{ A passage that breaks the rule. }}

> **After.** {{ The same passage, corrected under this rule alone. }}

{{ Optional explanation of the edit, when it is not obvious. }}
```

The guide below reproduces each rule's heading and summary, and each rule page adds `Details` and at least one example. Read the full page when a rule applies to your text but the summary does not show which edit to make. Each example shows one edit that its rule calls for. No example permits a change beyond what the user asked for.

## Table of contents

The guide has six sections of rules:

- [Story](#story): the argument from problem to implications, titles and headings, section order and openings, contributions, related work, results by question, and numbers that support claims.
- [Claims](#claims): reporting verbs matched to evidence, scope, overclaiming and underclaiming, consistency across the text, causal language, what an efficiency claim counts, statistical significance, hedges, speculation, and limitations.
- [Cutting](#cutting): what you may delete or move without asking the author, the triage order under a length limit, sentences to protect, and how to repair text around a cut.
- [Prose](#prose): explanation order, paragraphs, information flow from familiar to new, sentence structure, direct content instead of promises, concrete quantities, and plain wording.
- [Conventions](#conventions): voice, tense, terminology, mathematics, citations and credit, figures and tables, and typography.
- [Deslopping](#deslopping): blacklisted and tolerable patterns common in AI-written prose.

For a request narrower than a full polish, apply the sections it names and every rule they link to. Check each chunk of text against every rule in each applicable section. When two rules call for different edits to a sentence and one links to the other, follow the linked rule. Otherwise, choose the clearer sentence, or use your judgment as an expert editor if both are equally clear.

## Story

Organize the text around one five-part argument: a problem the reader cares about; a gap in what is known or possible, with the cost of leaving it open; an idea that fills the gap; evidence that the idea works; and what the findings imply.

Check this argument across the whole text and within each section before editing individual sentences, because sentence edits cannot fix a missing or illogical step. Use the [rules on claims](#claims) to match each claim to its evidence, the [rules on cutting](#cutting) to decide what stays, and the [rules on prose](#prose) to edit paragraphs and sentences.

Without a separate request from the author, you may:

- Reorder sentences in a paragraph or paragraphs in a section to put the claim first.
- Rewrite a section's opening using content already in that section.
- Remove numbers from running text when a table already gives them.
- Rewrite a caption to state a conclusion already drawn in the body.

Flag any change to the claims, section order, or list of contributions instead of making it yourself. Never add a cost, explanation, or number absent from the draft.

### The whole argument

**[Test the argument with five lines and one sentence](rules/story/argument-summary.md).** Write one line each for the problem, the gap and its cost, the idea, the evidence, and the implication, and flag any line the draft does not supply. Combine the idea and evidence into core claims for the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md).

Combine the five lines into one test sentence: "A matters, and B is known, but C fails because D; therefore we propose E, which achieves F." The lines and the sentence are notes, not text for the paper. If the sentence lists facts without a problem or gap, flag what is missing. If it requires unrelated problems, flag the competing focuses and ask which one the text addresses, without inventing a connection. Judge both cases by meaning, not by counting "but": a gap need not use that word, and several "but" clauses may describe obstacles to answering one question.

**[Match each section to its part of the argument](rules/story/section-functions.md).** In a paper, present the argument in this order: Abstract, Introduction, Background and related work, Methods, Results, Discussion, and Conclusion; a thesis chapter, report, or proposal follows the same order under its own section names. Each result must follow the method description it needs. Keep combined or repeated sections if the argument stays clear, and follow venue rules.

The introduction states the problem, gap, idea, and contributions; its opening states the problem and what is missing. Background gives only the context needed for the gap and comparisons; its opening states what the reader must know to see the gap. Methods explains the idea, Results gives the evidence, Discussion interprets the evidence, limitations, and alternatives, and the conclusion states the implications. When the venue places Methods after Results, keep a brief description of the procedure with each result and refer readers to Methods for the full one. State the contributions in the first quarter of the text, and flag a central result that first appears later.

The abstract opens with the problem and states all five parts of the argument in one or two sentences each when the venue's word limit allows, with the headline result and recognizable terms for the problem, system, and technique. Make it understandable without the body or bibliography: identify in the abstract any cited work readers need, and remove a reference to a section or figure only when the abstract already states that result. Flag a missing part or result, or needed content that exceeds the limit. A proposal describes the evidence the authors plan to gather, and a survey abstract may state what the reviewed studies establish together.

**[Keep the argument linear](rules/story/linear-argument.md).** Order material by the argument's logic, not by when each part was done or how much effort it took. Apply the [rule against alternation](rules/prose/topic-order.md) across sections (A, B, A), and flag a repair that would move material between sections. The core idea may appear in every section; keep a second round of methods and results that depends on an earlier result ([section functions](rules/story/section-functions.md)), reminders of needed definitions and of [section context](rules/story/section-openings.md), and the protected statements of each contribution. Give each component and counterargument space proportional to its role in the core claims. Explain each side of a comparison under the [rule on comparing with prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md). Under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md), keep a rejected approach in the main text only as a negative result, stating its flaw before its details.

**[Test the argument by skimming](rules/story/skim-test.md).** Check that the abstract, headings, first paragraph of each section, and [captions](rules/conventions/captions.md) alone state all five parts of the argument. When the request allows, restore any missing part or connection; otherwise, flag it.

**[Flag titles that are unclear or overstate the contribution](rules/story/titles.md).** Flag a title that hides the subject, omits searchable terms for what distinguishes the work, has two reasonable readings, contains needless wording, overstates or understates the contribution, or uses an acronym unfamiliar outside the subfield ([acronym rule](rules/conventions/acronyms.md)). Suggest a revision that keeps the body's claim strength and conditions, leave the title unchanged, and follow venue requirements.

### Problem, gap, and contribution

**[Open with the problem](rules/story/open-with-problem.md).** Name what the reader cares about in the first sentence, unless readers need context to understand the problem; then apply the [brief-setup rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md). Delete any [generic opening](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md). If the draft never says why the problem matters, flag the missing motivation rather than invent an example. If the opening describes a different, broader, or narrower problem than the rest of the paper, revise it, following the [rule on scope](rules/claims/scope.md), only when the draft makes the intended focus clear; otherwise, flag the mismatch.

**[State the gap's cost](rules/story/gap-cost.md).** State the specific gap in what is known or can be done, and its cost: what readers lose, pay, or cannot do because of it. "No prior work studies X" states a gap but not its cost. Replace "little is known" with the specific unknown when the draft supplies it. The cost may be a question readers cannot answer or the value of extending earlier work to a new case; it need not be a flaw in prior work or a hypothesis, so do not invent either. Flag a missing gap or cost, and flag rather than make any rewrite of the objectives that stating the gap requires. Distinguish established consequences from [speculative applications](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Explain why the problem is hard](rules/story/why-hard.md).** Explain why the obvious way to solve the problem fails, or flag the missing explanation.

**[State each contribution as a claim and name its supporting section](rules/story/contribution-claims.md).** Each claim must be checkable. If a contribution states only an activity, replace it with a claim under the [underclaiming rule](rules/claims/underclaiming.md), matching its strength to the evidence with the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md). Flag contributions without evidence, and check a major result that supports no contribution with the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md), which makes it a candidate for cutting only under a length request. When contributions name their sections, delete any roadmap that states no content under the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md).

### Section openings and connections

**[Open each section with its context, question, and answer](rules/story/section-openings.md).** Connect each section's question to the preceding text by placing [familiar information before new information](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). State the question and answer in the first paragraph, following [paragraph opening order](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md); openings that only name the topic are [signposts](rules/prose/signposts.md). Make the introduction understandable without the abstract. Keep short reminders for readers who start at Results, Discussion, or Conclusion, including essential context when the full methods appear in supplementary material, unless a length request reaches them in the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md). Open a methods section with one sentence stating its key idea before the steps. Put an existing [explanatory example](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) before the general case.

**[Use headings to show how the argument is organized](rules/story/headings.md).** Make each parent heading describe all its subsections, and give headings under the same parent [parallel form](rules/prose/parallel-form.md). Flag a parent with only one subheading, a fourth-level heading, or a very short section when it makes the argument hard to follow. Propose, but do not make, a heading that states its section's finding with the body's claim strength and conditions, and any change to how sections are grouped or nested. Keep distinctions that help readers, and follow venue requirements.

### Related work

**[Compare prior work fairly and state specific differences](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md).** End each paragraph or group of paragraphs about prior work with a checkable difference in an assumption, setting, metric, result, cost, or guarantee; a comparison that is vague on both sides is a [false concession](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md). Explain both sides enough for readers to judge the comparison, using only the draft's account of each source, and flag missing context or differences. State what each source does and assumes before criticizing it, and preserve its [credit](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md). Do not write "ignores" or "fails to" for a difference in scope unless the draft shows that the source failed. Keep a source's tentative verbs, and attribute each claim, concession, and reported objection to whoever holds it, not to the paper. In a survey, end the discussion of each group of related sources with the pattern, disagreement, or gap they establish.

**[State what is new and what comes from earlier work](rules/story/novelty.md).** Distinguish what the authors contribute from standard components, borrowed ideas, and their earlier results ("we use the probe of Smith et al. [4]; our change is to ..."), and flag an unclear contribution rather than assign it. Keep wording that identifies the authors' earlier work (or flag it if the venue requires anonymity), and do not present that work as confirmation by other researchers.

**[Flag comparisons with prior work that readers cannot yet understand](rules/story/prior-work-placement.md).** Leave each comparison with prior work in place, and flag it only if it appears before material that readers need to understand it; do not flag background that readers need to understand the problem.

### Results

**[Organize results around research questions](rules/story/results-by-question.md).** State which questions the Results section answers, and connect each to a contribution. Flag a heading that names only a dataset or setup, and propose one that states a question or finding. When one result motivates the next experiment, keep that sequence, with the interpretation beside the result. Flag for cutting an experiment that answers no question, and flag, but delete neither, a result with no described procedure or an evaluation procedure with no reported use.

**[Answer one question per results paragraph](rules/story/one-question-per-paragraph.md).** Keep each answer and its evidence in one paragraph, ordered under the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md). Split a paragraph that answers two independent questions, and flag one that answers none.

**[Explain why the evidence supports the claim](rules/story/explain-evidence.md).** Explain the reasoning when readers outside the subfield may not see how the evidence supports a claim, and check that its assumptions hold in this study; flag a connection the draft omits. Follow [scope](rules/claims/scope.md) for claims that exceed what the measurement establishes, and [formal support](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md) for conclusions from a theorem.

**[Identify which part produces the effect](rules/story/component-effects.md).** When a method, treatment, or design combines several parts or changes, the results must show which part produces the reported effect; otherwise, flag the missing evidence and credit only the combination under [causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md). Flag any raised counterexample, conflicting result, or obvious alternative explanation the draft does not address, and any reply to a raised counterexample, conflicting result, or alternative explanation that gives no evidence, no reason the objection does not apply, and no statement of the [limitation](rules/claims/limitations.md) that remains.

### Numbers

The most common flaw in the argument of AI-generated text is listing results before stating what they show, or never stating the point. Reorder the text so that every number supports a claim already stated in words.

**[State each finding before its representative numbers](rules/story/claim-before-number.md).** Describe the pattern in words: whether the effect is consistent, where it grows, and where it fails. For each claim, give one representative headline result, not an atypical best case, with its baseline and any interval or range needed to interpret it. Use the table to establish the pattern and, outside the [abstract](rules/story/section-functions.md), refer to an existing table for the remaining values; remove duplicated numbers only when the prose keeps the comparison, [uncertainty](rules/claims/variability.md), and [statistical conclusion](rules/claims/statistical-significance.md). Use the same finding-first order for [quantified comparisons](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md). Flag numbers that support no claim and, when the request permits, move them only to an existing table or appendix.

**[Match precision to the claim](rules/story/number-precision.md).** Flag precision beyond what the measurement and comparison support, and unjustified differences in precision between comparable quantities; never change a number's value or precision without the author's approval and the source data, except to replace a threshold such as p < 0.05 with the draft's reported [p-value](rules/claims/statistical-significance.md). Keep words that mark estimates or approximations.

### Discussion and conclusion

**[Explain how the results address the gap](rules/story/return-to-gap.md).** Give this explanation in the discussion's first paragraph, for the gap stated in the introduction, then state the limitations under the [rule on limitations](rules/claims/limitations.md). If the introduction promises more than the results establish, flag the mismatch under the [rule on scope](rules/claims/scope.md).

**[Use the conclusion to explain what the findings mean](rules/story/conclusion-perspective.md).** Keep one statement of each contribution under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md), connect these statements, then state what the findings let readers understand or do. Label speculative implications as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md). Flag a conclusion that only repeats general statements or the abstract, gives new supporting evidence, or introduces a new subject. Future work may state a specific question the findings raise and what answering it would establish; do not invent a research plan.

## Claims

Every claim must match its evidence. Claiming either more or less than the evidence supports is an error. An unreached goal may motivate the work if the text states that the goal remains unreached.

### Strength and scope

**[Choose a reporting verb from the verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md).**

| Verb | Required support |
|---|---|
| **prove** | only a theorem with stated assumptions |
| **show / demonstrate / establish** | direct evidence that rules out plausible alternatives |
| **find / observe / report** | a directly measured empirical result |
| **suggest / indicate**; **hypothesize / conjecture** | an inference or interpretation that could reasonably be wrong |

A figure or table "shows" only what it directly displays, so match the verb of any interpretation of it to the evidence. Replace *reveal* or *confirm* with a weaker verb unless the evidence establishes the conclusion. Flag a claimed disproof that rests only on an objection or an inconclusive test. For cited work, follow [prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md), and preserve whether the source treats a result as central or incidental and whether it accepts or doubts it: do not write "notes in passing" for a result the source presents as central, or "assumes" for a premise it establishes.

Keep each claim's hedge, such as "suggests", and its rung on the ladder in the abstract, introduction, conclusion, and other summaries. Trace each summary claim to its supporting result and flag any unsupported one; for a supported one, add any missing section, table, or figure reference, except in the abstract.

**[Match scope and confidence to the evidence](rules/claims/scope.md).** Scope ranges from every case ("X always holds") through a tendency across contexts ("X generally happens") and named settings ("X is best for settings V and W") to one observed case ("we find one case where X"). Choose the broadest scope and the most direct confidence the evidence supports, avoiding [underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md), but keep every claim within the conditions, datasets, and settings its evidence covers. An unqualified plural noun ("Solar cells degrade in humid air") may state a tendency; if the evidence covers only the tested cases, name them ("the four cell designs we test"). Check whether words such as "always" or "unique" extend a claim to untested cases.

A single example, such as a generated sample or saliency map, can establish that a case occurs. Flag a universal claim contradicted by an in-scope example; an exception need not refute a tendency. Flag a broader claim from one example or from sampled data unless the draft explains how it was selected (such as at random, as typical, or as the best case) and, for sampled data, justifies the target population. Keep an explicit label marking an example as hypothetical or constructed, but never add one by inference. Check [independent confirmation](rules/claims/variability.md) under its own rule.

Unless the draft supports the interpretation, flag a research question or conclusion that treats a score as the broader property it measures indirectly, or a ratio of scores as a proportional change in that property, without rewriting the question or adding a caveat. A result without a comparison supports "X works", not "X is better" or "X is practical"; flag any stronger claim that lacks a relevant baseline. A claim such as "ready for clinical use" or "ready for production" also needs evidence on costs and operating conditions beyond test gains, and must address the adverse consequences and relevant alternatives that the draft identifies; flag missing support or unsupported assurances unless the draft explicitly marks the claim as tentative. A claimed trend over time needs observations over time, and a small sample needs both its count of cases and its total visible in the text. Flag words that assume an unstated fact, such as "the solution" (which may assume that a solution exists or is unique).

**[Remove or flag unsupported labels on empirical claims](rules/claims/unsupported-labels.md).** Labels such as *solves*, *optimal*, *guarantees*, and *first to* need evidence for the full empirical claim. Flag unsupported superlatives and any *state-of-the-art*, *record*, or *human-level* claim that omits the task or quantity, the benchmark, the compared methods or results, or the metric. Remove *novel* when it adds only praise. Beyond removing a label, rewrite the claim only when the draft's evidence determines its [scope](rules/claims/scope.md); otherwise, flag it. An experiment or observation shows uniqueness only among the alternatives compared, so a uniqueness claim must name those alternatives, the property compared, and how finely the comparison distinguishes them.

**[State supported contributions without underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md).** Remove a hedge, such as "may", that makes a contribution sound less certain than its evidence supports. Replace a contribution that says only what the authors *study*, *explore*, *investigate*, *describe*, or *discuss* with the finding from the corresponding section, or flag the missing finding.

**[Flag conflicting claims and numbers](rules/claims/internal-consistency.md).** Compare claims about the same object across sections and lists, and check totals, bounds, and trends against the draft's numerical constraints. Flag both passages of an apparent conflict, and either propose a revision stating the difference in setting or definition that the draft gives to explain it, or flag the missing explanation. Flag conflicting numbers without choosing a value. Flag a claim that two options are the only possibilities when the draft names another option in that scope, and name that option.

### Causation and formal support

**[Reserve causal language for causal evidence](rules/claims/causal-language.md).** Use causal wording such as "causes", "leads to", or "improves because" only with evidence that isolates the factor's effect, such as an ablation of that component; describe a correlation as "is associated with" or "correlates with". Keep cause and effect in the order the evidence supports, and do not call one of several causes the only cause. When the draft tests several changes only together, credit their combination, not one component, and do not call each component necessary. Mark an untested mechanism as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Identify the kind of evidence supporting each claim](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md).** Link a sentence that reads like a theorem but gives no formal result or conditions ("all local minima have error close to that of the global minimum") to the proved result and its assumptions, restate it as the observed or intuitive claim the draft supports, or flag it. Flag a claim that applies a theorem to observations or experiments that violate its assumptions, such as a convergence result for convex objectives cited for non-convex training; the theorem is then inapplicable, not false. Never drop a condition to simplify a formal statement; flag one that seems unnecessary. Flag an unsupported claim that a result from a model or simulation holds in the real system; a valid claim limited to the model needs no experiment or field study.

**[Specify what an efficiency claim counts](rules/claims/efficiency-claims.md).** For a claim about speed, yield, cost, samples needed, or scaling, state the unit counted, the conditions and assumptions, and the costs included or excluded. Name the baseline of each comparison, and define a ratio's numerator and denominator and an analytical bound's size variables and the case analyzed (such as the worst, average, or amortized case). Fill missing detail from elsewhere in the draft, or flag it. A better ratio or asymptotic bound alone does not show lower total cost under the claimed conditions. An upper bound on one algorithm's cost does not establish a lower bound for every algorithm solving the problem. Flag suspected errors in the analysis without changing the mathematics.

### Numerical comparisons

**[Report variability and compare like with like](rules/claims/variability.md).** For each numerical comparison, check that the draft states, beside it or in a general statement, the number of runs, repetitions, or samples; how they were combined (such as by mean, median, or best run); whether ± is a standard deviation, standard error, or confidence interval; and what varied (such as the random seed, data split, or the test examples a bootstrap resamples). Flag any that is missing. A best run is not comparable with a mean. Flag a comparison, including one with other papers' results, unless the draft establishes that both sides use equivalent tasks, outputs, quality requirements, aggregation, and procedures. When one measure improves and another worsens, state both changes, or flag the missing comparison. Check that the draft states what each percentage is of and whether a percentage change is relative or in percentage points; clarify the wording from the draft, or flag the ambiguity. Name the measure behind each ranking, and flag selective reporting.

Flag a claim of independent confirmation from data used to generate a hypothesis, select or tune the method, related datasets, or correlated measures unless the draft establishes a separate test. Also flag a mean that a skewed distribution, missing outcomes, or unequal groups make unrepresentative of a typical case; a stated test condition that could push every measurement in the same direction, such as calibration or a warm cache; and a convergence claim supported only by successive estimates rather than a measure of error.

**[Draw only the conclusions a statistical test supports](rules/claims/statistical-significance.md).** Use *significant* only for a named statistical test, reported with its p-value or significance level and any effect size in the draft; flag any that is missing. Where *significant* means large or important, replace it with the reported magnitude. Keep the draft's effect and uncertainty estimates even for a result that is not significant, and use the numeric p-value that the draft reports anywhere rather than a threshold such as p < 0.05. A p-value is not the probability that a hypothesis is true. Preserve the tested hypothesis and the test's direction. Failing to reject the null hypothesis does not establish equality: replace "the two methods perform the same (paired bootstrap, p = 0.21)" with "we find no significant difference between the two methods (paired bootstrap, p = 0.21)". Significance does not establish practical importance. A result that is not significant does not by itself show that the test had too little power. Flag a claim about whether one result is better that rests only on interval overlap, without a test. When the draft tests significance across several datasets, outcomes, or measures, flag a missing account of how it handles multiple comparisons.

### Qualification and limits

**[Hedge interpretations, not measurements or procedures](rules/claims/hedges.md).** Tentative interpretations include proposed mechanisms. Keep reported measurement uncertainty; for a procedure, hedge only the reason for performing it. Use at most one hedge per uncertain claim, in a standard form such as "may", "likely", "suggests", or "appears to". Deleting a claim's only hedge strengthens it, and removing a redundant hedge must leave other uncertain claims hedged. A qualifier that limits the conditions or cases a claim covers, such as "usually" or "on average", is a [scope condition](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md), not a hedge; keep it. Replace a vague softener such as "somewhat", "relatively", or "essentially" with the conditions or magnitudes the draft supplies; otherwise, keep the softener and flag the missing information.

**[Never state speculation as fact](rules/claims/speculation.md).** Mark an explanation or mechanism that no test or argument supports as a conjecture ("We conjecture that") or put it in a passage explicitly labeled speculative. Flag, instead of marking as a conjecture, an explanation that relies on an undefined term or an unstated measurement: "The gain comes from reduced internal covariate shift" cannot be checked until the text states how the shift is measured. A claim that one property does not imply another needs a counterexample, a supporting result, a reference, or a statement that the question is open. Failure under the tested conditions does not show that success is impossible, so flag any unsupported negative conclusion.

**[State limitations precisely and explain their effects](rules/claims/limitations.md).** State each limitation in full and without apology where it first affects a method or inference. Beside each limitation, keep the draft's explanation of how it affects the results ("results may not transfer to larger models, since every run uses the same 100M-parameter backbone") and which conclusions still hold, or flag the missing explanation. Flag unexplained unexpected results, unjustified data exclusions, and decoy limitations: a minor weakness named while a larger one documented elsewhere in the draft goes unmentioned. Every future-work statement must name the current limitation it addresses. Keep brief reminders that limit later claims; move a limitation, or delete a repeated full statement of it, only as the [relocation rules](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md) and the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md) allow.

## Cutting

Use the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md) to find the core claims and the paragraphs readers need to understand or evaluate them. Then check the request to see what you may delete or move.

### Deciding what to cut

**[Delete empty wording without asking](rules/cutting/bloat.md).** Delete [chatbot artifacts](rules/deslopping/chatbot-phrases.md), [generic openings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md), and [leaked artifacts](rules/deslopping/generation-artifacts.md), repair [em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md), and delete content announcements and signposts only when the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md) allows. Shorten remaining sentences without cutting [ideas](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md).

**[Cut or relocate ideas only on request](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md).** Cut or relocate substantive content (anything the author would miss: ideas, results, caveats, evidence, examples, reasons, and necessary transitions) only when the author asks to condense the text (not merely to polish or tighten) or sets a length limit; otherwise, keep it and flag it ("this paragraph is off-spine; cut it?"). Reordering within a section to [build understanding](rules/prose/explanation-order.md) is a prose edit; moving material between sections or out of the main text is relocation.

**[Apply the spine test before cutting sentences](rules/cutting/spine-test.md).** State the core claims (the text's spine) in one to three sentences, and each paragraph's role in one line. Keep content because the spine needs it, not because it is true or interesting. Classify each paragraph as **central** if it states a core claim, **supporting** if readers need it to understand or evaluate one or if it holds [protected content](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md), **ancillary** if it is relevant but readers can evaluate the core claims without it, and **off-spine** if it does not relate to them. If no short, specific title summarizes a section, flag its competing points before cutting. When [the request](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md) permits cutting, cut or relocate off-spine paragraphs, shorten ancillary ones, and then edit [sentence by sentence](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md). Keep a cut only if the argument remains at least as clear.

**[Follow the triage order to meet a length limit](rules/cutting/triage-order.md).** Preserving [protected content](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md) and following [relocation safeguards](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md), cut material in this order:

1. **Repeated points** across the introduction, section openings, and conclusion. Keep the most informative statement (usually the one that explains the point), one statement of each contribution in each of the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, and [shorthand reminders](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md). Keep a closing restatement when readers need the intervening explanation to understand the opening claim. Revise a word-for-word copy, such as a conclusion that repeats the abstract, to state established results, or flag it. Apply the [deletion test](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).
2. **Remaining signposts**, including the introduction's closing [roadmap](rules/prose/signposts.md). Keep references to evidence and proofs.
3. **Unrelated background and related work:** background that does not motivate the research question and citation lists that state no relation to the gap. Keep the few works and comparisons that define the gap.
4. **Minor results, secondary analyses, exploratory findings, and accounts of what the authors tried first.** Remove results that do not support a central claim from the main text, and relocate material that supports rigor: sensitivity analyses, robustness checks, and the negative result a skeptical reviewer will ask about. In the main text, keep any failure or counterevidence that alters a central claim, and state a failed approach as a negative result.
5. **Full parameter lists and sweeps, and subsidiary details.** Relocate them, but keep [explicit procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) and conditions in the main text so readers can reproduce the method in outline.

**[Protect sentences the argument depends on](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md).** Keep the material below even when it adds no new information, and move it only when the [sentence-by-sentence steps](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md) allow:

- Nonredundant hedges, qualifiers, scope conditions, limitations, supporting evidence, and counterevidence. Cut a repeated limitation only when the triage order reaches it and a full statement remains elsewhere. Removing a caveat changes a [claim's scope](rules/claims/scope.md); after an authorized cut, [recalibrate the claim](rules/cutting/post-cut-check.md).
- The reasons for method choices and the details needed to reproduce the work, such as the statistical tests, the handling of missing data, and corrections for multiple comparisons.
- Topic sentences, necessary framing, [high-level summaries](rules/prose/direct-content.md), [logical transitions](rules/prose/transitions.md), and references back to earlier sections that later text relies on. When cutting a minor result, usually keep the sentence on why this class of question matters if it frames surviving results. Keep [standalone caption content](rules/conventions/captions.md), including "adapted from" credits and permission notices, even when it repeats the body.
- Fair credit to prior work and accurate descriptions of it, acknowledgments, funding statements, and disclosures of assistance (including from AI tools) and of conflicts of interest.

### Making the cut

**[Repair text that depends on a sentence you cut or move](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md).** For each sentence within [the request's scope](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md):

1. **Check whether a core claim or the reader needs it;** if so, apply "Protect sentences the argument depends on".
2. **Check what depends on it:** a later reference such as "this" or "as shown above", a term defined or source cited only here, a hedge or evidence for another claim, or the paragraph's topic string. Repair every dependent passage in the same edit, or keep the sentence.
3. **Choose whether to delete or move it.** Delete only content that no reader needs, and never delete details needed to reproduce the work; move content that only some readers or a skeptical reviewer will need. Move material to an appendix or supplement only if the draft already has one; otherwise, keep it in place and flag what to move. Make moved material understandable on its own. In the main text, point to moved material where it becomes relevant. Keep enough of the method and the reasons for choosing it in the main text for readers to judge the results. If the venue permits footnotes, you may move secondary comments into new footnotes. Keep essential steps of a method, derivation, or algorithm, and the only definition of a term later text uses, in the main explanation, not in footnotes or parentheses.
4. **Read across the gap.** The sentence after the gap must open with a term, result, or referent supplied by the sentence before it. Restore any missing connection only from the draft, or keep the original sentence; if the link requires an inference that the deleted sentence made, add a clause to an adjacent sentence or reuse the original framing sentence to open what follows.

**[Revise cuts when stop rules apply](rules/cutting/stop-rules.md).** If a cut leaves a paragraph without needed explanation or support, or breaks its [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md), merge it with an adjacent paragraph that makes the same point, or restore what is missing. Before cutting a confusing passage, look for a missing step or [referent](rules/prose/referents.md) and restore it only from elsewhere in the draft, or flag it. If a reader outside the subfield cannot follow the shortened passage, restore the needed context and steps instead of cutting further. These stop rules override the length target. Never meet a target by adding negative `\vspace`, reducing font sizes, or narrowing figures; report the remaining excess instead.

**[Recheck claims, the argument, and references after cutting](rules/cutting/post-cut-check.md).** Each remaining claim must keep its evidence, scope, and hedges under the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md). Refer precisely to the new location of moved material, and weaken a claim only when the remaining evidence supports less, not because the evidence moved. Reread the problem, gap, approach, findings, and implications, and restore reasoning steps the cut removed. Repair references to cut text or nonexistent appendix sections, cite every remaining figure and table, and define each term before its first use.

## Prose

**[Explain ideas in the order readers need](rules/prose/explanation-order.md).** At any point, readers should understand as much as the preceding text could have explained. State the main idea before how it works, and a finding and its interpretation before the supporting evidence. Put a section's claim in its first paragraph, and open each paragraph as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md) directs. Define unfamiliar terms before using them. To test the order, cover everything after a given sentence and ask what the reader knows so far: if a covered sentence would explain more of the visible text than an uncovered one, move it earlier, but a sentence that only adds precision to an already understood claim can stay. You may reorder sentences within a paragraph and paragraphs within a section, but flag moves between sections. Within a sentence, follow [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md) and [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).

**[Apply the deletion test without changing meaning](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).** Test every clause by deleting it, and keep the deletion only if the word or clause adds nothing and the sentence stays as easy to understand. Every deletion, replacement, or restructuring must keep the [structural words](rules/prose/first-reading.md) readers need on the first reading, and must preserve who does what, what each word refers to, whether something is possible, required, or actual, definitions, logical relations, distinctions, the cases covered, and the author's stance. Apply the [claim rules](rules/claims/scope.md) to any substantive change. If you are unsure whether an edit changes the meaning, keep the original and flag the ambiguity.

### Paragraphs

**[Develop one point per paragraph](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md).** State the point as a specific claim in the first sentence ("The gain vanishes without pretraining") instead of [announcing](rules/prose/signposts.md) a theme ("Results were mixed"). Only when readers cannot understand the claim without context, give at most one sentence of context from the draft before it. A results paragraph may open with the specific question it tests if the next sentence answers it, followed by the evidence; a descriptive or procedural paragraph may open with its first fact or step, except where a Methods section opens with its key idea ([section openings](rules/story/section-openings.md)). Judge a paragraph by whether its point is developed and coherent, not by its sentence count. Keep both sides of a comparison in one paragraph when they support one point. Split a paragraph where its point changes, and merge short paragraphs only when they develop the same point.

**[Use lists when readers need separate items](rules/prose/list-structure.md).** Use a list when readers must tell items apart or follow them one by one. If bullets make an argument but hide reasons or connections between claims, rewrite them as connected prose. Group long lists using categories the draft establishes, retaining every item and qualification and distinguishing primary from secondary items. Suggest a table when readers compare objects across shared properties. Remove item numbers that imply an unsupported order or ranking, unless the text refers to them. For a list of contributions or objectives, flag rather than make any regrouping, renumbering, or conversion to prose.

**[Use connecting words only for relations the draft supports](rules/prose/transitions.md).** Replace "as", "since", or "while" when it leaves time, cause, or contrast ambiguous. Use "conversely" only when the second statement reverses the first. Remove a redundant connecting word, but keep one that readers need. Flag "therefore" if the preceding statements do not support the conclusion. Follow the [causal-evidence rule](rules/claims/causal-language.md) for causal words.

**[Finish one topic before starting another](rules/prose/topic-order.md).** Repair any unexplained return to an earlier topic (A, B, A). In a comparison organized by named properties, such as weight or cost, each property can be the topic: discuss every method on one property before moving to the next, instead of regrouping the text by method.

**[Check sentence openings with the topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md).** When individual sentences are clear but readers cannot follow the paragraph, list each sentence's opening words (the topic string) and check that they advance the paragraph's point rather than merely repeating words. If concepts share a theme but their connection is unclear, state it from the draft or flag the gap. Consecutive sentences may keep the same topic, and a new term at a sentence's end need not recur unless the text promises to explain it. If most sentences start with new material, rewrite them to start with [old information](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). Next, insert the implied connective in brackets at each sentence boundary ("[however]", "[therefore]"). If no connective fits, or you cannot tell which fits, the link is missing: supply it from the draft or flag it. If "[therefore]" fits but a reader outside the subfield could not explain why the second sentence follows, flag the missing step in the argument. If two adjacent paragraphs could swap places without other edits, add a [summary naming their parallel items](rules/prose/direct-content.md) when they present parallel items, or state how the second depends on the first.

### Information flow within a sentence

**[Put old information before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md).** Open each sentence with a term from the preceding text, or a part, property, or alternative that readers can infer from it; a concept known in the field is not old information until the text introduces it. Among several old items, open with the one the sentence discusses, or the most recent one if they are equally relevant. When changing voice or clause order, keep the paragraph's topic as the main subject. Keep useful passives under [alternative sentence orders](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).

**[Put important new information in the stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).** The stress position is the end of a sentence or independent clause. Check sentence, paragraph, and section endings for undeserved emphasis; move incidental material earlier. Move a qualifier such as "in these tests" that follows the main point to the front instead of deleting it, unless it is a short condition that is clear at the end ([first reading](rules/prose/first-reading.md)): "In our experiments, adding the auxiliary loss improves accuracy by 12%", not "Accuracy improves by 12% when we add the auxiliary loss, in our experiments". If several important items share one ending, split the sentence so that each gets its own ending. Check whether a paragraph's first sentence ends with the topic that the paragraph develops. When joining or subordinating clauses, keep the relative emphasis of findings and qualifications. Move an essential qualification out of parentheses if they make it look optional. Keep each scope condition with the claim it limits. Follow [pause punctuation](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) when using colons or semicolons.

**[Keep old information first in passives, fronting, and inversions](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).** In a passive with a by-phrase, keep the subject at least as familiar as the actor in that phrase. A fronted phrase or an inversion ("Closest to our work is X") must begin with old information; otherwise, use ordinary subject-verb-object order. In that order, you may depart from [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md) and [final emphasis](rules/prose/stress-position.md) when doing so is clearer. Keep a passive, "there is", or "it ... that" construction when it introduces new information without making that information the subject.

### Sentence structure

**[Put actors in subjects and actions in verbs](rules/prose/character-action.md).** Rewrite a nominalization as a verb when it follows a weak verb ("perform an evaluation of", "lead to an improvement in") or shares a clause with other nominalizations. List a paragraph's main verbs: if most are "is", "has", or "leads to", check whether its actions are in nouns or missing. Flag any actor, or any action of one actor on another, that the draft never states. Keep a nominalization that names a technique ("quantization") or a recurring concept, refers to the previous sentence ("This reduction in noise ..."), replaces an awkward "the fact that" clause, or appears alone in a clear sentence.

Keep the subject-verb pairing literal: the figurative "the schedule launches the jobs" needs the actual actor ("we launch the jobs on the schedule"), but a component, instrument, or organism may be the subject of an operation it performs ("the filter removes duplicates"); keep an instrument or organism as that subject. When an inanimate subject takes a verb of intending, thinking, perceiving, feeling, remembering, or eating ("hopes", "forgets"), replace the verb with what literally happens, as the draft states it: "the parser swallows the error" becomes "the parser catches the error and returns False". Keep established technical verbs with inanimate subjects ("the function expects an integer", "the model learns"), and a result or figure as the subject of a [reporting verb](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) ("the results suggest"). Follow [voice](rules/conventions/active-voice.md) and [information flow](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).

**[Make every sentence clear on the first reading](rules/prose/first-reading.md).** Length alone is not a defect. Put subjects next to verbs and verbs next to their objects or required phrases ("compare X with Y"): "the five expression types (attribute access, subscripts, calls, nested calls, and string literals) leave the cursor in place" becomes "the five expression types leave the cursor in place: attribute access, subscripts, calls, nested calls, and string literals". Shorten a long opening phrase or clause. Move a long subject clause toward the end ("It remains open whether the gain survives at larger scale"), and, when grammar allows either order, put the longer of two phrases last, unless the long part states the old information. Keep short, familiar asides. Move an interruption that introduces new material or a full clause ("the gain, once the cache is warm, disappears") to the beginning or end, make it a separate sentence, or cut it if it adds nothing, unless it states old information readers need there. Never nest one such interruption inside another before the main verb.

Rewrite strings of three or more nouns with a preposition or a clause: "the retrieval failure recovery path" becomes "the code path that runs when retrieval fails". Also break up long sequences of modifiers, usually more than three words before the head noun. Count an established technical name ("large language model") as one noun.

Keep articles so that sentences read as prose, not notes ("Model reaches 90% accuracy on benchmark"). Keep optional words that mark where a long or hard-to-predict clause begins. Restore "that" after verbs such as "assume", "suppose", "show", "prove", or "find" when readers could at first take the next noun as the verb's object ("We assume that the samples ..."); after other verbs, adjectives ("certain that ..."), and conjunctions ("so that") when a long clause follows; and before a long relative clause ("the bound that the analysis in Section 4 gives"). Restore "that were" in a shortened clause such as "the samples stored in the cabinet" when readers could take "stored" for the main verb. Use "in order to" when a bare "to" could look like a value ("we limit the batch size in order to fit in memory"). Do not add optional words to short, predictable clauses, or "that" after "we have".

Read from left to right for words that mislead at first: "We prove that A and B imply C" first reads as proving A, so write "We prove that the two conditions A and B together imply C". Make clear which noun a relative clause or verb-form phrase describes. Put "only" immediately before the word it restricts: "We only evaluate on two datasets" restricts the verb, and "We evaluate on only two datasets" restricts the count, which is usually what is meant. Put a condition before its claim unless it is short and clear at the end ("unbiased under assumption A1"), give a long condition its own sentence ("Suppose that A, B, and C hold. Then X."), and list three or more steps or conditions instead of packing them into one sentence. If a sentence still packs in too many concepts, follow [definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).

**[Read the sentence aloud](rules/prose/read-aloud.md).** Listen for where you hesitate, cannot tell what to stress, or miss a connection, and fix the structure, not only the punctuation. A sentence that reads smoothly can still be unclear if it relies on pauses, stress, or background knowledge. Do not add a [writerly aside](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md) telling readers how to read it.

**[Give parallel ideas the same grammatical form without changing their meaning](rules/prose/parallel-form.md).** Ideas are parallel when they have the same logical role; do not rename unlike objects or drop conditions merely to force symmetry. When "and" or "or" joins items and it is unclear which modifiers and verbs apply to each item, repeat words or split the sentence; if the draft does not make clear which reading is meant, flag the ambiguity instead of choosing one. Compare things of the same kind, restoring an omitted quantity or verb when readers need it to tell what is compared with what ("the mass of the steel ball exceeds the mass of the wooden ball"). When joining items as equals hides a causal or qualifying relation, state the relation the draft supports. Keep clear natural variation.

**[Do not open a sentence with "And", "But", "So", or "Plus"](rules/prose/sentence-initial-conjunctions.md).** Keep the relation the conjunction signaled. A "But" that corrects or limits the previous sentence becomes "However," or joins that sentence as ", but ...". Keep a cited author's objection theirs: "Lee (2021) objects that the bound is loose", not "However, the bound is loose (Lee, 2021)". Use "Finally" for the last item of a series, or join the item to the series, not "In addition", "Moreover", or "Furthermore", which add support. A "So" that introduces a consequence becomes "Thus," "Hence," or "Therefore," or joins the previous sentence as ", so ...". Vary the connective. Keep clear uses of these conjunctions within a sentence ("we expect a gain but find none").

### Content instead of promises

**[State the content directly](rules/prose/direct-content.md).** When a sentence only promises later content, calls a topic complex, or gives a quantity or category but defers the content ("Three changes alter the reported accuracies, and we note each where it lands"), replace it with the draft's content, or flag the gap. Delete trailing promises such as "and we discuss each below" when the content is already present. Keep a summary of the main idea, a count whose items follow immediately ("We make four observations. First, ..."), and statements of real uncertainty, a real dispute, an unresolved definition, or a [limitation](rules/claims/limitations.md). A sentence is empty if, read without the rest of the paragraph, it amounts to "there are some, and they are below".

**[State findings instead of how they are reported](rules/prose/reporting-policies.md).** When a sentence only states a reporting policy, such as "we report both metrics", replace it with the draft's conclusions, measurements, and outcomes, or flag the missing results. Keep a sentence that describes an actual reporting or analysis procedure when it explains what the authors did rather than replacing a result.

**[State the content a signpost announces](rules/prose/signposts.md).** Replace a signpost with the content it announces and, if needed, a location reference: replace "The previous section described the apparatus. This section presents the measurements." with the measurements themselves. Keep references that locate content ("as proved in Appendix A"; subject to the [abstract rule](rules/story/section-functions.md)), [genuine transitions and summaries](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md), and a roadmap of at most two sentences at the end of an introduction or chapter opening, unless the contributions already name their sections ([contribution claims](rules/story/contribution-claims.md)). Delete announcements that state no content ("Section 7 concludes"), roadmaps within a section that repeat its subheadings, and signposts that take more effort to understand than they save. In a kept roadmap, check that the count, order, names, and distinctions of its items match the actual sections or items, and correct mismatches that the draft resolves unambiguously. Flag conflicting categories.

**[State the reasons for a choice](rules/prose/choice-reasons.md).** Unless the omitted items are obvious, replace "for various reasons" or a list's final "etc." with the draft's reasons or items, or flag the gap. When a claim depends on an unexplained method choice, give the draft's reason or flag its absence; do not ask authors to justify routine choices whose role is clear. A reason for a choice is not evidence that the choice caused a result ([causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md)).

### Concrete quantities and operations

**[Replace empty verbs with specific changes or operations](rules/prose/empty-verbs.md).** When "affects", "varies", or "has implications for" reports an unspecified change, state its direction and size, or flag the missing measurement. When "provides", "enables", or "allows" states only an outcome ("the cache enables fast retrieval"), state the operation that produces it, or flag the missing operation. Keep wording that already explains the change or capability. For an explicitly untested hypothesis, state the hypothesized direction without requiring a measured size.

**[Name the measured quantity instead of an umbrella noun](rules/prose/umbrella-nouns.md).** For example, replace "performance" or "quality" with accuracy, yield, or energy use. Keep a defined collective label when the statement applies to all quantities it covers.

**[Replace vague comparisons with measurements](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md).** For comparatives such as "more" or "faster", use the reported measurement and name what it is compared with: write "Our method runs in 0.3 s per example versus 1.1 s for the baseline (3.7× faster)", not "Our method is much faster". A label such as "large" needs a stated comparison or threshold and cannot replace the measurement. Do not calculate a ratio that the draft does not report, such as a speedup or a fold change.

**[Cut words that add only emphasis](rules/prose/intensifiers.md).** Replace words such as "very" and "extremely" with the [measurement](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md) when the draft supplies it, and otherwise cut only the emphasis. Treat softeners such as "essentially" and "largely" as [hedges](rules/claims/hedges.md). Delete "Note that" and similar prompts unless they prevent a likely misreading. Keep "obviously", "clearly", or "trivially" only before a step in a derivation or proof that is routine for readers outside the subfield, and flag any unexplained nonroutine step or wording that substitutes for an argument.

### Plain wording

**[Say what literally happens](rules/prose/literal-wording.md).** Name the actual event, object, and quantity, in words you would use at a whiteboard. Replace "utilize" or "leverage" with "use" when the meaning is the same. Replace an abstract word such as "approach", "framework", "strategy", or "issue" with the more specific thing it stands for, unless the word is defined and reused as shorthand: "we adopt a data-augmentation strategy to address the issue of overfitting" becomes "we augment the training data to reduce overfitting". A functional label names an object by its role in the authors' setup ("the assessment word" for "the word *true* or *false*"); name the object before describing its role.

Replace a figurative verb with the literal event the draft states, without guessing from the image: a loader "retires" malformed rows (the rows are deleted), or a filter "passes on" borderline examples (the filter accepts, or rejects, them). Rewrite a noun used as a temporary verb as a verb phrase naming the action ("we footnote the caveat" becomes "we state the caveat in a footnote"), following the [parts-of-speech rule](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md). Rewrite unusual word pairs that readers must decode ("verified red") in ordinary wording ("verified that the test fails on the original code"). Keep established technical terms, verbs ("we benchmark"), and word combinations. Replace an opaque phrasal verb ("dial back") only with an exact equivalent.

Keep an analogy whose correspondence the draft makes clear, replace decorative or misleading imagery with literal wording, and never add a new analogy. For slogan-like sentences, follow the [aphorism rule](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md). When explaining an unfamiliar term, keep the population, metric, and denominator it covers. Only when the draft makes the meaning clear, rewrite an ambiguous preposition such as "with", "by", or "in", and make clear which item "replace" or "substitute" removes and which takes its place; otherwise, flag the ambiguity.

**[Use logical words unambiguously](rules/prose/logical-words.md).** Replace ambiguous "any" with "every" or "at least one", whichever the draft means ("the bound holds for every learning rate below 1/L"). If "all" with a plural noun could suggest that objects share one value, use "each" with a singular noun ("each sample is held at its own temperature"). Rewrite a negative statement as a positive one only when both hold in exactly the same cases ("not nonincreasing" does not mean "increasing"), including values equal to a threshold. Keep a deliberate denial as a denial. With "not" and "because", clarify whether the denial concerns the outcome or its proposed cause. When "can" or "should" could mean observation, capability, possibility, prediction, recommendation, or obligation, use the meaning the draft establishes, or flag the ambiguity; also follow [hedges](rules/claims/hedges.md) and [performed actions](rules/conventions/performed-actions.md). Keep impossibility, prohibition, and lack of obligation distinct when negating modals. Replace an ambiguous slash in prose with the words it stands for, such as "or" or "and", keeping standard abbreviations and notation. Preserve whether a list is complete; "includes" alone does not imply omitted members. Keep "then" after an "if" clause whose conclusion is a formula or technical claim. When "contains" could mean membership or subset inclusion, state which. Combine nested conditions ("if A, then if B, then C") into one ("if A and B, then C") only when both govern the same conclusion, and keep necessary and sufficient conditions distinct. Distinguish "not all" from "none". When it affects the claim, clarify whether "some" allows all cases or means fewer than all. When "if" introduces a comparison rather than a condition, state the comparison separately from the claim.

**[Spell out definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).** Describe every metric, rule, or procedure readers rely on well enough to interpret and recompute its results, usually in one or two full sentences. A definition must name the kind of thing and its distinguishing traits; expanding an acronym is not enough. Pair formal definitions readers need with plain explanations, and for related concepts, state their shared category and their differences. When definitions compete, state which one the paper adopts and the draft's reason, without inventing competing definitions. Do not flag standard steps covered by a precise citation, but keep every modification and the explanation readers need at that point. For a procedure that only lists steps, state its purpose from the draft, or flag it. Flag a worked example that does not match the stated method or definition, without changing either. During a story or overclaiming check, or when a result depends on an unstated setting, check that the draft states the settings its kind of work needs, such as an algorithm's preconditions, the seeds and software versions of numerical work, an experiment's calibration, data exclusions, or how participants were recruited and blinded; flag each missing item without filling in a plausible default. Delete or move details needed for reproduction only where the [cutting rules](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md) allow it. When you cannot follow an explanation, find the missing step with an example already in the draft, or flag the gap. Build difficult explanations from a simple case through necessary complications, using cases from the draft. Reuse an illustrative input across methods unless it hides their differences. Prefer an available real input that explains the operation; retain toy cases when they explain it better. State what each example illustrates without inventing an outcome for any case.

**[Resolve references without changing the claim's status](rules/prose/referents.md).** Make each pronoun, demonstrative, comparative, and shorthand noun phrase identify one referent from the text alone. Name ambiguous baselines or previous runs with the draft's labels ("the v1.2 measurement in Section 4.1") and reuse those names. The first sentence under a section title or a run-in paragraph title must make sense without it ("**Gradient caching.** Gradient caching recomputes ...", not "**Gradient caching.** This technique recomputes ..."). Restore context lost during editing, and flag connections you cannot resolve rather than choosing a referent. Replace an ambiguous "this", "that", or "it" with wording that names the referent the draft supports, preserving its status and evaluation (a conjecture is not a finding, and a change is not necessarily an improvement): in "We prune low-magnitude weights and retrain. This improves generalization.", write "This pruning step improves generalization" if the draft identifies pruning as the cause. Allow bare "This" to introduce an interpretation after one unambiguous claim in openings such as "This means that", "This is because", and "This suggests that".

**[State claims and reasons in complete, literal sentences](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md).** Replace conversational idioms such as "Call it a wash" and editorial commands with complete, literal statements of the claim and the draft's reason for it, or flag a missing reason: "Treat this as an upper bound." becomes "This estimate is an upper bound, because the scan also counts duplicates." Keep a deliberately short sentence when it is complete and literal. Follow [intensifiers](rules/prose/intensifiers.md) for "Note that" and similar prompts. Preserve the author's supported stance under [author voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).

## Conventions

When a convention in this section conflicts with general writing advice, follow the convention.

### Voice and tense

**[Use active voice and keep the actor consistent](rules/conventions/active-voice.md).** Use active "we" for the authors' actions and decisions, but not for the research community or people in general; "we" may include the reader in derivations and proofs. In method descriptions, use either the method ("the algorithm refines the estimate") or "we" ("we refine the estimate") throughout, but "we", not the method, for design choices ("we set k = 5"). Keep a single author's choice of "I" or "we" and apply it consistently. Keep passives when the actor is obvious, irrelevant, or unknown ("Images are normalized to zero mean"); when they put [old information first](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md); when a long actor belongs at the end; and in clear passive modifiers ("the samples selected by technicians"). Do not assign an action to the authors when the draft omits the actor.

**[State performed actions directly](rules/conventions/performed-actions.md).** When the draft shows an action was performed, replace "can", "aims to", or "seeks to" with a direct verb. Keep them for a capability the study did not use or a goal the authors have not reached, where removing them would [change the claim](rules/claims/unsupported-labels.md). Replace the ungrammatical "allows to model" with "models" or "allows us to model", as the draft means.

**[Match tense to the time frame](rules/conventions/tense.md).** Use present tense for the paper's contents ("Section 4 shows", not "we will show"), results, method and setup, established facts, and prior work that still applies ("Smith et al. propose"). Use past tense for events at a specific time outside the described procedure ("we collected 500 documents in 2024"), and keep it for reported findings ("Lee found that the alloy was weaker"), hypotheticals ("if the sample were larger"), and a draft's consistent past tense for experiments. Use future tense only for uncompleted plans, as in a proposal. Keep tense consistent within each time frame. Prefer simple to perfect tense ("Smith et al. show" or "showed", not "have shown"), but keep perfect tense for current relevance ("no prior work has evaluated X at this scale") or an event before another past event ("had tested"). Do not turn a general research recommendation into the authors' own promise. Replace a relative date, such as "last year", with the draft's calendar date, or flag it.

### Terminology

**[Use one term per concept](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md).** Do not alternate terms such as "device", "sensor", and "system" for variety, including in captions and figure labels; vary sentence structure and function words instead. Keep different names only for distinct concepts, such as different types, scopes, or measured quantities. A clear pronoun or bare demonstrative may refer back to the term in the same or the next sentence, but repeat a specific name rather than replace it with a general category noun ("this compound"). To find synonyms, list the names the draft uses for each recurring concept, and flag any inconsistency that prose edits cannot fix, such as a mismatched figure label. When describing a parallel or more general case, repeat the earlier wording exactly, then state what differs. When naming a formula's role helps readers follow without looking back, name its established role, such as a bound or definition, rather than calling every formula an equation.

**[Avoid using one technical term as both a noun and a verb](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md).** When a technical term appears as both noun and verb in one sentence ("we sample 1,000 samples") and forces rereading, replace the verb with an ordinary one ("we draw 1,000 samples") or split the sentence; otherwise, leave the sentence alone.

**[Spell out acronyms and introduce few new ones](rules/conventions/acronyms.md).** Spell out an acronym where repeated use begins, and use the full term for isolated earlier mentions. Define it separately in the abstract and body, and again at the start of each major section or chapter where it returns. Prefer the full term unless the acronym is standard or shortens a long, recurring name; spell out an acronym that appears once or twice, has another common expansion in the field ("LR"), or saves no syllables read aloud ("TS" for time step). Flag a title acronym that readers outside the subfield would not know before the abstract, and propose a spelled-out title, but leave the title unchanged. Flag opaque coined labels rather than renaming them. If an acronym you would replace with its full term also appears in tables, figures, or code, flag the replacement instead.

**[Remind readers what shorthand means after a gap](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md).** When shorthand or a symbol returns after a long gap or section boundary, remind readers of its name and either its definition or where it was defined ("the graph-search step (Dijkstra's algorithm)", "the contrastive loss L_c defined in Section 3"), phrased as a reminder, not a new definition. Replace ambiguous labels such as "the rate" with the original full name, and name a distant display referred to by number alone ("the energy balance (9)").

**[State what ambiguous or suggestive terms mean](rules/conventions/suggestive-terms.md).** A suggestive coinage names a technical object with a colloquial expression (an "intelligent" coating). An overloaded term changes a precise meaning used elsewhere ("deconvolution" for transposed convolution); also check technical words used loosely ("exponential" for rapid growth). For a suitcase word, which has several meanings in the field ("generalization": train-to-test performance, transfer, or validity outside the experiment), state which meaning applies. Flag coined suggestive terms, even when defined, and suggest literal alternatives. Keep established technical wording ("the enzyme recognizes its substrate"), but explain the literal operation at first use. When an ordinary word collides with a nearby technical term ("a normal sample" near "normal distribution"), replace the ordinary word and keep the technical term. Replace an intention or mental state attributed to a system ("the particle wants to minimize its energy") with the literal operation or the authors' design goal, or flag it; follow [marks and forms](rules/conventions/marks-and-forms.md) for distancing quotes. Follow [definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) for unfamiliar categories and competing definitions.

### Mathematics

**[Define each symbol by its first use and give it one meaning](rules/conventions/symbol-definitions.md).** An informal definition suffices at first use; [refresh it after a gap](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md). Give each object one symbol, and introduce no symbol the text does not use again ("Every continuous function on a compact set is bounded" needs no "f"). Flag conflicting meanings, symbols used against their standard meaning in the field, unused definitions, easily confused symbols, dense notation, and functions equated with their values ("$V = 0.31$" meaning "$V(x_0) = 0.31$"). Keep local definitions near the passages that use them. Move scattered definitions of symbols used throughout the paper into an existing Preliminaries or Notation section only if the request [allows relocation](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md); otherwise, flag them. Never create such a section. Suggest, rather than make, any renaming or other notation improvement, such as words in place of an index that distinguishes nothing, a short name for a recurring complex expression, or a vector, set, or other collection grouping related quantities.

Flag unspecified factor order in noncommuting products and inconsistent use of definition signs. For ranges, flag unclear finite bounds, endpoint inclusion, index origin, element types, or empty-range conventions. Supply conventions only from the draft; never assume commutativity or merge boundary cases to shorten notation.

**[Write mathematics as grammatical prose](rules/conventions/math-prose.md).** An equality can supply a sentence's verb; to check the grammar, mentally replace each formula with words that play the same role and reread. Begin sentences and clauses with words, not bare symbols ("A function f is semicontinuous if...", not "f is semicontinuous if..."), and spell out sentence-initial numbers regardless of the number-spelling cutoff. Separate adjacent formulas with words ("Consider S_q, where q < p"), even across sentence boundaries. Reword a sentence-final symbol that could be read as part of the next name or abbreviation. Except in work on logic, write quantifiers and implications in prose as words ("for all" for $\forall$). Never let a relation symbol stand for words in a verbal phrase ("is at most 0.1", not "is $\le 0.1$"). End a displayed formula with a period when the sentence ends or a comma when it continues. Put a colon before a display only after a complete clause ("we define the loss as follows:"), never after an incomplete one ("the loss is:"). A trailing "where" clause may define symbols, but put any definition needed to understand a claim before the claim: in "Every convex polynomial has even degree, where a function is convex if ...", move the definition of convexity first. In LaTeX source, put no blank lines around a displayed formula that continues its paragraph.

**[Explain why an equation or definition is needed](rules/conventions/formal-material-purpose.md).** Flag any central equation, definition, or intermediate result (a lemma, theorem, or algorithm) whose purpose the draft never states; do not infer one. Move the draft's explanation of why such material is needed ahead of it within the same section; flag a move from another section. Follow [formal support](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md) when an application depends on unstated conditions.

**[Use the correct type for operators, subscripts, and signs](rules/conventions/math-typography.md).** Use upright type for multi-letter operators (`\log`, `\operatorname{softmax}`) and word subscripts (`\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}`), but italic for variable subscripts (`x_i`). Use × (`\times`), not x or `*` (except in code), for multiplication and multiplicative factors, and write negative values in math mode for minus signs (`$-0.3$`), not hyphens. Fix LaTeX typography only where mathematical meaning stays unchanged, and flag any change to symbols, grouping, delimiters, or operator meaning. Propose a clearer form for ambiguous notation, such as a slash fraction whose denominator has no clear end, only when the draft establishes the intended meaning. Flag stacked inline fractions that disrupt line spacing, exponent fractions too small to read, and oversized delimiters; suggest slash fractions or smaller delimiters only with grouping preserved.

**[Number referenced and central displays](rules/conventions/display-numbering.md).** Number central displays, such as the objective and the main bound, even when the text never refers to them; leave unreferenced intermediate steps unnumbered. Identify rejected formulas and counterexamples beside their displays using the draft's stated status; retain central counterexamples and negative results.

**[Make theorem statements self-contained](rules/conventions/theorem-statements.md).** Introduce a theorem, lemma, or algorithm with a complete sentence. In the formal statement, name its objects and any needed definition reference ("The function H defined in (5) is continuous"), and define every symbol before use; keep motivation and informal comments outside it. If hypotheses run for several lines, flag the statement and suggest extracting them into named conditions defined before it ("Assumption 1 (smoothness)").

**[Open each proof with its strategy and state when each goal is met](rules/conventions/proof-strategy.md).** Make clear whether each statement is assumed, defined, established, or deferred to a later proof. Treat cases in the order and under the names the proof announces, and at the transition from constructing an object to using it, state the property that justifies the next step. Add strategy or progress statements only from the draft; otherwise, flag the omission. Follow [intensifiers](rules/prose/intensifiers.md) for "clearly" and "it is easy to see". Flag deferred proofs whose stated dependencies form a cycle; a forward reference alone is no defect. Flag missing equality links between successive bounds when the draft establishes that their right-hand sides are equal.

### Citations, figures, and tables

**[Keep citation markers out of subject and object positions](rules/conventions/citation-markers.md).** Replace a citation marker used as a subject or object ("[8] introduces") with a textual citation that names the authors. When asserting an established result, shorten "In a recent study, ... showed that X" to "X (citation)". Keep cited authors as the subject when reporting, characterizing, or contrasting a claim the paper does not assert, following [prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md). Place each citation beside its clause when the draft identifies that link; otherwise, flag a trailing cluster that obscures which source supports which claim. Flag any passage that leaves unclear where a source's material begins or ends. Use the document's citation package commands, such as natbib's `\citet` (textual) and `\citep` (parenthetical), with a non-breaking space before a parenthetical citation (`result~\citep{key}`). Use upright "et al." only for three or more authors.

**[Flag missing citations and citation keys absent from the references](rules/conventions/missing-citations.md).** Flag a technical claim presented as prior knowledge without an identifiable source ("It is well known that"), and any citation key missing from the supplied references. Common knowledge needs no citation, but never delete an existing citation as unnecessary. Preserve secondhand wording such as "as cited in". Follow [vague attribution](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md) for "Studies show". For a specific passage or result cited from a book or long survey, flag a missing page, section, or theorem locator.

**[Write captions that stand alone and state the finding](rules/conventions/captions.md).** A caption that only names its figure or table ("Results on ImageNet.") is incomplete: complete it with facts from the citing paragraph, and flag those absent from the draft. Identify quantities, units, axes, and conditions (dataset, model, setting); explain each visual encoding (line styles, colors, shaded regions, [error bars](rules/claims/variability.md)); and state whether higher or lower is better when that is not obvious. State the supported finding, check that it matches the body text, and name compared methods or conditions directly, not by color alone. In tables, flag unexplained blanks, dashes, and columns with mixed or missing units. Flag claims drawn from visual slopes or distances that a plot's stated axis ranges, dual vertical axes, or fitted-curve range do not support, and any extrapolated value described as measured; a truncated axis is not by itself an error.

Flag table layouts that separate comparison partners or obscure groups; suggest adjacent entries and ordering that exposes the intended comparison, preserving meaningful chronological or semantic order.

**[Cite each figure and table in the sentence that states its finding](rules/conventions/figure-table-references.md).** Replace announcements like "In Figure 6 we show the results" with the finding and its reference ("Accuracy falls as the noise rate rises (Figure 6)" or "Figure 6 shows that accuracy falls ..."; in the abstract, follow the [abstract rule](rules/story/section-functions.md)). Point to the specific panel or comparison the draft identifies, with numbered references rather than relative pointers such as "below". When a claim needs an exact value readers can only estimate from a plot, state the draft's value in the sentence or caption, or flag its absence. Flag unresolved citation or reference markers ("??"). Write `Figure~\ref{...}` with a non-breaking space, and capitalize "Figure", "Table", "Section", and "Theorem" for numbered items. Place an existing `\label` after its `\caption`.

### Typography

**[Make every quantity unambiguous without changing it](rules/conventions/quantities.md).** Give every measurement and axis its unit, separated from the number by a non-breaking space (`0.3~s`). Preserve established number formats, including a number-spelling cutoff that the draft or its style guide applies consistently; match forms within a comparison only when value and precision stay unchanged. Resolve ambiguous unit meanings, measurement bases, binary or decimal prefixes, and duration formats from the draft, or flag them. Flag, rather than perform, any unit conversion.

**[Use marks and type styles only for meaning, and keep the draft's accepted forms](rules/conventions/marks-and-forms.md).** Use sentence case for headings unless the venue requires title case; remove decorative bold and italics, but keep definition italics and emphasis that affects meaning. Use quotation marks only for quoted text, words mentioned as words, and words used loosely or without endorsement (the model's "reasoning"), not around a word used in its ordinary sense. Beside a word used loosely, state the draft's explanation, or flag the word if the draft never explains it. Check that commas marking a non-restrictive clause match the intended meaning ("the encoder, which has 12 layers, ..."); do not add commas merely because a restrictive clause uses "which", or around a symbol that names the preceding noun ("a directed graph G with source s"); and change restrictive "which" to "that" only when the venue requires it.

Change accepted spelling varieties and term forms only to meet a venue requirement or resolve a genuine inconsistency; if the draft mixes two acceptable forms ("dataset" and "data set") and the venue specifies neither, use the more frequent one. Keep an established field term unhyphenated when the draft consistently writes it so ("a large language model"). Put sentence punctuation outside an exact literal, such as a command or string, unless it belongs to the literal. Use "cf." only to mean "compare", never for "see".

## Deslopping

Patterns common in AI-written prose fall into two groups:

1. **Blacklisted patterns.** Delete every instance, even a single one. The three-instance threshold below does not apply.
2. **Tolerable patterns.** Edit a single instance when the edit makes the sentence clearer. Rewrite a whole passage only when it contains three or more instances of tolerable patterns.

**[Preserve the author's voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).** Keep the draft's [varied rhythm](rules/deslopping/repetitive-syntax.md) and the authors' genuine opinions and reactions; never add opinions, reactions, emphasis, sentence fragments, opposing positions, or unstated details. [Verify that each rewrite keeps the meaning](rules/prose/deletion-test.md). Never treat ["we"](rules/conventions/active-voice.md), [present tense](rules/conventions/tense.md) for the text or prior work, or [repeated technical terms](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md) as defects merely because generated prose uses them. [Matching a claim's confidence to its evidence](rules/claims/scope.md) adds no opinion.

### Blacklisted patterns

**[Delete em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md).** Delete every em dash (`---` in LaTeX) and every en dash used as a pause. Replace the dash with a colon, semicolon, or parentheses only if that mark passes the [pause punctuation](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) test in that position and, for parentheses, does not make an essential qualification look optional under [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md); otherwise, rewrite the sentence without the pause and keep its emphasis. Keep hyphens in compound words, and keep en dashes (`--`) in numeric ranges and in names formed from two people's names ("Sections 3–5", "Kullback–Leibler").

**[Delete conversational replies left by a chatbot](rules/deslopping/chatbot-phrases.md).** Delete "Certainly!", "Great question", "I hope this helps", "As of my last update", and similar replies to the user. Keep such wording in material the paper analyzes, such as a prompt, a model output, or text shown to study participants.

**[Delete generic openings and closings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md).** Delete filler that could fit any paper in the field, even a sentence that names the topic ("In the ever-evolving field of deep learning", "The future looks bright"), including popularity claims that sound factual ("There has been much recent interest in X"). If the draft gives a specific reason the topic matters, use that reason instead, and if readers need background first, follow [brief setup](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md).

**[Remove stray citation codes and placeholders, and flag missing information](rules/deslopping/generation-artifacts.md).** Remove artifacts of an unedited paste, such as chat citation codes (`citeturn0search0`, `contentReference[oaicite:...]`) and unfilled placeholders ("[Insert citation]"), keeping the surrounding claim and flagging the missing information. Flag AI-referrer URL parameters such as `utm_source=chatgpt.com` for removal.

### Tolerable patterns

**[Apply prose rules to ornate words, actions written as nouns, counts, and lists](rules/deslopping/prose-rule-patterns.md).** Use [literal wording](rules/prose/literal-wording.md) for ornate words such as "delve", "underscore", "showcase", and "pivotal", keeping an ornate word when it is precise; [character and action](rules/prose/character-action.md) for clauses that express several actions as nouns; [direct content](rules/prose/direct-content.md) for deferred counts; and [list structure](rules/prose/list-structure.md) for bulleted arguments.

**[Expand compressed wording that slows the first reading](rules/deslopping/compressed-constructions.md).** Check for [inanimate subjects given human actions](rules/prose/character-action.md), [nouns used as verbs and unusual word pairs](rules/prose/literal-wording.md), and [noun strings, omitted "that" or articles, and long gaps between subject and verb](rules/prose/first-reading.md), and keep short forms that are clear on the first reading.

**[Repair pause punctuation between a claim and its evidence](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md).** Read the clause before each colon or semicolon on its own. If it states no specific fact, as in "Annealing helps: conductivity rises by 40%", join the claim to its evidence in words ("..., as shown by Vasquez (2024), who reports ...") or delete the vague clause when the evidence states the whole claim; a period in place of the mark does not fix the vague clause. If it states a specific fact, keep a semicolon before a related fact or a colon before an explanation or example, but never a colon that implies an identity, a cause, or a complete list that the draft does not support. A colon may also introduce a list, a displayed formula under [mathematical punctuation](rules/conventions/math-prose.md), a quoted definition, or a label, unless it separates a vague claim from its evidence. Restore any missing verb in a phrase after a comma ("adds 3 ms, below the limit" becomes "adds 3 ms, which is below the limit"), but keep appositives, clear participial phrases, and parallel phrases that share an earlier verb ("the tank holds 5 L, the bottle 1 L").

**[Replace narration of the thinking process with conclusions and evidence](rules/deslopping/reasoning-leakage.md).** Such narration includes "Let us think step by step", "Breaking this down", and numbered steps narrating the writer's own thinking. Keep mathematical setup ("Let X be"), the steps of a proof, substantive reasoning that the result depends on, and model reasoning that the paper quotes or analyzes as experimental material.

**[Describe the work without narrating manuscript revisions](rules/deslopping/revision-narration.md).** Replace revision phrases such as "In this revised version, we clarify" or "As suggested by the reviewer" with the scientific content itself; such accounts belong only in a response letter or change log, where they stay. Keep all added explanations, evidence, and justifications, and any comparison between method versions that is part of the study.

**[Name the cited source when an attribution is vague](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md).** For attributions such as "studies show", "experts believe", or "X has been shown to", name a source only when the draft cites one for that claim, following [citation-marker](rules/conventions/citation-markers.md) and [prior-work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md) rules; otherwise, flag the missing attribution. An objection that the paper clearly frames as hypothetical and then answers needs no cited objector, but "Some may argue" alone is not such framing.

**[Replace negative parallelism with the positive claim](rules/deslopping/negative-parallelism.md).** When a contrast rejects an alternative invented for emphasis, as in "not only faster but also more accurate", "It is not just an optimizer, it is a paradigm shift", or paired sentences ("The headline is not the speedup. The real story is ..."), state the supported result or measurement directly; a contrast that excludes a real alternative ("we measure X, not Y") is not this pattern.

**[Make both sides of a false concession specific](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md).** In a concession such as "While X has advanced, Y remains a challenge", replace each vague side with the draft's specific finding, and flag any side the draft leaves unspecified. Keep concessions whose sides are both specific ("the gain holds on the two smaller benchmarks but not the largest").

**[Revise rule-of-three lists written for rhythm](rules/deslopping/rule-of-three.md).** In a list such as "faster, cheaper, and more robust", remove empty or redundant words and flag unsupported items, but never drop a substantive item just to change the count.

**[Replace false ranges with the settings tested](rules/deslopping/false-ranges.md).** When the endpoints of "from X to Y" lie on no ordered scale ("tasks ranging from image classification to reinforcement learning", unlike "from 10² to 10⁶ parameters"), replace the phrase with the settings tested. Never shorten a longer list to its endpoints.

**[Delete transition chains that announce more text](rules/deslopping/transition-chains.md).** Delete repeated openings such as "Moreover", "Furthermore", and "Additionally", letting the paragraph order convey the sequence, and never replace them with unsupported causal, temporal, or contrastive links. Keep transitions that show a [valid logical relation](rules/prose/transitions.md).

**[Replace needless substitutes for "is", "has", or action verbs](rules/deslopping/copula-avoidance.md).** Replace phrases such as "serves as", "acts as", "represents", or "boasts" with "is" or "has" when the meaning stays the same, and a following action noun with its verb ("serves as a temperature monitor" becomes "monitors temperature"). Keep these verbs when they express a role distinct from identity (a compound acting as a catalyst) or a precise technical meaning.

**[Delete or replace empty commentary in trailing "-ing" phrases](rules/deslopping/trailing-ing.md).** Delete trailing "-ing" phrases, such as "highlighting its importance", that add no checkable claim, or replace them with specific claims the draft supports.

**[Delete sentences that only label results important or surprising](rules/deslopping/self-labeling.md).** Delete a sentence such as "This is the key result"; if the draft explains why the result matters, organize the passage around that reason, and otherwise add no explanation. Keep a word of authorial stance that introduces a claim ("Interestingly,").

**[Replace rhetorical questions used as transitions with their answers](rules/deslopping/rhetorical-questions.md).** Delete a question that only introduces the next topic ("But how well does this scale?"), even if the next sentence answers it, and make its answer the paragraph's first sentence. Keep a research question that states what the text tests ("RQ1: ..."), and keep a mid-paragraph question only if it turns preceding details into a puzzle that the next sentence answers. Rewrite a question that assumes readers agree with a claim ("Does this not prove ...?") as a statement of that claim, choosing its verb from the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md).

**[Vary sentence structure to show how ideas connect](rules/deslopping/repetitive-syntax.md).** Look for consecutive sentences or paragraphs that share an opening, clause pattern, or length, as when every sentence is 15 to 25 words long and every paragraph has the same structure. Vary them by letting relations the draft states (a shared object, condition, time, or consequence) decide how sentences join and are structured, using the [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md), not by random length changes. Preserve [parallel wording for parallel ideas](rules/prose/parallel-form.md), and keep sentences complete and literal, without [writerly asides](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md).

**[State what a result implies without exaggerating its importance](rules/deslopping/significance-inflation.md).** Delete grand-trend phrases such as "marking a pivotal moment" or "paving the way", or replace them with the supported implication and its conditions. Keep and flag an implication that lacks evidence.

**[Replace slogan-like analogies with literal explanations](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md).** Replace an analogy such as "X is the Y of Z" ("attention is the currency of deep learning") only with a mechanism the draft states. Delete a slogan that makes no substantive claim, but keep an analogy that asserts an unexplained mechanism, and flag it.

**[Make reported failures as noticeable as successes](rules/deslopping/relentless-positivity.md).** When a draft presents every result as a success, give its failures and counterevidence comparable prominence and [match each claim to its evidence](rules/claims/scope.md). Never invent a failure, or add a caveat or hedge for balance.
