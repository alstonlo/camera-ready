# Writing guide

This guide is generated from a collection of writing rules. Each rule has its own page in the folder `rules/` next to this guide, and every page has the following format:

```markdown
# {{ Name of the rule }}

{{ A summary of the rule: what to look for, the edit or flag it calls for, and the exceptions that change the edit. }}

## Details

{{ Optional explanation, further cases, and procedures, omitted from the summary for concision. }}

## Examples

{{ Optional context that the example needs. }}

- Bad: {{ A passage that breaks the rule. }}
- Good: {{ The same passage, corrected under this rule alone. }}
```

The guide below reproduces each rule's heading and summary. Every rule page has at least one example, and some pages also have Details. Read a rule page fully when the rule applies to the text being edited and its summary does not settle the edit, or when the summary names a case that its page covers. An example shows one edit under its rule; it does not widen what the request lets you change.

## Table of contents

The guide has six sections of rules:

- [Story](#story): the argument from problem to implication, the title and headings, section order and openings, contributions, related work, results organized by questions, and numbers that support claims.
- [Claims](#claims): match each claim to its evidence by checking the reporting verb, scope, over- and underclaiming, consistency across the text, causal language, what an efficiency claim counts, significance, hedges, speculation, and limitations.
- [Cutting](#cutting): what may be deleted or relocated without the author's request, the triage order under a length limit, what to protect, and how to repair the text around a cut.
- [Prose](#prose): ordering, paragraphs, information flow, sentence structure, content in place of promises, concrete quantities, and plain wording.
- [Conventions](#conventions): voice, tense, terminology, mathematics, citations and credit, figures and tables, typography, and LaTeX source.
- [Deslopping](#deslopping): the patterns of AI-written prose, in two tiers.

For a request narrower than a polish, apply the sections the request names and every rule linked from them. Check each chunk against every rule in each applicable section. If two rules prescribe different edits for one sentence and one links to the other, follow the linked rule. Otherwise choose the clearer sentence; if both are equally clear, use your judgment as an expert editor.

## Story

Organize the text around one argument: a problem the reader cares about, a gap with a cost, an idea that closes the gap, evidence that the idea works, and the implication. Check this argument across the whole text and its sections before editing sentences, because sentence edits cannot repair a broken argument. Use the [rules on claims](#claims) to set each statement's strength, the [rules on cutting](#cutting) to decide what stays, and the [rules on prose](#prose) to edit paragraphs and sentences.

Without a separate request from the author, you may:

- Reorder sentences within a paragraph or paragraphs within a section to put the claim first.
- Rewrite a section's opening using content already in that section.
- Remove numbers from running text when a table already contains them.
- Rewrite a caption to state a conclusion already drawn in the body text.

Flag changes to the text's claims, section order, or list of contributions instead of making them. Never supply a cost, explanation, or number that the text lacks.

### The whole argument

**[Summarize the argument in five lines](rules/story/five-line-summary.md).** Before editing, use the text itself to write one line each for the problem, the gap and its cost, the idea, the evidence, and the implication. Condense the idea and evidence lines into the core claims for the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md). Flag any line you cannot write from the text.

**[Summarize the argument in one sentence](rules/story/one-sentence-summary.md).** Combine the five lines into one sentence: "The problem matters; existing work leaves this gap, so we propose this idea, supported by this result." If the sentence identifies no gap, flag the missing problem. If it requires unrelated problems, flag the competing focuses; do not choose one or invent a claim that unifies them.

**[Map argument stages to section functions](rules/story/section-stages.md).** Check section functions: the introduction states the problem, gap, idea, and contributions; background supplies only the context that the gap and comparison need; methods explain the idea; results supply evidence; discussion interprets evidence, limits, and alternatives; the conclusion gives implications. Keep combined or repeated sections when the argument remains coherent, and follow venue requirements. Check that each result follows the method context needed to understand it; for formats that put methods last, see Details. Make the contributions clear within the first quarter of the text. If a central result first appears after that point, or if the sections should be reordered, flag the problem instead of moving sections. A proposal describes the evidence it plans to gather.

The abstract states all five stages within the venue's word limit, using recognizable terms for the task and technique. It must be understandable without the body or bibliography; remove a pointer to a body section or figure only when the abstract already states that result. Flag a missing stage or result, or necessary content that cannot fit the limit; for a citation the abstract needs, see Details.

**[Keep the argument linear](rules/story/linear-argument.md).** Order material by the logic of the argument, not by the chronology or effort of the research. Across sections, avoid returning to a topic after another topic (A, B, A); this extends the [rule against alternation](rules/prose/topic-order.md) beyond a single section. The core idea may recur in every section. Preserve repeated method and result stages when later reasoning depends on an earlier result; see [section functions](rules/story/section-stages.md). Also keep reminders of definitions the reader needs and contribution restatements that the triage order protects. Give components and counterarguments space in proportion to their role in the core claims. For how much to explain each side of a comparison, follow the [rule on comparing with prior work](rules/story/prior-work.md). Under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md), a rejected approach stays in the main text only as a negative result; state its flaw before its details. Flag repairs that move material between sections or that reorder sections.

**[Test the argument by skimming](rules/story/skim-test.md).** Check that a reader can identify all five stages of the argument from only the abstract, headings, first paragraph of each section, and [captions](rules/conventions/figures-tables.md). Restore missing connections using content from the draft, within the permitted scope; flag absent content or repairs that require changing claims or section order.

**[Flag titles that misrepresent or obscure the contribution](rules/story/titles.md).** Flag a title that hides the subject, has two plausible readings, or overstates the contribution. Suggest a revision that keeps the body's claim strength and conditions, but leave the title itself unchanged. Under the [acronym rule](rules/conventions/acronyms.md), flag an acronym in the title that readers outside the subfield would not know. Follow venue requirements.

### Problem, gap, and contribution

**[Open with the problem](rules/story/open-with-problem.md).** Name the paper's actual problem in its opening. If readers need context first, apply the [brief-setup rule](rules/prose/paragraphs.md). Check that the opening predicts the problem and scope that the paper develops. Delete a [generic opening](rules/deslopping/generic-openings.md); naming the topic alone does not establish a problem. Flag missing motivation rather than inventing an opening example.

**[State the gap's cost](rules/story/gap-cost.md).** State the specific gap and its cost: what readers lose, pay, cannot do, or cannot understand because of it. "No prior work studies X" states only the gap, not its cost. Replace "little is known" with the specific unknown when the draft supplies it. Flag a missing gap or cost. If stating the gap would require rewriting the objectives or contributions, flag the proposed rewrite instead of making it. The cost may be a question readers cannot answer or the value of extending earlier work to a new case; it need not be a flaw in prior work or a hypothesis, so do not invent either. Distinguish established consequences from [speculative applications](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Explain why the problem is hard](rules/story/why-hard.md).** State why the obvious approach fails. Without that explanation, the idea seems arbitrary, and the reader cannot tell whether the gap remains because it is hard to close or because it is unimportant. Flag a missing explanation.

**[State contributions as claims with their support](rules/story/contribution-claims.md).** For each contribution, state a checkable claim and name the section that supports it. Turn a contribution that names only an activity into a claim under the [underclaiming rule](rules/claims/underclaiming.md), at the strength the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) allows. Flag contributions without evidence. Treat a major result that supports no contribution as a candidate for the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md); do not delete it or change the list of contributions. When each contribution names its supporting section, delete a roadmap that names no content, under the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md).

### Section openings and connections

**[Open every section with its question and answer](rules/story/section-openings.md).** In each section's first paragraph, state the section's question and finding using content already in the section. An opening that merely announces the topic is a [signpost](rules/prose/signposts.md).

**[Connect each section to the preceding text](rules/story/section-connections.md).** Connect each section's question to content the text has established, following [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). Make the introduction understandable without the abstract. Keep short reminders for readers who start reading at Results, Discussion, or Conclusion, following [paragraph opening order](rules/prose/paragraphs.md). Open a methods section with the key idea before the steps; place an existing [explanatory example](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) before the general case.

**[Make headings reveal the argument's structure](rules/story/headings.md).** Use headings whose subheadings fit under them; give sibling headings (those under one parent) [parallel form](rules/prose/parallel-form.md). Flag any of the following when it obscures the argument: a level with only one subheading, a fourth-level heading, or a very short section. When a heading could state its section's finding, flag a proposed heading that keeps the body's claim strength and conditions; do not rewrite the heading yourself. Flag, rather than make, changes to the hierarchy, such as merging sections or removing a level; preserve distinctions that help readers, and follow venue requirements.

### Related work

**[Compare prior work fairly on named dimensions](rules/story/prior-work.md).** End each paragraph or group of paragraphs about prior work with a checkable difference in an assumption, metric, cost, or guarantee. Describe each source only as the draft does; flag missing context or a missing difference rather than supplying it. Explain both sides enough to judge the comparison, stating each source's contribution and assumptions before criticizing it. Do not write "ignores" or "fails to" for a difference in scope unless the draft shows failure. Preserve [credit](rules/cutting/protect.md) and a source's "suggests" rather than turning it into "shows". Keep each position attributed to whoever holds it, including concessions and reported objections; a source's claim must not become the paper's assertion. For survey synthesis, see Details. A comparison that is vague on both sides is the [false concession](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md) pattern.

**[Distinguish new contributions from borrowed components](rules/story/novelty.md).** Distinguish the present contribution from standard components, borrowed ideas, and the authors' earlier results. When the text extends a preliminary publication, identify the stated additions. Preserve wording that identifies the authors' earlier work unless the venue requires anonymity; in that case, flag the wording. Do not make the authors' earlier work sound like independent confirmation. If the draft leaves the contribution or attribution unclear, flag it rather than assigning credit.

**[Flag related work the reader cannot yet understand](rules/story/related-work-placement.md).** Related work belongs where the reader can understand the comparison, usually after the idea. Leave the related-work section where it is, and flag it only when it comes before the method and cannot be understood without it. Follow the [rule on comparing with prior work](rules/story/prior-work.md) for its content.

### Results

**[Organize results by questions, not by experiments](rules/story/results-by-question.md).** State the questions that the Results section answers and connect each to a contribution. For a heading that names only a dataset or setup, flag a proposed heading that states a question or finding and keeps the body's claim strength and conditions. Preserve a sequence in which one result motivates the next experiment, and keep the interpretation of that result next to it; distinguish [measurement from inference](rules/claims/hedges.md). Flag experiments with no identifiable question as candidates for cutting, and flag proposed section moves or reordering. Flag a result with no described procedure or an evaluation procedure with no reported use; delete neither.

**[Answer one question per results paragraph](rules/story/one-question-per-paragraph.md).** Give each results paragraph one question and its answer, using the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraphs.md) for where a question and answer belong. Put the evidence after the answer. Split a paragraph that answers two independent questions, and flag one that answers none.

**[Explain why the evidence supports the claim](rules/story/explain-evidence.md).** When readers outside the subfield may not see why a measurement or reason supports a claim, explain the connecting inference and check that its assumptions hold in this study. Use only the support that the draft gives, or flag the missing connection; do not supply a plausible reason that the draft does not state. Apply the rule on [scope](rules/claims/scope.md) to claims that exceed what the measurement establishes, and the rule on [formal support](rules/claims/formal-informal.md) to inferences from a theorem.

**[Identify what produces the gain](rules/story/source-of-gain.md).** For a method with several parts, the results must show which part produces the improvement. If an ablation or equivalent evidence is missing, flag the missing evidence and credit only the combination under the [rule on causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md). Treat an untested explanation as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md). Flag any obvious alternative explanation that the text does not address. When the draft raises a counterexample, conflicting result, or rival explanation, flag the draft's answer to it if that answer gives neither evidence nor a reason the objection does not apply; restating the claim is not an answer.

### Numbers

The most common failure in the argument of AI-generated text is to obscure the point with results. Correct the order so that every number supports a claim stated first in words.

**[State the claim before the number](rules/story/claim-before-number.md).** State the finding in words before the number that supports it, then refer to the table for the remaining values. Identify the claim that each number supports. Flag a number that supports no identifiable claim; move it only when the request permits relocation and a suitable destination exists. Remove a number that a table already contains only under the [headline-number rule](rules/story/headline-number.md), preserving needed comparisons and uncertainty.

**[Choose a headline number for each claim](rules/story/headline-number.md).** Choose a headline number for each claim and describe the pattern that the results support, including where it fails. Retain the baseline and any interval or range needed to interpret that number. Refer to an existing table for the remaining values; remove numbers that duplicate the table only when the text preserves the comparison, [uncertainty](rules/claims/uncertainty.md), and [statistical conclusion](rules/claims/significance.md). Do not select an unrepresentative best case.

**[Match precision to the claim](rules/story/precision.md).** Match reported precision to the measurement and comparison, not to the number of digits software prints. Preserve words marking estimates or approximations. Flag unsupported precision or inconsistent precision among comparable quantities; never add digits, round values, or replace numbers without the author's authorization and the necessary source data.

### Discussion and conclusion

**[Return to the gap](rules/story/return-to-gap.md).** In the first paragraph of the discussion, explain how the results address the gap stated in the introduction. Follow with the limits, under the [rule on limitations](rules/claims/limitations.md). If the introduction promises more than the results establish, flag the mismatch under the [rule on scope](rules/claims/scope.md).

**[Use the conclusion to add perspective](rules/story/conclusion-perspective.md).** Keep one connected restatement of each contribution under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md), then state what the established findings let readers understand or do. If readers could fully understand the conclusion without reading the paper, check whether it repeats generic statements instead of interpreting the findings. Flag such generic repetition, a conclusion that merely repeats the abstract, new supporting evidence, or a change to a subject not introduced earlier; being understandable on its own is not a defect. Future work may identify a concrete question that arises from the findings and what answering it would establish; do not invent a research plan. Preserve the findings' scope and [limitations](rules/claims/limitations.md), and label [speculative implications](rules/claims/speculation.md). When shortening the restatements of contributions, repair the connections between them; do not join copied sentences that rely on context the conclusion omits.

## Claims

Every claim must match its evidence. Both overclaiming and underclaiming are errors. An aspirational goal may motivate the text if the text states that the goal remains unattained.

### Strength and scope

**[Choose the reporting verb from the verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md).** Choose each reporting verb from the verb ladder using the evidence for the claim, regardless of enthusiasm for the result.

| Verb | Required support |
|---|---|
| **prove** | a theorem with stated assumptions, and nothing else |
| **show / demonstrate / establish** | direct evidence that rules out plausible alternatives |
| **find / observe / report** | a directly measured empirical result |
| **suggest / indicate**; **hypothesize / conjecture** | an inference or interpretation that could reasonably be wrong |

A benchmark does not prove a mechanism. A figure or table shows only what it displays; choose an inferred claim's verb by its evidence. Replace *reveal* or *confirm* with a lower-rung verb if the evidence does not establish the stated conclusion. Flag a claimed disproof supported only by an objection or an inconclusive test. When reporting cited work, preserve whether the source presents a result as central or incidental, and whether it accepts or doubts the result. See the page for verb choices and [prior work](rules/story/prior-work.md) for attribution.

A claim hedged in Results keeps its hedge in the abstract, introduction, conclusion, and every other summary. Find each summary claim's supporting result, and flag a summary claim that has none. Add a missing section or table pointer when support exists, except in the abstract, which states the result itself.

**[Match scope and confidence to the evidence](rules/claims/scope.md).** Use the broadest scope and most direct confidence the evidence supports, from a guarantee to one observed case, without [underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md). Limit each claim, especially a headline claim, to the conditions, datasets, and settings its evidence covers. If support is uncertain, leave the claim and flag it. A bare generic plural such as "Transformers fail to retrieve early tokens" can state a tendency, but it still makes a claim about the whole class; when the evidence covers only the tested models, name them ("the four Transformers we test"). Check whether absolutes such as "always" or "unique" extend a claim to untested cases. One in-scope counterexample defeats a universal claim, but need not defeat a tendency.

A generated sample, saliency map, or case study shows that a case occurs, not how often. For a broader claim, the draft must explain how the example was selected, such as at random, as typical, or as the best; flag missing information. Keep the draft's explicit label of an example as hypothetical or constructed; never add such a label by inference. A broad claim from sampled data needs a justified population and an account of how the sample was selected; check a claim of [independent confirmation](rules/claims/uncertainty.md) under its own rule.

Flag a research question or conclusion that, without support, treats a score as the broader property it measures indirectly, or treats a ratio of scores as a proportional change in that property. Do not rewrite the question or add a caveat to fix the mismatch. A claim of superiority needs a relevant baseline; flag missing comparisons. A claim such as "ready for production" needs evidence about costs and operating conditions beyond benchmark gains; flag missing support, but keep applications that the draft explicitly marks as tentative.

For temporal trends, small-sample probabilities, baseline selection, score differences, and wording that assumes an unstated fact ("the solution", "restores accuracy"), see the page.

**[Remove or flag unsupported empirical labels](rules/claims/overclaiming.md).** Remove or flag unsupported empirical labels such as *solves*, *optimal*, *guarantees*, and *first to*; keep them only when the draft's evidence supports the full claim. Reserve *proves* for theorems, as the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) requires. Flag unsupported superlatives and *human-level* or *state-of-the-art* claims that do not name a task, benchmark, and metric. Remove *novel* when it adds only praise. Beyond removing a label, change what the claim asserts only when the draft's evidence settles its [scope](rules/claims/scope.md); otherwise flag it. For claims that a method is empirically unique, see the page.

**[State supported contributions without underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md).** State supported contributions directly, without hedges that the evidence does not call for. Each item in a Contributions list should state a checkable claim and refer to its support where the draft provides it. An item that merely says *study*, *explore*, or *investigate* names an activity. Replace it with the finding in the corresponding section, or flag the missing finding.

**[Reconcile conflicting claims and numerical constraints](rules/claims/consistency.md).** Compare claims about the same object across sections and lists, including one passage saying that the object supports an operation and another saying that it cannot. Check totals, bounds, and trends against constraints the draft establishes. If a difference in setting or definition stated in the draft reconciles the claims, flag both passages with a revision that makes the difference explicit; otherwise flag both passages and the missing explanation. Flag numerical conflicts rather than choosing a value. Also flag an exhaustive either/or claim when the draft names another in-scope option; identify that option.

### Causation and formal support

**[Reserve causal language for causal evidence](rules/claims/causal-language.md).** Use "causes", "leads to", "improves because", or other wording that credits a design choice with an effect only when the evidence isolates that effect, as a suitable ablation can. Describe correlations with "is associated with" or "correlates with". Preserve the direction of cause and effect, and do not present a partial contribution as the sole cause. When several changes are tested only together, credit their combination, not a single component, and do not claim that each component is necessary. Flag unsupported attribution; mark an untested mechanism as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Identify the kind of evidence supporting each claim](rules/claims/formal-informal.md).** When a sentence reads like a theorem but cites no result and states no conditions, clarify its status: link it to the result and its assumptions, restate the empirical or intuitive claim the draft supports, or flag it. When a theorem supports an empirical claim, check its assumptions in that setting and flag an invalid application. An unmet assumption does not make the theorem false. Never remove a condition from a theorem or other formal statement to simplify it; flag a condition that seems unnecessary. Distinguish proof, model calculation, simulation, and system measurement. Flag a claim that transfers a result from a model to real system behavior without supporting evidence; a valid claim about the model needs no deployment study.

**[Specify what an efficiency claim counts](rules/claims/cost-model.md).** For an efficiency claim such as "linear", "optimal", or "asymptotically faster", check that the text states the input-size variable, the resource counted (time, memory, or messages), the case analyzed (worst, average, or amortized), the assumptions, and whether preprocessing and output costs are included. Add a missing fact that the draft states elsewhere; otherwise flag it. A better asymptotic bound alone does not establish a lower running time. Flag suspected analytical errors without changing the mathematics.

### Numerical comparisons

**[Report variability and compare like with like](rules/claims/uncertainty.md).** For each numerical comparison, check that the text states the number of runs, how they are aggregated, what ± means, and the source of variation, either beside the comparison or in a reporting convention; flag missing information. A best run is not comparable with a mean. Flag a comparison, including one with results borrowed from other papers, unless the text establishes that both sides use equivalent tasks, outputs, accuracy requirements, aggregation, and protocols. When the draft reports a speed gain with lower accuracy, state the trade-off. If it does not establish comparable accuracy, flag the missing comparison.

A percentage needs its base, and a reported change must say whether it is a relative percentage change or a difference in percentage points. Edit the wording when the draft settles which; otherwise flag it, and never relabel by guessing. Keep each ranking tied to its metric, and flag selective reporting.

Data used to select or tune a method do not independently test that choice. Flag a claim of independent confirmation that rests on reused data, related datasets, or correlated metrics, unless the draft establishes a separate test. For misleading averages, systematic timing errors, and how convergence error is measured and normalized, see the page.

**[Draw only the conclusions a statistical test supports](rules/claims/significance.md).** Use *significant* only for the result of a named statistical test, reported with its p-value or significance level and, if the draft reports it, its effect size; flag missing information. If it means large or important, replace it with the reported magnitude. Keep available effect estimates and uncertainty even when the test is not significant. A p-value is the probability, under the null hypothesis and test assumptions, of results at least as extreme as observed, not the probability a hypothesis is true. Preserve the tested hypothesis and the test's direction. Non-rejection does not establish equality; significance does not establish practical importance. Whether two intervals overlap settles neither superiority nor its absence; flag superiority inferred from interval overlap alone, without a test. When the draft tests significance on several datasets or metrics, flag a missing explanation of how it corrects for multiple comparisons. For reporting only a significance threshold, and for statistical power, see the page.

### Qualification and limits

**[Hedge interpretations, not measurements or procedures](rules/claims/hedges.md).** Hedge tentative interpretations, including proposed mechanisms; state measurements and performed procedures directly, retaining measurement uncertainty. For a performed procedure, hedge only its rationale. Change a claim's confidence only when the evidence settles it. A summary keeps both the claim's [verb-ladder strength](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) and its hedge. Use at most one hedge per uncertain claim, in a standard form ("may", "likely", "suggests"). When removing a redundant hedge, keep scope conditions and hedges on other uncertain claims. Replace vague softeners such as *somewhat* or *relatively* with conditions or magnitudes the draft supplies; otherwise keep them and flag the gap. Deleting the only hedge strengthens the claim. A qualifier whose deletion changes the conditions or cases a claim covers, such as *usually*, *on average*, or *in most runs*, is a [scope condition](rules/cutting/protect.md); preserve it when removing redundant hedges.

**[Never state speculation as fact](rules/claims/speculation.md).** Do not present a proposed explanation as established when the draft supplies no supporting test or argument. Mark an untested mechanism without other support as conjecture ("We conjecture that"), or place it in a passage explicitly labeled speculative. Flag an explanation that relies on an undefined term or an unstated measurement; unclear writing alone does not make an explanation a conjecture. A claim that one property does not imply another needs a counterexample, supporting result, or reference; retain an explicitly open question as open. Failure under tested conditions does not establish impossibility. Flag unsupported negative conclusions.

**[State limitations precisely and explain their effects](rules/claims/limitations.md).** Describe each limitation precisely and without apology, in full where it first affects a method or inference; retain brief reminders that bound later claims. Next to the limitation, keep the draft's explanation of how it affects the results and which conclusions still hold; flag a missing explanation. Flag unexplained anomalies and unjustified exclusions. Also flag a decoy limitation, which names a minor weakness while hiding a larger one documented in the draft. A future-work statement must identify the current limit. A limitation of an interpretation that the Discussion first raises may first appear in the Discussion. Move a limitation to another section only as the [relocation rules](rules/cutting/idea-level.md) allow; delete duplicate full accounts only when a length request reaches them in the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md).

## Cutting

Use the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md) to determine what the text needs, and use the request to determine what you may delete or relocate.

### Deciding what to cut

**[Delete bloat without asking](rules/cutting/bloat.md).** Delete [chatbot artifacts](rules/deslopping/chatbot-artifacts.md), [generic openings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings.md), and [leaked artifacts](rules/deslopping/leaked-artifacts.md), and repair [em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md), following each linked rule and its exceptions. Delete content announcements and signposts only as the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md) allows. Tighten surviving sentences without changing their meaning. These edits need no separate request; [idea-level cuts](rules/cutting/idea-level.md) do.

**[Cut or relocate ideas only on request](rules/cutting/idea-level.md).** Delete or relocate substantive content only when the request authorizes condensation or imposes a length limit. Otherwise leave the content in place and flag it as a candidate. This restriction covers anything the author would miss, such as an idea, result, caveat, piece of evidence, example, rationale, or necessary transition. Reordering within a section to [build understanding](rules/prose/build-understanding-early.md) is a prose edit; moving content between sections or out of the main text is relocation. A polish or tighten request permits bloat removal, not idea-level cuts.

**[Apply the spine test before cutting sentences](rules/cutting/spine-test.md).** State the text's core claims, which form its spine, in one to three sentences, then give each paragraph's role in one line. Tag each paragraph **central** if it states a core claim, **supporting** if readers need it to understand or assess one, **ancillary** if it is relevant but dispensable to that assessment, or **off-spine** if it is unrelated to those claims. Paragraphs carrying counterevidence or other [protected content](rules/cutting/protect.md) are supporting unless central; never treat them as expendable. If you cannot write a short, specific title summarizing a section, flag its competing points before cutting. Subject to [the request](rules/cutting/idea-level.md), cut or relocate off-spine paragraphs, trim ancillary ones, then work [sentence by sentence](rules/cutting/sentence-by-sentence.md). Keep a cut only if the argument remains at least as clear.

**[Cut in triage order under a length constraint](rules/cutting/triage-order.md).** Under a length constraint, cut in this order, subject to [protected content](rules/cutting/protect.md) and [relocation safeguards](rules/cutting/sentence-by-sentence.md):

1. **Repeated restatement.** Cut points repeated across the introduction, section openings, and conclusion. Keep the most useful instance, usually the one that develops the point. Always keep one statement of each contribution in each of the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, plus [shorthand reminders](rules/conventions/refresh-shorthand.md). Keep a paragraph's closing restatement if readers need the intervening explanation to understand the opening claim. If a restatement copies another passage (a conclusion repeating the abstract word for word), revise it using established results or flag it. Apply the [deletion test](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).
2. **Signposting left after bloat removal.** This includes the introduction's permitted [roadmap](rules/prose/signposts.md). Preserve pointers to evidence and proofs.
3. **Tangential background and related work.** Cut background that does not motivate the question, and cut citation lists unrelated to the gap. Keep the few works and comparisons that define the gap.
4. **Minor results, secondary analyses, and exploratory findings.** Remove from the main text results that advance no central claim. Relocate sensitivity analyses and robustness checks needed for rigor; keep in the main text any failure or counterevidence that changes a central claim. A failed approach stays in the main text only as a stated negative result, not as a history of attempts.
5. **Exhaustive parameters and implementation detail.** Keep the method reproducible in outline, including [explicit procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md), essential steps, and conditions; relocate full sweeps and subsidiary detail.

**[Protect sentences the argument depends on](rules/cutting/protect.md).** A sentence that adds no new information can still be necessary. Preserve the following, relocating only as the [sentence-by-sentence steps](rules/cutting/sentence-by-sentence.md) allow:

- Keep hedges, qualifiers, scope conditions, limitations, supporting evidence, and counterevidence. Cut a repeated statement of a limitation only in the triage order, and only when a full statement remains elsewhere. Removing a caveat changes a [claim's scope](rules/claims/scope.md); after an authorized cut, [recalibrate the claim](rules/cutting/after-the-pass.md) only as the evidence requires.
- Keep the reasons for methodological choices and reproducibility details, including statistical tests, missing-data handling, and multiple-comparison procedures.
- Keep definitions, topic sentences, necessary framing, [high-level summaries](rules/prose/direct-content.md), and [logical transitions](rules/prose/transitions.md). Also keep back-references that later text needs. Preserve [standalone caption content](rules/conventions/figures-tables.md), including "adapted from" credits and permission notices, even when it repeats the body.
- Preserve fair credit and characterization of prior work, acknowledgments, funding statements, disclosures of help received (including help from AI tools), and disclosures of financial or other relationships that could bias the work.

### Making the cut

**[Repair dependencies when cutting or relocating sentences](rules/cutting/sentence-by-sentence.md).** For each sentence within [the request's scope](rules/cutting/idea-level.md):

1. **Check its role.** Does a core claim or the reader need it? Apply "Protect sentences the argument depends on" before considering deletion.
2. **Check dependencies.** Look for a later "this" or "as shown above", a term defined or citation supplied only here, a claim whose hedge or evidence is here, or the paragraph's topic string. Repair each dependency in the same edit or keep the sentence.
3. **Choose deletion or relocation.** Delete only content that no reader needs. Relocate content that only some readers, or a skeptical reviewer, need; never delete reproducibility content. Use an appendix or supplement only if it already exists; otherwise keep the content and flag what the destination would need to contain. Make relocated material understandable on its own, point to it from the main text where it is relevant, and keep enough method and rationale in the main text to judge the results. You may create footnotes for subsidiary comments if venue requirements allow footnotes. Keep essential algorithm steps in the main explanation, along with definitions used later. For parenthetical asides, see the page.
4. **Read across the gap.** Check that the sentence after the gap opens with a referent or result that the remaining text supplies. Restore any lost link using content from the draft, or keep the original framing sentence.

**[Revise cuts when stop rules apply](rules/cutting/stop-rules.md).** Revise a cut if it leaves a paragraph underdeveloped or breaks its [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md): merge the paragraph with an adjacent one that makes the same point, or restore the missing development and connections. Before cutting a confusing passage, check for a missing step; confusion usually signals an omission, not excess. Supply a missing step or [referent](rules/prose/referents.md) only from elsewhere in the draft; otherwise flag it. If the reader outside the subfield cannot follow the shortened version, restore the needed framing and steps rather than cutting further. These conditions override the length target. Do not meet the target by adding negative `\vspace`, reducing font sizes, or narrowing figures; report the remaining excess instead.

**[Recheck claims, the argument, and references after cutting](rules/cutting/after-the-pass.md).** Confirm that each surviving claim keeps its evidence, scope, and hedges, using the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md). Add a precise reference to relocated support; relocation alone is no reason to weaken a claim. Weaken a claim only if the surviving evidence supports less than before. Reread the problem, gap, approach, findings, and implications, and restore lost logical steps so that readers need not reconstruct them. Repair dangling references, uncited figures or tables, references to nonexistent appendix sections, and terms used before their definitions.

## Prose

**[Build understanding early](rules/prose/build-understanding-early.md).** Give the main idea before its mechanism, and give a finding and its interpretation before the evidence for them. State a section's claim in its first paragraph, and open each paragraph as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraphs.md) directs. Define each unfamiliar term before its first use. Test the order by covering everything after a sentence. If a covered sentence explains something that the visible text needs, move it earlier. Reorder sentences within a paragraph and paragraphs within a section; flag moves between sections. Within a sentence, follow [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md) and [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).

**[Apply the deletion test without changing meaning](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).** Delete a word or clause only when it adds nothing and the sentence remains as easy to understand. For every deletion, replacement, or restructuring, check that the revision keeps the original's referents, actors, actions, definitions, logical relations, modality (whether something is possible, required, or actual), scope, and authorial stance. Preserve distinctions even when the simpler version conveys the gist. Keep referents and [structural words](rules/prose/first-reading.md) the reader needs. Apply [claim rules](rules/claims/scope.md) to substantive changes. When equivalence is uncertain, keep the original and flag the ambiguity. Brevity alone does not justify an edit.

### Paragraphs

**[Develop one point per paragraph](rules/prose/paragraphs.md).** Develop one point per paragraph, and state it as a specific claim in the paragraph's first sentence. "Results were mixed" and "We analyze robustness" [announce](rules/prose/signposts.md) a topic; "Accuracy falls after pruning" states a claim. Allow brief setup only when the claim cannot be understood first: one sentence of necessary context from the draft, immediately followed by the claim. A results paragraph may open with the specific question it tests if the next sentence answers it. Put the evidence after the answer. A procedural or descriptive paragraph may begin with its first fact or step. Keep both sides of a comparison in one paragraph when they support one point. Split a paragraph where its point changes, and merge short paragraphs only when they develop the same point. Judge a paragraph by development and coherence, not length.

**[Use lists for genuine enumeration](rules/prose/list-structure.md).** Use a list for items readers must distinguish or follow separately. Turn bullets that make an argument into connected prose when the bullets hide the reasons or the links between items. Remove numbering that implies an unsupported order or ranking; keep numbers the text uses to refer to items. Flag any proposed change to a list of contributions or objectives, such as regrouping, renumbering, or converting it to prose, instead of making it.

**[Use transitions that express a valid logical relation](rules/prose/transitions.md).** Use a connective, such as "therefore" or "however", only for the relation the draft supports. When "as", "since", or "while" could mean time, cause, or contrast, reword it so that one reading remains; "conversely" needs an actual reversal. Remove a redundant connective when the sentence already expresses that relation, but keep a link the reader needs. If "therefore" introduces a conclusion the preceding premises do not support, flag the missing inference. Causal wording must satisfy the [causal-evidence rule](rules/claims/causal-language.md).

**[Finish one topic before starting another](rules/prose/topic-order.md).** Finish one logical topic before switching to another, and repair any unexplained return to an earlier topic. In a comparison organized by named dimensions, each dimension can be the topic: discuss both methods on one dimension before moving to the next. Preserve that structure rather than regrouping the text into one block per method. Reorder sentences and paragraphs within a section when needed; flag changes to section order.

**[Read the topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md).** When each sentence is clear but the paragraph does not cohere, list the opening words of each sentence; this list is the topic string. Check that the openings develop the paragraph's point. Repeating words between sentences can connect them without developing that point. If most openings introduce new material, rewrite so that each sentence opens with [old information](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). Then identify the relation between each pair of adjacent sentences, such as contrast, consequence, or example. Restore a missing link from elsewhere in the draft or flag the gap; a connective cannot replace missing reasoning. Consecutive sentences may keep the same topic. A new term at the end of a sentence need not recur unless the text promises to develop it. If adjacent paragraphs could swap places without other edits, add a [summary naming their parallel items](rules/prose/direct-content.md) when they present parallel items; otherwise, state how the second depends on the first.

### Information flow within a sentence

**[Put old information before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md).** Open each sentence with something the preceding text has given the reader: a term it has used, or a part, property, or alternative the reader can infer from it. A concept is not old information merely because the field knows it. When several elements are old, open with the one the new sentence builds on; if they are equally relevant, choose the most recently mentioned one. When changing voice or clause order, keep what the paragraph is about as the main subject, and preserve a useful passive as [alternative sentence orders](rules/prose/other-sentence-orders.md) allows.

**[Put emphasized information in the stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).** The stress position is the end of a sentence or independent clause, where it becomes grammatically complete. Put important new information there. When a trailing qualifier such as "in these tests" buries the point, move the qualifier to the front of the sentence rather than deleting it. If several important items compete for one ending, split the sentence so each receives emphasis. Check whether a paragraph's first sentence ends on the topic that the paragraph goes on to develop. When joining or subordinating clauses, preserve the relative weight of findings and qualifications. Move an essential qualification out of parentheses when the parentheses make it look optional. Keep each scope condition attached to its claim, and follow [pause punctuation](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) when using colons or semicolons.

**[Keep old information first in passives, fronting, and inversions](rules/prose/other-sentence-orders.md).** In a passive with a by-phrase, keep the subject at least as familiar as the agent named in the by-phrase. A fronted phrase (one placed before the subject) or an inversion ("Closest to our work is X") must begin with information the passage has established; otherwise use ordinary subject-verb-object order. A sentence in ordinary order may deliberately open with new information when clearer. Preserve a passive, a "there is" opening, or an "it ... that" construction when it introduces new material without making that material an unfamiliar subject.

### Sentence structure

**[Put actors in subjects and actions in verbs](rules/prose/character-action.md).** A nominalization is an action expressed as a noun. When a phrase combines a verb with little meaning and a nominalization ("perform an evaluation of", "lead to an improvement in"), or several nominalizations occur in one clause, put the actor in the subject and the action in the verb. Keep a nominalization that names a technique ("quantization") or a concept the text keeps discussing, that refers back to the previous sentence ("This reduction in memory ..."), that replaces an awkward "the fact that" clause, or that appears alone in an otherwise clear sentence. Preserve every action. If the draft never says who does what, flag it. With an inanimate subject, replace a verb that requires intention, thought, perception, feeling, memory, or eating ("hopes", "forgets", "swallows") with a verb that names the literal event. Keep established technical usage, such as a model learning or a function expecting input, and keep a component as the subject of the operation it performs. A result or figure may be the subject of a [reporting verb](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) ("the results suggest"). Follow [voice](rules/conventions/voice.md) and [information flow](rules/prose/other-sentence-orders.md).

**[Make every sentence clear on the first reading](rules/prose/first-reading.md).** Repair what forces rereading; length alone is not a defect. Bring subjects next to verbs and verbs next to complements; shorten an overloaded introduction. Move a long subject clause or interruption later unless it carries old information; keep short familiar asides. Unpack stacks of three or more nouns and long chains of modifiers, counting an established technical compound as one unit. Clarify which noun a trailing relative clause describes and what each participle modifies. Read left to right for false starts, including coordination that first seems to join the wrong items. Keep or restore articles and clause markers such as "that" after "assume", "show", or "find" when their absence permits a misreading; do not add optional words to short predictable clauses, or "that" after "we have". Put "only" beside what it restricts. Place conditions before a claim unless a short trailing condition is unambiguous. Give long conditions their own sentence, and enumerate three or more steps or conditions packed into one sentence; preserve their scope when splitting. For omitted markers or unclear modifier attachment, use the page's diagnostics. If grammatical prose still compresses too many concepts, unpack the explanation under [definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).

**[Express parallel ideas in parallel form without changing meaning](rules/prose/parallel-form.md).** Give ideas with the same logical role the same grammatical form, but preserve meaningful differences and qualifications. Do not rename unlike objects or delete a condition to create symmetry. Check which coordinated items share each modifier and predicate; when that scope is unclear, repeat words or split the sentence. Compare things of the same kind, and restore an omitted metric or predicate when needed to identify both sides of a comparison. When joining words, phrases, or clauses as equals hides a causal or qualifying relation, state the relation the draft supports. Keep clear idiomatic variation. Follow [list structure](rules/prose/list-structure.md) when choosing layout.

**[Do not open a sentence with "And", "But", "So", or "Plus"](rules/prose/sentence-initial-conjunctions.md).** Do not begin a sentence with "And", "But", "So", or "Plus". Join it to the preceding sentence or replace the opening, keeping its relation. Keep a contrast as a contrast, and mark a consequence only when the draft supports it. For the final item of a series, use "Finally", not "In addition", "Moreover", or "Furthermore", which mark further support. For a cited author's objection, follow [prior-work attribution](rules/story/prior-work.md). Keep clear coordination within a sentence.

**[Read the sentence aloud](rules/prose/read-aloud.md).** Read the sentence aloud, or simulate doing so, to locate hesitation, unclear stress, or missing links; then repair the structural cause. A smooth delivery does not prove clarity: the words and syntax must supply the intended reading without imagined pauses, emphasis, or prior knowledge. A punctuation change alone may not repair the structure. Repair the wording itself with literal statements; do not add a [writerly aside](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md) that tells readers how to read the sentence.

### Content instead of promises

**[State the content directly](rules/prose/direct-content.md).** Replace promises of deferred content and generic apologies about complexity with the content the draft supplies; flag missing content. Delete a trailing promise such as "and we discuss each below" when the content is already present. Keep a summary sentence that states the high-level idea, and keep a count whose items follow immediately ("We make four observations. First, ..."). Keep sentences that state a real dispute, an unresolved definition, or a [limitation](rules/claims/limitations.md); delete only a generic warning about complexity, never a statement of real uncertainty. To test a sentence, cover the rest of the paragraph and ask whether the sentence alone says anything readers can understand or check.

**[State the finding instead of a reporting policy](rules/prose/policy-for-finding.md).** When a sentence such as "we report both metrics" says how findings will be reported instead of stating them, state the conclusions, measurements, and outcomes that the sentence refers to, using the draft's results. Flag missing results. Keep an actual reporting or analysis procedure where it describes what was done rather than standing in for a result.

**[Combine signposts with the content they introduce](rules/prose/signposts.md).** Replace sentences about the text, such as "The previous section analyzed the encoder. This section presents the decoder.", with the content they announce and, if needed, a reference to its location. Keep references that locate evidence or a proof, and keep a roadmap of at most two sentences at the end of an introduction or chapter opening. Delete three kinds of signpost: announcements that name no content ("Section 7 concludes"), roadmaps within a section that repeat its subheadings, and signposts that take more effort to understand than they save. Preserve [genuine transitions and summaries](rules/cutting/protect.md). For a retained roadmap, check its count, order, labels, and distinctions against the actual sections or items. Repair a mismatch that the draft resolves unambiguously; flag conflicting categories and any repair that would change the section order or the contributions.

**[State the reasons for a choice](rules/prose/reasons.md).** Unless the omitted items are obvious, replace "for various reasons", or an "etc." that ends a list, with the reasons or items the draft supplies; if the draft omits them, flag the gap. When an unexplained method choice is central to a stated claim, give the rationale the draft states, or flag the missing rationale. Do not request justifications for routine choices whose role is clear. A stated reason for a choice is not evidence that the choice caused a result; claims of cause follow [causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md).

### Concrete quantities and operations

**[Replace empty verbs with the missing change or operation](rules/prose/empty-verbs.md).** When "affects", "varies", or "has implications for" leaves a reported change unspecified, state its direction and magnitude. When "provides", "enables", or "allows" gives only an outcome, name the operation that achieves it. When the draft lacks the operation or measurement, flag it. Keep wording that already explains the change or capability. Do not demand a measured magnitude for an explicitly untested hypothesis.

**[Name the measured quantity instead of an umbrella noun](rules/prose/umbrella-nouns.md).** When a measurement is hidden behind "performance" or "quality", name the quantity, such as accuracy, latency, or memory use. Keep a defined collective label when it accurately covers several named quantities.

**[Quantify comparatives](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md).** Replace a vague comparative with the measurement the draft reports, and name what it is compared with. Lead with the [headline number](rules/story/headline-number.md), describe the pattern, and refer to the table for other values. A label such as "large" needs a stated comparison or threshold; it cannot replace the measurement. Do not calculate an unreported speedup merely to make the prose more concrete.

**[Cut intensifiers](rules/prose/intensifiers.md).** Cut empty intensifiers such as "very" and "extremely"; use a [measurement](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md) when supplied. Treat "essentially" and "largely" as [softeners](rules/claims/hedges.md), preserving their scope or uncertainty unless the draft supplies a precise replacement. Delete "Note that" and similar prompts unless they prevent a likely misreading. Keep "obviously", "clearly", or "trivially" only for a proof step that is routine for the reader outside the subfield. Remove them from empirical claims such as "clearly outperforms", and flag a nonroutine step whose explanation is missing.

### Plain wording

**[Say what literally happens](rules/prose/literal-wording.md).** Name the actual object and operation. Replace inflated "utilize" or "leverage" with "use" when equivalent. Check whether "approach", "framework", "strategy", or "issue" hides a more specific meaning; keep a defined term the paper reuses as shorthand. Name an object before labeling its role. Expand figurative verbs, nouns used as verbs for one occasion ("we changelog the fix"), and unusual word pairs when readers must decode them, following [parts of speech](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md). Preserve established technical terms, verbs, and word combinations; when explaining an unfamiliar term, keep the population, metric, and denominator it covers. Replace an opaque phrasal verb only with an exact equivalent. Keep an existing analogy whose correspondence the draft makes clear; replace decorative or misleading imagery with literal wording, without adding an analogy. The [aphorism rule](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md) governs slogan formulas. For an ambiguous preposition, or a "replace" or "substitute" phrase that leaves unclear which item takes the other's place, use the page's diagnostics.

**[Use logical words unambiguously](rules/prose/logical-words.md).** Replace ambiguous "any" with "every" or "at least one" according to the intended meaning; "some" requires only one case and leaves open whether every case qualifies. Use "each" with a singular noun instead of "all" with a plural when the plural could suggest that the objects share one value ("each run uses its own learning rate"). Rewrite a negative statement as a positive one only when the two statements hold in exactly the same cases, including values equal to a threshold. Keep a deliberate denial as a denial. Do not replace a negated property, including a monotonicity property, with its apparent opposite: "not connected" does not mean "every vertex is isolated". When "can" or "should" could mean capability, possibility, or obligation, use the meaning the draft establishes; otherwise flag it. For "can" and "should", also follow [hedges](rules/claims/hedges.md) and [performed actions](rules/conventions/actions.md). Replace an ambiguous slash in prose with the words it stands for, such as "or" or "and", keeping standard notation and abbreviations. Preserve whether a list is complete; "includes" alone does not imply omitted members. Keep "then" after an "if" clause whose conclusion is a formula or technical claim. When "contains" could mean membership or subset inclusion, say which. For nested conditions, negation scope, or a rhetorical "if" mistaken for a condition, use the page's diagnostics.

**[Spell out definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).** Explain each metric, rule, and procedure the reader relies on, including how to interpret or reproduce its results. A definition must identify the kind of thing named and what distinguishes it; an acronym expansion is not enough. Explain needed formal definitions in plain language. For related concepts, state their shared category and difference. When definitions compete, identify the adopted meaning and its stated justification; neither invent alternatives nor drop those stated. Do not flag standard steps covered by a precise citation, but keep every modification to them and the explanation readers need at that point. When an algorithm's description only restates its steps, state the purpose the draft gives, or flag that the purpose is missing. Check that worked examples match the stated method or definition; flag mismatches rather than changing either. For unclear explanations or examples with no stated purpose, use the page's diagnostics. Use the page's algorithm, numerical, dataset, or human-evaluation checks only during a story or claims check, or when a result depends on a missing setting. Delete or move reproducibility detail only where the [cutting rules](rules/cutting/sentence-by-sentence.md) allow it.

**[Make every referent clear](rules/prose/referents.md).** Make each pronoun, demonstrative, comparative, and shorthand noun identify one referent from the text alone. Name an ambiguous baseline or previous run using the draft's identifiers, then reuse that name. Restore context lost during editing or flag the missing connection. The first sentence under a heading, including a run-in paragraph title such as "**Gradient caching.**", must make sense without that heading. When adding a noun after an ambiguous "this", follow the [rule on ambiguous "this"](rules/prose/ambiguous-this.md).

**[Add an accurate noun after an ambiguous "this"](rules/prose/ambiguous-this.md).** When "this", "that", or "it" could refer to several nouns or claims, add a concise noun identifying the supported referent. When the draft does not settle the referent, flag it rather than choosing one. The noun must preserve the earlier statement's status and evaluation: a conjecture is not a finding, and a change is not necessarily an improvement. A bare demonstrative may remain in a clear interpretive opening such as "This suggests that" after one unambiguous claim.

**[Replace writerly asides with literal claims and reasons](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md).** Replace conversational idioms such as "Call it a wash" and clipped editorial instructions with complete literal statements of the claim and its reason. Use only reasons the draft supplies; flag a missing explanation rather than adding one. Keep a deliberately short sentence when it is complete and literal. Preserve the author's supported stance under [author voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).

## Conventions

These conventions override generic writing advice.

### Voice and tense

**[Use active voice with a consistent agent](rules/conventions/voice.md).** Default to active "we" for the authors' actions and decisions. Do not use this "we" for the research community or people in general; in derivations and proofs, "we" may also mean the author and reader together. Use one agent throughout a method description, either the method or "we", and always use "we" for design choices. Preserve a single author's consistent "I" or "we". Keep passives when the actor is obvious, irrelevant, or unknown, when they keep [old information first](rules/prose/other-sentence-orders.md), when a long agent belongs last, or when a passive modifier ("the prompts selected by annotators") is easier to read. Do not assign an unidentified action to the authors.

**[State performed actions directly](rules/conventions/actions.md).** When the draft says an action was performed, replace "can", "aims to", or "seeks to" with the direct action. Keep the wording for a capability the work did not use and for a goal the draft says remains unattained: removing that distinction would [change the claim](rules/claims/overclaiming.md).

**[Match tense to the time frame](rules/conventions/tense.md).** Use present tense for the paper's contents ("Section 4 shows", not "will show"), results, method, setup, established facts, and continuing prior work. Preserve a draft's consistent past tense for its experiments; use past tense for events outside the described procedure. Future tense describes uncompleted plans, not later sections. Keep tense stable within a time frame. Prefer simple tense unless perfect tense marks current relevance or an event before another past event. Keep past tense in reported findings ("Lee found that the cache was slower") and hypotheticals ("if the cache were larger"). "No work had studied X before Lee" does not establish whether the gap remains open. Do not turn a research recommendation into the authors' promise. For reported clauses, hypothetical past, and relative dates, see the page.

### Terminology

**[Use one term per concept](rules/conventions/terminology.md).** Use one term per concept; do not alternate "network", "model", "architecture", and "system" for variety. List each recurring concept's names: multiple names are an error unless they denote distinct concepts. Preserve differences in type, scope, or measured quantity. In a parallel or more general case, repeat the earlier wording exactly, then state the difference. A pronoun ("it") or bare demonstrative ("this") may refer back unambiguously within the same or next sentence. Do not refer back with a category noun ("this model", "the network"); repeat the name instead. Check captions and visual labels too; flag inconsistencies that prose edits cannot repair.

**[Use each technical term as one part of speech where possible](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md).** Use each technical term as one part of speech where possible. Words such as "sample", "estimate", and "model" can be nouns or verbs; using both roles in one sentence can force rereading. Replace one occurrence with an ordinary verb or separate the clauses when needed for clarity.

**[Spell out acronyms and coin them sparingly](rules/conventions/acronyms.md).** Spell out an acronym where repeated use begins; use full wording for isolated earlier mentions. Define it separately in the abstract and body, and in each major section where it returns. Prefer full wording unless the acronym is standard or replaces a long, recurring name. Spell out an acronym used only once or twice, one with another common expansion in the field ("LR"), or one that saves no syllables aloud ("BS" for batch size). Flag an unfamiliar title acronym with a spelled-out alternative; leave the title unchanged and define the acronym in the body. Flag opaque coined labels rather than renaming them. If the acronym also appears in tables, figures, or code, flag the change rather than making the prose inconsistent with them.

**[Spell each word one way throughout](rules/conventions/spelling.md).** Use US or UK spelling consistently throughout. When acceptable forms alternate, such as "dataset" and "data set", follow the venue requirements or otherwise use the draft's more frequent form.

**[Refresh long-lived shorthand](rules/conventions/refresh-shorthand.md).** When shorthand returns after a long gap or section boundary, remind the reader of its established name and its definition or the definition's location. Make the wording a reminder rather than a new definition. Replace a generic label such as "the loss" when several losses could fit, using the original name. For a bare display number far from its display, add the draft's name for that display, as in "the admission score (9)".

**[Clarify suggestive, overloaded, and suitcase terms](rules/conventions/suggestive-terms.md).** Clarify suggestive coinages ("thought vector"), overloaded terms ("deconvolution" for transposed convolution), and suitcase words with several field meanings ("generalization"): state which meaning applies. Flag an undefined coinage and suggest a literal alternative. Keep established technical usage and explain its operation at first use; preserve "The network learns features". Replace "the model tries to reason" with the stated operation or author goal ("we train the model to"). Replace an ordinary word that collides with a nearby technical meaning, such as "a heap of requests" in a paper about binary heaps, keeping the technical term. Follow [definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) for unfamiliar categories and competing definitions.

### Mathematics

**[Define each symbol by its first use and give it one meaning](rules/conventions/notation.md).** Define symbols, at least informally, at or before first use and [refresh them after gaps](rules/conventions/refresh-shorthand.md). Keep one meaning per symbol and one symbol per object. Flag conflicting meanings, unused definitions, confusable names, or dense notation; do not rename symbols. Distinguish a function from its value: if L is a loss function, flag "L = 0.31" where "L(θ_T) = 0.31" is intended. Keep local definitions near use. If global definitions are scattered, move them into an existing notation section only when the request [allows relocation](rules/cutting/idea-level.md); otherwise flag them. Never create that section. For unnecessary indices, repeated expressions, and verbal alternatives, see the page.

**[Write mathematics as grammatical prose](rules/conventions/math-prose.md).** Read each formula as a phrase; the surrounding text must still form grammatical sentences. Begin sentences and clauses with words, not bare symbols, and spell out sentence-initial numbers. Separate adjacent formulas with words, including across sentence boundaries. In prose, write quantifiers and relations as words ("for all", not ∀; "is at most 0.1", not "is ≤ 0.1"), except in work on logic. A displayed formula takes a period when ending its sentence and a comma when continuing it; a preceding colon needs a complete clause. A trailing "where" may define symbols, but a definition needed to understand the claim must come before the claim. For LaTeX paragraph breaks, see the page.

**[Explain why formal material is needed](rules/conventions/equation-meaning.md).** Flag a central equation or definition whose purpose the text never states; do not infer a purpose the draft does not give. If the same paragraph or section explains its purpose later, move that explanation before the formal material; if it appears only in another section, flag the move instead. Follow [formal support](rules/claims/formal-informal.md) when the claimed application depends on unstated conditions.

**[Set operators, subscripts, and signs in the correct type](rules/conventions/math-type.md).** Set multi-letter operators and word subscripts upright; variable subscripts stay italic. Use × for multiplication and speedup factors, not x or *, and use a minus sign for negative values. Fix LaTeX typography when the mathematical content stays unchanged. Flag, rather than make, changes to symbols, grouping, delimiters, or operator meaning; preserve code syntax. For ambiguous slash fractions, tiny exponent fractions, oversized delimiters, and LaTeX commands, see the page.

**[Number referenced and central displays](rules/conventions/displays.md).** Number every display referred to in the text and every central display, such as the objective, update rule, or main bound. Leave intermediate steps unnumbered.

**[Make theorem statements self-contained](rules/conventions/theorems.md).** Introduce a theorem, lemma, or algorithm with a complete sentence, using a colon only after a complete clause. Make the statement self-contained: name its objects ("the transition relation R") and define symbols before use. Keep motivation and commentary outside the statement. When the hypotheses run for several lines, flag the statement and propose named conditions defined before it ("Assumption 1 (smoothness)").

**[Open each proof with its strategy and mark its progress](rules/conventions/proofs.md).** Open each proof with its strategy and mark subgoals as they are completed. Distinguish what is assumed, defined, established, or deferred to a later proof. Supply a strategy or progress description only when the draft makes it identifiable. For "clearly" and "it is easy to see", follow [intensifiers](rules/prose/intensifiers.md). For matching case order and separating a construction from its use, see the page.

### Citations, figures, and tables

**[Keep citation markers out of subject and object positions](rules/conventions/citation-markers.md).** A bracketed or parenthetical citation marker is not a noun. Replace "[8] introduces" with a textual citation (`\citet{key}` in LaTeX), keeping the key. For an established result the paper asserts, shorten "In a recent study, ... showed that X" to "X (citation)"; keep the authors as the subject when the sentence reports, characterizes, or contrasts a claim the paper does not itself assert, following [prior work](rules/story/prior-work.md). When the draft shows which source supports which clause, place each citation beside that clause; otherwise flag a trailing cluster that leaves several claims' sources unclear. Flag a passage where the draft leaves unclear how far a source's material extends. For citation commands, author-name formatting, and attribution across sentences, see the page.

**[Flag missing or unresolvable citations](rules/conventions/source-attribution.md).** Flag a technical claim presented as prior knowledge ("It is well known that") without an identifiable source, or a citation key missing from supplied references. Common knowledge needs no added citation; do not delete an existing citation because it seems unnecessary. Preserve explicit secondhand wording such as "as cited in" rather than implying direct consultation. Follow [vague attribution](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md) for "Studies show" and [reference markers](rules/conventions/float-refs.md) for "??".

**[Write captions that stand alone and state the finding](rules/conventions/figures-tables.md).** A caption that only names its visual is incomplete. Complete it from the paragraph citing the visual; flag facts that appear nowhere in the draft. Identify quantities, units, axes, conditions, visual encodings such as colors and line styles, and [error bars](rules/claims/uncertainty.md); clarify whether higher or lower is better when that is not obvious. State the supported finding and check agreement with the body. Name compared methods or conditions instead of identifying them only by color. Flag unexplained blank or dash cells and mixed or missing column units. When the plot source or caption gives axis ranges, a second vertical axis, or the range of a fitted curve, flag a claim read from apparent distances or slopes that those scales do not support, and flag an extrapolated value called measured; an axis zoomed to part of its range is not by itself an error.

**[Cite each figure and table where its claim is stated](rules/conventions/float-refs.md).** Cite each figure or table in the sentence stating its claim, rather than writing "In Figure 6 we show the results". Point to the panel or comparison that the draft identifies, and use stable numbered references instead of "below". If the claim requires an exact value that a reader could only estimate from a plot, state the draft's value or flag its absence. Flag unresolved citation or reference markers such as "??". For LaTeX float commands and capitalization, see the page.

### Typography

**[Hyphenate compound modifiers before nouns](rules/conventions/hyphens.md).** Hyphenate compound modifiers before nouns ("real-time inference", "three-layer network"). Leave the predicate form unhyphenated ("inference runs in real time"). Preserve an established field term without hyphens when the draft consistently writes it that way ("a large language model").

**[Spell out small counts and use numerals for measurements](rules/conventions/number-forms.md).** Spell out small counts used as adjectives in prose ("two passes"). Keep numerals for measurements, hyperparameters even when small ("10 epochs"), quantities with units, mathematical values, and numbered names. Follow the draft's consistent cutoff for spelling out counts unless venue requirements set one. Write compared magnitudes in the same form ("1.5M and 0.2M", not "1.5M and 200,000"); flag a change requiring rounding or unit conversion.

**[Use "i.e.", "e.g.", and "cf." for their meanings](rules/conventions/latin-abbreviations.md).** Use "i.e." to mean "that is" and "e.g." to mean "for example"; never interchange them. In US style, follow both with a comma. Italicize neither. Use "cf." to mean "compare", not "see".

**[Set off non-restrictive clauses with commas](rules/conventions/clause-commas.md).** Use commas around a clause that adds information rather than identifying which objects are meant. Preserve a restrictive clause's meaning: "which" does not require commas and need not become "that" unless the venue requirements say so. Do not put commas around a symbol that names the preceding noun ("a directed graph G with source s").

**[Use punctuation and type only for their formal functions](rules/conventions/formal-marks.md).** Avoid contractions, exclamation marks, and emoji. Use title-case headings only when the venue requires them. Use italics for definitions, not decorative emphasis. If emphasis identifies what a negation denies, express that focus in words when the intended focus is clear; otherwise retain it and flag the ambiguity. Use quotation marks for quotations, mentioned words, and unendorsed terms. Keep marks on unendorsed terms (the model's "reasoning"); if the draft never says what the term refers to, flag that. Do not add scare quotes to ordinary words. Keep sentence punctuation outside exact literals, such as commands and outputs, unless it belongs to them; preserve literal characters.

**[Give units, separated from numbers by a non-breaking space](rules/conventions/units.md).** Give units for measurements and axes, with a non-breaking space between number and unit. If "Mb" could mean bits or bytes, expand the unit from the draft or flag the ambiguity. For ambiguous binary or decimal prefixes and time representations, see the page; never normalize them by changing a value.

**[Preserve LaTeX boundaries when reflowing prose](rules/conventions/latex-boundaries.md).** When reflowing LaTeX source, compare it with the original: keep paragraph breaks (blank lines) where they were, and keep each % comment hiding exactly the text it hid before. Joining a prose line onto the end of a comment line hides that prose; breaking a comment line can expose the rest of the comment as text. Undo boundary changes introduced by reflow.

## Deslopping

Classify recognizable patterns of AI-written prose in two tiers.

1. **Blacklisted patterns.** Delete every instance, regardless of the threshold for tolerable patterns below.
2. **Tolerable patterns.** All other patterns are tolerable. Edit an instance when doing so improves the sentence itself. Rewrite a whole passage only when it contains three or more instances of tolerable patterns.

**[Preserve the author's voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).** Keep the draft's [varied rhythm](rules/deslopping/uniform-rhythm.md) and the stance its authors genuinely express. Never add stance, emphasis, sentence fragments, opposing positions, or details that the draft lacks. After a rewrite for style, [compare its meaning with the original](rules/prose/deletion-test.md). Follow the conventions on [voice](rules/conventions/voice.md), [tense](rules/conventions/tense.md), and [terminology](rules/conventions/terminology.md): first-person "we", present tense for the text and prior work, and repeated technical terms are not defects merely because generated prose also uses them. [Match confidence to evidence](rules/claims/scope.md) without adding stance.

### Blacklisted patterns

**[Delete em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md).** Delete every em dash, including `---` in LaTeX, and every en dash used as a pause. Replace one with a colon or semicolon only if the [pause-punctuation test](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) permits it there. Use parentheses only if they do not make an essential qualification look optional, following [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md). Otherwise rewrite the sentence without the pause while preserving its emphasis. Keep compound hyphens and en dashes in numeric ranges or two-person names ("Sections 3–5", "Kullback–Leibler"). For `--` source forms, see the page.

**[Delete chatbot artifacts](rules/deslopping/chatbot-artifacts.md).** Delete conversational wording left by an assistant, such as "Certainly!", "Great question", "I hope this helps", or "As of my last update".

**[Delete generic openings and closings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings.md).** Delete opening or closing filler that could fit any paper in the field ("In the ever-evolving field of deep learning", "The future looks bright"), including apparently factual popularity claims ("There has been much recent interest in X"). Naming a topic does not make a sentence necessary. For prerequisite context, apply the rule on [brief setup](rules/prose/paragraphs.md). If the draft states a specific reason the topic matters, use that reason instead.

**[Remove leaked generation artifacts and flag the gaps](rules/deslopping/leaked-artifacts.md).** Remove leaked chat citation tokens such as `citeturn0search0` and `contentReference[oaicite:...]`, and unfilled placeholders such as "[Insert citation]". Keep the surrounding claim and flag the missing information. Flag the AI-referrer parameter `utm_source=chatgpt.com` for removal rather than changing the URL.

### Tolerable patterns

**[Apply prose rules to ornate words, nominalizations, counts, and lists](rules/deslopping/prose-rule-patterns.md).** Check [ornate wording](rules/prose/literal-wording.md) such as "delve", "underscore", "showcase", and "pivotal", clauses with [several nominalizations](rules/prose/character-action.md), and [counts that defer their items](rules/prose/direct-content.md). For arguments formatted as bullet lists, apply [list structure](rules/prose/list-structure.md). Follow each linked rule's exceptions. Keep a precise technical word, a useful nominalization, or a count whose items follow immediately.

**[Expand compressed constructions that hinder the first reading](rules/deslopping/compressed-constructions.md).** Check [inanimate subjects given human actions ("the model hopes")](rules/prose/character-action.md), [nouns used as verbs and unusual word pairs](rules/prose/literal-wording.md), and [noun stacks, omitted words such as "that" or an article, and long gaps between subject and verb](rules/prose/first-reading.md). Apply those pages' diagnoses and exceptions. Expand only constructions that impede the first reading; preserve clear short forms and established technical terms.

**[Repair pause punctuation between a claim and its evidence](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md).** Look for a vague claim followed by a colon or semicolon and then its evidence ("Retrieval helps: recall rises by 4 points"). Read the first clause alone. If it states no specific fact, connect the claim to its evidence in words ("as shown by ..."), or delete the vague clause if the evidence states its whole claim. Replacing the mark with a period does not fix this. Apply the test to every such colon or semicolon, not only to one that replaced an em dash. When the first clause states a specific fact, keep a semicolon before a related fact or a colon before an explanation or example. Colons introducing lists, displays, quoted definitions, or labels need no factual assertion before them; this exception does not permit vague claims followed by evidence. The colon must not imply an unsupported cause or exhaustive list. Follow [mathematical punctuation](rules/conventions/math-prose.md) for displays. Restore a missing verb in a trailing comma phrase ("adds 3 ms, below the limit" becomes "adds 3 ms, which is below the limit"); keep appositives, clear participial phrases, and parallel phrases sharing an earlier verb.

**[Replace reasoning-chain leakage with conclusions and evidence](rules/deslopping/reasoning-leakage.md).** Replace "Let us think step by step", "Breaking this down", and numbered internal monologues with the conclusion and evidence. Preserve mathematical setup such as "Let X be", proof steps, and substantive reasoning that justifies the result.

**[Describe the work without narrating manuscript revisions](rules/deslopping/diff-anchored-prose.md).** In the paper, replace revision accounts (diff-anchored prose), such as "In this revised version, we clarify" or "As suggested by the reviewer", with the resulting scientific content. Preserve the explanation, evidence, and justification, including material added during review. Revision history belongs in the response letter or change log. Keep a substantive comparison between versions of a method when that comparison is itself part of the study.

**[Name the source of a vague attribution](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md).** For "studies show", "experts believe", or "X has been shown to", name a source only when the draft cites it for that claim, usually in the same or adjacent sentence; otherwise flag the missing attribution. An explicitly hypothetical objection that the paper poses and answers needs no citation merely to identify an objector. "Some may argue" alone does not establish that case. Follow [prior-work rules](rules/story/prior-work.md) for who holds each position.

**[Replace negative parallelism with the positive claim](rules/deslopping/negative-parallelism.md).** Replace "not only … but also" and "It is not just X, it is Y" with the positive claim when the alternative they reject is invented for emphasis. The pattern can span two sentences ("The headline is not the speedup. The real story is ..."). Keep contrasts that exclude a real alternative ("we measure X, not Y"). State the supported result and its measurements directly.

**[Name both sides of a false concession](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md).** When a concession such as "While X has advanced, Y remains a challenge" states no concrete comparison, name both sides specifically, using the comparison that the draft reports. Flag a side the draft leaves unspecified. Keep concessions whose two sides are specific and supported.

**[Revise reflexive rule-of-three triads](rules/deslopping/rule-of-three.md).** Revise a three-part list such as "faster, cheaper, and more robust" when it is assembled for rhythm rather than to state three distinct, supported items. Keep a list whose subject calls for three items. Do not drop a substantive item merely to change the count; remove only empty or redundant wording, and flag unsupported attributes.

**[Replace false ranges with the settings tested](rules/deslopping/false-ranges.md).** Replace "from X to Y" when its endpoints do not lie on an ordered scale. In that case, the phrase suggests breadth without defining a range. Name the tested settings the draft supplies. Keep a range along an ordered scale. Do not reduce a longer stated list to its endpoints.

**[Delete transition chains that announce more text](rules/deslopping/transition-chains.md).** Delete repeated openings such as "Moreover", "Furthermore", and "Additionally" when they merely announce more text. Keep words that express a [valid logical relation](rules/prose/transitions.md). Do not replace an additive sequence with an unsupported causal, temporal, or contrastive connection.

**[Replace copula avoidance with "is", "has", or action verbs](rules/deslopping/copula-avoidance.md).** Replace phrases such as "serves as", "acts as", "represents", or "boasts" with "is" or "has" when the meaning stays the same. If the noun names an action, use its verb ("serves as a feature extractor" becomes "extracts features"). Preserve a role distinct from identity, such as a component acting as a regularizer, and precise technical uses of these verbs.

**[Delete or replace trailing "-ing" pseudo-analysis](rules/deslopping/trailing-ing.md).** Delete trailing participial commentary such as "highlighting its importance" or "reflecting broader trends" when it adds no checkable claim, or replace it with a specific claim the draft supports. Keep substantive participial clauses; use the [first-reading rule](rules/prose/first-reading.md) when their attachment or meaning is ambiguous.

**[Make importance clear without self-labeling significance](rules/deslopping/self-labeling.md).** Delete a sentence that merely labels an earlier result important or surprising ("This is the key result"). When the draft explains its importance, use that explanation to organize the passage; if not, delete the empty label without adding an explanation. Keep a word of genuine authorial stance that introduces a claim ("Interestingly,").

**[Replace rhetorical questions used as transitions with their answers](rules/deslopping/rhetorical-questions.md).** Replace a transition question ("How well does this scale?") with its answer as the topic sentence. A question used only to connect paragraphs is not a permitted opening, even if the next sentence answers it. Keep a research question that states what the text tests; the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraphs.md) governs answer placement. Keep a mid-paragraph question only when it reframes details already given and the next sentence answers it. For a leading question that presumes agreement, apply the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) to its implied claim.

**[Vary repetitive syntax through the ideas' connections](rules/deslopping/uniform-rhythm.md).** Look for successive sentences with the same opening, clause pattern, or length, and paragraphs built the same way. Connect sentences through relations already stated in the draft, following the [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md): a shared object, condition, time, or consequence. Do not invent causal, temporal, or contrastive links. Let those connections determine sentence structure instead of changing lengths at random. Preserve [parallel wording for parallel ideas](rules/prose/parallel-form.md) and [consistent technical terms](rules/conventions/terminology.md). Keep sentences complete and literal. Do not add [writerly asides](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md), contrasts, or emphasis, or vary a passage in ways that reduce clarity.

**[Replace significance inflation with the supported implication](rules/deslopping/significance-inflation.md).** Delete grand-trend commentary such as "marking a pivotal moment" or "paving the way" when it adds no substantive claim, or replace it with the draft's specific implication and scope. If it asserts a substantive but unsupported implication, keep it and flag the missing support.

**[Replace aphorism formulas with the mechanism](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md).** Replace a slogan-like analogy ("X is the Y of Z") with the mechanism that the draft states. Delete an empty slogan that makes no substantive claim. If the analogy asserts a substantive mechanism that the draft does not explain, keep it and flag the missing explanation.

**[Present successes and failures with comparable prominence](rules/deslopping/relentless-positivity.md).** When every result is framed as a success (relentless positivity or sycophantic framing), give reported failures and counterevidence comparable prominence and [calibrate each claim to its evidence](rules/claims/scope.md). Do not add a failure or balancing caveat, or hedge a supported finding, merely to make the tone seem cautious.
