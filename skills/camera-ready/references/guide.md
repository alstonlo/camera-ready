# Writing guide

This guide is generated from a collection of writing rules. Each rule has its own page in the `rules/` folder next to this guide and follows this format:

```markdown
# {{ Name of the rule }}

{{ A summary of the rule: what to look for, the edit or flag that it calls for, and the exceptions that change the edit. }}

## Details

{{ Explanation, further cases, and procedures omitted from the summary for concision, or, if there are none, the sentence "This rule has no further details." }}

## Examples

> **Context.** {{ Optional context that the example needs. }}
>
> **Bad.** {{ A passage that breaks the rule. }}
>
> **Good.** {{ The same passage, corrected under this rule alone. }}
```

The guide below reproduces each rule's heading and summary; each rule page adds `Details` and at least one example. Read a rule page in full when the rule applies to the text you are editing and the summary does not settle the edit, or when the summary names a case that its page covers. Each example illustrates one edit under its rule and does not widen what the request permits you to change.

## Table of contents

The guide has six sections of rules:

- [Story](#story): the argument from problem to implication, titles and headings, section order and openings, contributions, related work, results organized by question, and numbers that support claims.
- [Claims](#claims): reporting verbs that match claims to their evidence, scope, overclaiming and underclaiming, consistency across the text, causal language, what an efficiency claim counts, statistical significance, hedges, speculation, and limitations.
- [Cutting](#cutting): what to delete or relocate without a separate request from the author, the triage order under a length limit, what to protect, and how to repair the text around a cut.
- [Prose](#prose): explanation order, paragraphs, information flow, sentence structure, direct content in place of promises, concrete quantities, and plain wording.
- [Conventions](#conventions): voice, tense, terminology, mathematics, citations and credit, figures and tables, typography, and LaTeX source.
- [Deslopping](#deslopping): two tiers of patterns common in AI-written prose.

When a request is narrower than a full polish, apply the sections it names and every rule they link to. Check each chunk against every rule in each applicable section. When two rules prescribe different edits for the same sentence and one links to the other, follow the linked rule. Otherwise, choose the clearer sentence; if both are equally clear, use your judgment as an expert editor.

## Story

Organize the text around one argument: a problem the reader cares about, a gap with a cost, an idea that closes the gap, evidence that the idea works, and the implication. Check this argument across the whole text and its sections before editing individual sentences, because sentence-level edits cannot repair a broken argument. Use the [rules on claims](#claims) to set each statement's strength, the [rules on cutting](#cutting) to decide what stays, and the [rules on prose](#prose) to edit paragraphs and sentences.

Without a separate request from the author, you may:

- Reorder sentences within a paragraph or paragraphs within a section to put the claim first.
- Rewrite a section's opening using content already in that section.
- Remove numbers from running text when a table already contains them.
- Rewrite a caption to state a conclusion already drawn in the body text.

Flag any changes to the text's claims, section order, or list of contributions instead of making them yourself. Never supply a cost, explanation, or number that the draft does not contain.

### The whole argument

**[Summarize the argument in five lines](rules/story/five-line-summary.md).** Before editing, use the text itself to write one line each for the problem, the gap and its cost, the idea, the evidence, and the implication. Condense the idea and evidence lines into the core claims for the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md). Flag any line you cannot write from the text.

**[Summarize the argument in one sentence](rules/story/one-sentence-summary.md).** Combine the five lines into one sentence: "The problem matters; existing work leaves this gap, so we propose this idea, supported by this result." If the sentence identifies no gap, flag the missing problem. If it requires unrelated problems, flag the competing focuses; do not choose one or invent a claim that unifies them.

**[Map argument stages to section functions](rules/story/section-functions.md).** Check that each section serves its stage of the argument: the introduction states the problem, gap, idea, and contributions; background supplies only the context that the gap and comparisons need; methods explain the idea; results supply the evidence; discussion interprets the evidence, limitations, and alternatives; and the conclusion states the implications. Keep combined or repeated sections when the argument remains coherent, and follow venue requirements. Check that each result appears after the method context needed to understand it; for formats that place methods last, see Details. Make the contributions clear within the first quarter of the text. If a central result first appears after that point, or if the sections should be reordered, flag the problem instead of moving sections. A proposal describes the evidence it plans to gather.

The abstract states all five stages within the venue's word limit, using recognizable terms for the problem, the system studied, and the technique. It must be understandable without the body or bibliography; remove a pointer to a body section or figure only when the abstract already states that result. Flag a missing stage or result, or necessary content that cannot fit the limit; for a citation the abstract needs, see Details.

**[Keep the argument linear](rules/story/linear-argument.md).** Order material by the logic of the argument, not by the chronology or effort of the research. Across sections, avoid returning to a topic after leaving it for another (A, B, A); this extends the [rule against alternation](rules/prose/topic-order.md) beyond a single section. The core idea may recur in every section. Preserve repeated method and result stages when later reasoning depends on an earlier result; see [section functions](rules/story/section-functions.md). Also keep reminders of definitions the reader needs and contribution restatements that the triage order protects. Give components and counterarguments space in proportion to their role in the core claims. For how much to explain each side of a comparison, follow the [rule on comparing with prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md). Under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md), a rejected approach stays in the main text only as a negative result; state its flaw before its details. Flag repairs that move material between sections or that reorder sections.

**[Test the argument by skimming](rules/story/skim-test.md).** Check that a reader can identify all five stages of the argument by reading only the abstract, headings, first paragraph of each section, and [captions](rules/conventions/captions.md). Where the request allows, restore missing stages or connections using content already in the draft; flag missing content or repairs that would change a claim or the section order.

**[Flag titles that misrepresent or obscure the contribution](rules/story/titles.md).** Flag a title that hides the subject, has two plausible readings, or overstates the contribution. Suggest a revision that keeps the body's claim strength and conditions, but leave the title itself unchanged. Under the [acronym rule](rules/conventions/acronyms.md), flag an acronym in the title that readers outside the subfield would not know. Follow venue requirements.

### Problem, gap, and contribution

**[Open with the problem](rules/story/open-with-problem.md).** Name the paper's actual problem in its opening. If readers need context first, apply the [brief-setup rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md). Check that the opening predicts the problem and scope that the paper develops. Delete a [generic opening](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md); naming the topic alone does not establish a problem. Flag missing motivation rather than inventing an opening example.

**[State the gap's cost](rules/story/gap-cost.md).** State the specific gap and its cost: what readers lose, pay, cannot do, or cannot understand because of it. "No prior work studies X" states only the gap, not its cost. Replace "little is known" with the specific unknown when the draft supplies it. Flag a missing gap or cost. If stating the gap would require rewriting the objectives or contributions, flag the proposed rewrite instead of making it. The cost may be a question readers cannot answer or the value of extending earlier work to a new case; it need not be a flaw in prior work or a hypothesis, so do not invent either. Distinguish established consequences from [speculative applications](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Explain why the problem is hard](rules/story/why-hard.md).** State why the obvious approach fails. Without that explanation, the idea seems arbitrary, and the reader cannot tell whether the gap remains because it is hard to close or because it is unimportant. Flag a missing explanation.

**[State contributions as claims with their support](rules/story/contribution-claims.md).** For each contribution, state a checkable claim and name the section that supports it. Turn a contribution that names only an activity into a claim under the [underclaiming rule](rules/claims/underclaiming.md), at the strength the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) allows. Flag contributions without evidence. Treat a major result that supports no contribution as a candidate for the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md); do not delete it or change the list of contributions. When each contribution names its supporting section, delete any roadmap that names no content, under the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md).

### Section openings and connections

**[Open every section with its question and answer](rules/story/section-openings.md).** In each section's first paragraph, state the section's question and finding using content already in the section. An opening that merely announces the topic is a [signpost](rules/prose/signposts.md).

**[Connect each section to the preceding text](rules/story/section-connections.md).** Connect each section's question to content the text has established, following [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). Make the introduction understandable without the abstract. Keep short reminders for readers who start reading at Results, Discussion, or Conclusion, following [paragraph opening order](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md). Open a methods section with the key idea before the steps; place an existing [explanatory example](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) before the general case.

**[Make headings reveal the argument's structure](rules/story/headings.md).** Make each parent heading cover all of its subheadings, and give sibling headings (those under the same parent) [parallel form](rules/prose/parallel-form.md). Flag any of the following when it obscures the argument: a level with only one subheading, a fourth-level heading, or a very short section. When a heading could state its section's finding, flag it and propose a heading that keeps the body's claim strength and conditions; do not rewrite the heading yourself. Flag changes to the heading hierarchy, such as merging sections or removing a level, rather than making them yourself; preserve distinctions that help readers, and follow venue requirements.

### Related work

**[Compare prior work fairly on named dimensions](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md).** End each paragraph or group of paragraphs about prior work with a checkable difference in an assumption, condition, scope, metric, measured result, cost, or guarantee. Describe each source only as the draft does; flag missing context or a missing difference rather than supplying it. Explain both sides enough for readers to judge the comparison, stating each source's contribution and assumptions before criticizing it. Do not write "ignores" or "fails to" for a difference in scope unless the draft shows failure. Preserve [credit](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md), and keep a source's tentative verb such as "suggests" rather than strengthening it to "shows". Keep each position attributed to whoever holds it, including concessions and reported objections; a source's claim must not become the paper's assertion. For survey synthesis, see Details. A comparison that is vague on both sides is the [false concession](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md) pattern.

**[Distinguish new contributions from borrowed components](rules/story/novelty.md).** Distinguish the present contribution from standard components, borrowed ideas, and the authors' earlier results. When the text extends a preliminary publication, identify the stated additions. Preserve wording that identifies the authors' earlier work unless the venue requires anonymity; in that case, flag the wording. Do not make the authors' earlier work sound like independent confirmation. If the draft leaves the contribution or attribution unclear, flag it rather than assigning credit.

**[Flag prior-work comparisons the reader cannot yet understand](rules/story/prior-work-placement.md).** Detailed comparisons with prior work may appear in any section once readers have the context to understand them. Leave comparisons in place, and flag only those that appear before the material they depend on and cannot be understood without it. Background needed to understand the problem is not such a comparison. For the content of a comparison, follow the [rule on comparing with prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md).

### Results

**[Organize results by questions, not by experiments](rules/story/results-by-question.md).** State the questions that the Results section answers and connect each to a contribution. When a heading names only a dataset, sample, site, or setup, flag it and propose a heading that states a question or finding while keeping the body's claim strength and conditions. Preserve a sequence in which one result motivates the next experiment, and keep the interpretation of that result next to it; distinguish [measurement from inference](rules/claims/hedges.md). Flag experiments with no identifiable question as candidates for cutting, and flag proposed section moves or reordering. Flag a result with no described procedure or an evaluation procedure with no reported use; delete neither.

**[Answer one question per results paragraph](rules/story/one-question-per-paragraph.md).** Give each results paragraph one question and its answer, placing them as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md) directs. Put the evidence after the answer. Split a paragraph that answers two independent questions, and flag one that answers none.

**[Explain why the evidence supports the claim](rules/story/explain-evidence.md).** When readers outside the subfield may not see why a measurement or reason supports a claim, explain the connecting inference and check that its assumptions hold in this study. Use only the support that the draft gives, or flag the missing connection; do not supply a plausible reason that the draft does not state. Apply the rule on [scope](rules/claims/scope.md) to claims that exceed what the measurement establishes, and the rule on [formal support](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md) to inferences from a theorem.

**[Identify which part produces the effect](rules/story/component-effects.md).** When a method, treatment, intervention, material, or design combines several parts or changes, the results must show which part produces the reported effect. If no evidence isolates a component's effect, flag the missing evidence and credit only the combination under the [rule on causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md). Treat an untested explanation as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md). Flag any obvious alternative explanation that the text does not address. When the draft raises a counterexample, conflicting result, or rival explanation, flag the draft's reply to it if that reply gives neither evidence nor a reason why the objection does not apply; restating the claim is not an answer.

### Numbers

The most common flaw in the argument of AI-generated text is burying the point in the results. Reorder the text so that every number supports a claim already stated in words.

**[State the claim before the number](rules/story/claim-before-number.md).** State the finding in words before the number that supports it, then refer to the table for the remaining values. Identify the claim that each number supports. Flag a number that supports no identifiable claim; move it only when the request permits relocation and a suitable destination exists. When a table already gives a number, remove the number from the text only under the [headline-number rule](rules/story/headline-number.md), preserving needed comparisons and uncertainty.

**[Choose a headline number for each claim](rules/story/headline-number.md).** Choose a headline number for each claim and describe the pattern that the results support, including where it fails. Retain the baseline and any interval or range needed to interpret that number. Refer to an existing table for the remaining values; remove numbers that duplicate the table only when the text preserves the comparison, [uncertainty](rules/claims/variability.md), and [statistical conclusion](rules/claims/statistical-significance.md). Do not select an unrepresentative best case.

**[Match precision to the claim](rules/story/number-precision.md).** Match reported precision to the measurement and comparison, not to the number of digits software prints. Preserve words marking estimates or approximations. Flag unsupported precision or inconsistent precision among comparable quantities; never add digits, round values, or replace numbers without the author's authorization and the necessary source data.

### Discussion and conclusion

**[Return to the gap](rules/story/return-to-gap.md).** In the first paragraph of the discussion, explain how the results address the gap stated in the introduction. Then state the limitations under the [rule on limitations](rules/claims/limitations.md). If the introduction promises more than the results establish, flag the mismatch under the [rule on scope](rules/claims/scope.md).

**[Use the conclusion to add perspective](rules/story/conclusion-perspective.md).** Keep one connected restatement of each contribution under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md), then state what the established findings let readers understand or do. If readers could fully understand the conclusion without reading the paper, check whether it repeats generic statements instead of interpreting the findings; being understandable on its own is not a defect. Flag such generic repetition, a conclusion that merely repeats the abstract, new supporting evidence, or a shift to a subject not introduced earlier. Future work may identify a concrete question that arises from the findings and state what answering it would establish; do not invent a research plan. Preserve the findings' scope and [limitations](rules/claims/limitations.md), and label [speculative implications](rules/claims/speculation.md). When shortening contribution restatements, repair the connections between them; do not join copied sentences that rely on context the conclusion omits.

## Claims

Every claim must match its evidence: both overclaiming and underclaiming are errors. An aspirational goal may motivate the work as long as the text states that the goal remains unattained.

### Strength and scope

**[Choose the reporting verb from the verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md).** Choose each reporting verb from the verb ladder according to the evidence for the claim, regardless of enthusiasm for the result.

| Verb | Required support |
|---|---|
| **prove** | a theorem with stated assumptions, and nothing else |
| **show / demonstrate / establish** | direct evidence that rules out plausible alternatives |
| **find / observe / report** | a directly measured empirical result |
| **suggest / indicate**; **hypothesize / conjecture** | an inference or interpretation that could reasonably be wrong |

An empirical test, such as a benchmark or an assay, does not prove a mechanism. A figure or table shows only what it directly displays; for an interpretation drawn from it, choose the verb that matches the evidence. Replace *reveal* or *confirm* with a lower-rung verb if the evidence does not establish the stated conclusion. Flag a claimed disproof supported only by an objection or an inconclusive test. When reporting cited work, preserve whether the source presents a result as central or incidental, and whether it accepts or doubts the result. See the page for verb choices and [prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md) for attribution.

A claim hedged in Results must keep its hedge in the abstract, introduction, conclusion, and every other summary. Trace each summary claim to its supporting result, and flag any summary claim that has none. When body support exists, add a missing section, table, or figure pointer, except in the abstract, which must state the result itself.

**[Match scope and confidence to the evidence](rules/claims/scope.md).** Use the broadest scope and most direct confidence the evidence supports, from a guarantee to one observed case, without [underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md). Limit each claim, especially a headline claim, to the conditions, datasets, and settings its evidence covers. If support is uncertain, leave the claim and flag it. A bare generic plural such as "Solar cells degrade in humid air" can state a tendency, but it still makes a claim about the whole class; when the evidence covers only the tested cases, name them ("the four cell designs we test"). Check whether absolutes such as "always" or "unique" extend a claim to untested cases. One in-scope counterexample defeats a universal claim but need not defeat a tendency.

A micrograph, case report, generated sample, or other single example shows only that a case occurs, not how often. For a broader claim, the draft must explain how the example was selected, such as at random, as typical, or as the best case; flag missing information. Keep any explicit label marking an example as hypothetical or constructed, and never add such a label by inference. A broad claim drawn from sampled data needs a justified population and an account of how the sample was selected; check a claim of [independent confirmation](rules/claims/variability.md) under its own rule.

Flag a research question or conclusion that, without support, treats a score as the broader property it measures indirectly or treats a ratio of scores as a proportional change in that property. Do not rewrite the question or add a caveat to fix the mismatch. A claim of superiority needs a relevant baseline; flag missing comparisons. A claim such as "ready for clinical use" or "ready for production" needs evidence about costs and operating conditions beyond gains under test conditions; flag missing support, but keep applications that the draft explicitly marks as tentative.

For temporal trends, small-sample probabilities, baseline selection, score differences, and wording that assumes an unstated fact ("the solution", "restores accuracy"), see the page.

**[Remove or flag unsupported empirical labels](rules/claims/unsupported-labels.md).** Remove or flag unsupported empirical labels such as *solves*, *optimal*, *guarantees*, and *first to*; keep them only when the draft's evidence supports the full claim. Reserve *proves* for theorems, as the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) requires. Flag unsupported superlatives as well as *state-of-the-art*, *record*, or *human-level* claims that omit the task or quantity, the compared methods or results, or the metric. Remove *novel* when it adds only praise. Beyond removing an unsupported label, change what the claim asserts only when the draft's evidence settles its [scope](rules/claims/scope.md); otherwise flag it. For claims that a method is empirically unique, see the page.

**[State supported contributions without underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md).** State supported contributions directly, without hedges that the evidence does not call for. Each item in a list of contributions should state a checkable claim and point to its support where the draft provides it. An item that uses only an activity verb such as *study*, *explore*, or *investigate* states what was done rather than what was found; replace it with the finding in the corresponding section, or flag the missing finding.

**[Reconcile conflicting claims and numerical constraints](rules/claims/internal-consistency.md).** Compare claims about the same object across sections and lists, such as one passage stating that the object supports an operation and another stating that it cannot. Check totals, bounds, and trends against constraints the draft establishes. If a difference in setting or definition stated in the draft reconciles the claims, flag both passages with a revision that makes the difference explicit; otherwise flag both passages and the missing explanation. Flag numerical conflicts rather than choosing a value. Also flag any exhaustive either/or claim when the draft names another in-scope option, and identify that option.

### Causation and formal support

**[Reserve causal language for causal evidence](rules/claims/causal-language.md).** Use "causes", "leads to", "improves because", or other wording that credits a factor (such as a design choice, treatment, or exposure) with an effect only when the evidence isolates that effect, as a suitable controlled comparison can. Describe correlations with "is associated with" or "correlates with". Preserve the direction of cause and effect, and do not present a partial contribution as the sole cause. When several changes are tested only together, credit their combination, not a single component, and do not claim that each component is necessary. Flag unsupported causal attribution, and mark an untested mechanism as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Identify the kind of evidence supporting each claim](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md).** When a sentence reads like a theorem but cites no formal result and states no conditions, clarify its status: link it to the proved result and its assumptions, restate it as the empirical or intuitive claim the draft supports, or flag it. When the text uses a theorem to support an empirical claim, check that the theorem's assumptions hold in that setting and flag any invalid application; an unmet assumption makes the theorem inapplicable, not false. Never drop a condition from a theorem or other formal statement to simplify it; instead, flag any condition that seems unnecessary. Distinguish proofs, model calculations, simulations, and measurements of the real system. Flag any claim that extends a model result to the real system without supporting evidence, though a valid claim limited to the model needs no experiment or field study of the real system.

**[Specify what an efficiency claim counts](rules/claims/efficiency-claims.md).** For a claim about efficiency, speed, yield, cost, sample efficiency, or scaling, check that the text states what is counted and per what unit, the conditions and assumptions, the comparator if a comparison is claimed, and which costs are included or excluded. Define both the numerator and the denominator of a ratio. For an analytical bound, also check that the text defines the size variables and specifies which case is analyzed (see Details). Add any missing detail that the draft states elsewhere; otherwise flag it. A better ratio or asymptotic bound alone does not establish lower total cost under the claimed conditions. Flag suspected analytical errors without changing the mathematics.

### Numerical comparisons

**[Report variability and compare like with like](rules/claims/variability.md).** For each numerical comparison, check that the text states the number of runs, replicates, or samples, how they are aggregated, what ± means, and the source of variation, either beside the comparison or in a reporting convention; flag missing information. A best run is not comparable with a mean. Flag a comparison, including one with results borrowed from other papers, unless the text establishes that both sides use equivalent tasks, outputs, quality requirements, aggregation, and protocols. When a gain in one measure comes with a loss in another, state the trade-off; if the draft does not establish comparable performance on the other measure, flag the missing comparison.

Every percentage needs its base, and a reported change must specify whether it is a relative percentage change or a difference in percentage points. Edit the wording when the draft settles which one is meant; otherwise flag the ambiguity, and never relabel by guessing. Keep each ranking tied to its metric, and flag selective reporting.

Data used to select or tune a method cannot independently test that choice. Flag any claim of independent confirmation that rests on reused data, related datasets, or correlated metrics, unless the draft establishes a separate test. For misleading averages, systematic measurement errors (including timing bias), and how convergence error is measured and normalized, see the page.

**[Draw only the conclusions a statistical test supports](rules/claims/statistical-significance.md).** Use *significant* only for the result of a named statistical test, reported with its p-value or significance level and any effect size the draft supplies; flag missing information. When *significant* means large or important, replace it with the reported magnitude. Keep available effect estimates and uncertainty even when the test is not significant. A p-value gives the probability, under the null hypothesis and test assumptions, of observing results at least as extreme as those measured, not the probability that a hypothesis is true. Preserve the tested hypothesis and the test's direction. Failing to reject the null hypothesis does not establish equality, and statistical significance does not establish practical importance. Whether two intervals overlap settles neither superiority nor its absence; flag any claim of superiority inferred from interval overlap alone, without a test. When the draft tests significance across several datasets, outcomes, or metrics, flag a missing explanation of how it corrects for multiple comparisons. For reporting only a significance threshold, and for statistical power, see the page.

### Qualification and limits

**[Hedge interpretations, not measurements or procedures](rules/claims/hedges.md).** Hedge tentative interpretations, including proposed mechanisms, but state measurements and performed procedures directly while retaining measurement uncertainty. For a performed procedure, hedge only its rationale. Change a claim's confidence only when the evidence settles it, and keep both the claim's [verb-ladder strength](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) and its hedge in every summary. Use at most one hedge per uncertain claim, in a standard form such as "may", "likely", or "suggests"; deleting the only hedge on a claim strengthens it. When removing a redundant hedge, keep hedges on other uncertain claims and preserve any [scope condition](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md), meaning a qualifier such as *usually*, *on average*, or *in most runs* whose deletion would change the conditions or cases a claim covers. Replace vague softeners such as *somewhat* or *relatively* with the conditions or magnitudes the draft supplies; otherwise keep them and flag the gap.

**[Never state speculation as fact](rules/claims/speculation.md).** Do not present a proposed explanation as established when the draft supplies no supporting test or argument. Mark an untested mechanism without other support as conjecture ("We conjecture that"), or place it in a passage explicitly labeled speculative. Flag an explanation that relies on an undefined term or an unstated measurement rather than marking it as a conjecture; unclear writing alone does not make an explanation speculative. A claim that one property does not imply another requires a counterexample, supporting result, or reference, and a question that the draft states is open must remain open. Similarly, failure under the tested conditions does not establish impossibility; flag any unsupported negative conclusion.

**[State limitations precisely and explain their effects](rules/claims/limitations.md).** State each limitation in full, precisely and without apology, where it first affects a method or inference, and keep brief reminders that bound later claims. Next to the limitation, keep the draft's explanation of how it affects the results and which conclusions still hold; flag a missing explanation. Flag unexplained anomalies and unjustified exclusions. Also flag a decoy limitation, which names a minor weakness while hiding a larger one documented in the draft. Any future-work statement must identify the current limit it addresses. When the Discussion introduces an interpretation, a limitation of that interpretation may appear there first. Move a limitation to another section only as the [relocation rules](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md) allow; delete duplicate full accounts only when a length request reaches them in the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md).

## Cutting

Use the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md) to determine what the argument needs, and check the request to see what you may delete or relocate.

### Deciding what to cut

**[Delete bloat without asking](rules/cutting/bloat.md).** Delete [chatbot artifacts](rules/deslopping/chatbot-phrases.md), [generic openings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md), and [leaked artifacts](rules/deslopping/generation-artifacts.md), and repair [em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md), following each linked rule and its exceptions. Delete content announcements and signposts only as the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md) allows. Tighten the remaining sentences without changing their meaning. You may make these edits without a separate request, whereas [idea-level cuts](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md) require one.

**[Cut or relocate ideas only on request](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md).** Delete or relocate substantive content only when the request authorizes condensation or sets a length limit; otherwise, leave the content in place and flag it as a candidate for cutting or relocation. Substantive content includes anything the author would miss, such as an idea, result, caveat, piece of evidence, example, rationale, or necessary transition. Reordering material within a section to [build understanding](rules/prose/explanation-order.md) is a prose edit, whereas moving content between sections or out of the main text is relocation. A request to polish or tighten the text permits bloat removal, not idea-level cuts.

**[Apply the spine test before cutting sentences](rules/cutting/spine-test.md).** State the text's core claims, which form its spine, in one to three sentences, and summarize each paragraph's role in one line. Tag each paragraph as **central** if it states a core claim, **supporting** if readers need it to understand or evaluate a core claim, **ancillary** if it is relevant but dispensable to that evaluation, or **off-spine** if it is unrelated to the core claims. Paragraphs that carry counterevidence or other [protected content](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md) are supporting if they are not central; never treat them as expendable. If you cannot write a short, specific title that summarizes a section, flag its competing points before cutting. When [the request](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md) permits cutting, cut or relocate off-spine paragraphs, trim ancillary paragraphs, and then edit [sentence by sentence](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md). Keep a cut only if the argument remains at least as clear.

**[Cut in triage order under a length constraint](rules/cutting/triage-order.md).** Under a length constraint, cut material in the following triage order while respecting [protected content](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md) and [relocation safeguards](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md):

1. **Repeated restatement.** Cut points repeated across the introduction, section openings, and conclusion, keeping the most useful statement (usually the one that develops the point). Always preserve one statement of each contribution in each of the abstract, the introduction, and the conclusion, along with [shorthand reminders](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md). Keep a paragraph's closing restatement when readers need the intervening explanation to understand the opening claim. If a restatement copies another passage verbatim, such as a conclusion that repeats the abstract word for word, revise it using established results or flag it. Apply the [deletion test](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).
2. **Signposting left after bloat removal.** Cut remaining signposts, including the [roadmap](rules/prose/signposts.md) otherwise permitted at the end of the introduction, but preserve pointers to evidence and proofs.
3. **Tangential background and related work.** Cut background that does not motivate the research question, and cut citation lists unrelated to the gap. Keep the few works and comparisons that define the gap.
4. **Minor results, secondary analyses, and exploratory findings.** Remove from the main text any results that do not advance a central claim. Relocate sensitivity analyses and robustness checks needed for rigor, but keep in the main text any failure or counterevidence that alters a central claim. If a failed approach remains in the main text, state it as a negative result rather than narrating a history of attempts.
5. **Exhaustive parameters and procedural detail.** Keep enough detail in the main text to make the method reproducible in outline, including [explicit procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md), essential steps, and conditions; relocate complete parameter lists, full parameter sweeps, and subsidiary details.

**[Protect sentences the argument depends on](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md).** A sentence can be necessary even when it adds no new facts. Preserve the following material, relocating it only when the [sentence-by-sentence steps](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md) allow:

- Keep hedges, qualifiers, scope conditions, limitations, supporting evidence, and counterevidence. Cut a repeated limitation only when the triage order reaches it and a full statement remains elsewhere. Because removing a caveat changes a [claim's scope](rules/claims/scope.md), [recalibrate the claim](rules/cutting/post-cut-check.md) after an authorized cut only as the remaining evidence requires.
- Keep the reasons for methodological choices and the details needed for reproducibility, including statistical tests, how missing data are handled, and corrections for multiple comparisons.
- Keep definitions, topic sentences, necessary framing, [high-level summaries](rules/prose/direct-content.md), [logical transitions](rules/prose/transitions.md), and back-references that later text relies on. Preserve [standalone caption content](rules/conventions/captions.md), including "adapted from" credits and permission notices, even when it repeats the body text.
- Preserve fair credit and accurate characterization of prior work, acknowledgments, funding statements, disclosures of assistance (including help from AI tools), and disclosures of financial or other relationships that could bias the work.

### Making the cut

**[Repair dependencies when cutting or relocating sentences](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md).** For each sentence within [the request's scope](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md), follow four steps:

1. **Check its role.** Ask whether a core claim or the reader needs the sentence, and apply "Protect sentences the argument depends on" before considering deletion.
2. **Check dependencies.** Check whether other text depends on the sentence through a later reference such as "this" or "as shown above", a term defined or citation supplied only here, a hedge or piece of evidence for a claim elsewhere, or the paragraph's topic string. Repair every dependency in the same edit, or keep the sentence.
3. **Choose deletion or relocation.** Delete only content that no reader needs. Relocate content that only some readers or a skeptical reviewer will need, and never delete details needed for reproducibility. Move material to an appendix or supplement only if that destination already exists; otherwise, keep the material in place and flag what the destination would need to contain. Make relocated material understandable on its own, point to it from the relevant place in the main text, and keep enough of the method and rationale in the main text for readers to judge the results. If the venue permits footnotes, you may move subsidiary comments into footnotes. Keep essential steps of a method, derivation, or algorithm in the main explanation, along with definitions used later; see the page for parenthetical asides.
4. **Read across the gap.** Confirm that the sentence after the gap opens with a referent or result supplied by the remaining text. Restore any lost link using content from the draft, or keep the original framing sentence.

**[Revise cuts when stop rules apply](rules/cutting/stop-rules.md).** Revise a cut if it leaves a paragraph underdeveloped or breaks its [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md): either merge the paragraph with an adjacent one that makes the same point, or restore the missing development and connections. Before cutting a confusing passage, check whether a step is missing, because confusion usually signals an omission rather than excess. Supply a missing step or [referent](rules/prose/referents.md) only from elsewhere in the draft, and flag it if the draft does not contain it. If a reader outside the subfield cannot follow the shortened passage, restore the needed framing and steps instead of cutting further. These stop rules override the length target. Never meet a length target by adding negative `\vspace`, reducing font sizes, or narrowing figures; report the remaining excess instead.

**[Recheck claims, the argument, and references after cutting](rules/cutting/post-cut-check.md).** After cutting, confirm that each surviving claim keeps its evidence, scope, and hedges under the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md). When supporting evidence has been relocated, add a precise reference to its new location; weaken a claim only when the remaining evidence supports less than before, not merely because the support moved. Reread the problem, gap, approach, findings, and implications, and restore any logical steps the cut removed so that readers do not have to reconstruct them. Finally, repair dangling references, uncited figures or tables, pointers to nonexistent appendix sections, and terms used before they are defined.

## Prose

**[Build understanding early](rules/prose/explanation-order.md).** Give the main idea before its mechanism, and give a finding and its interpretation before the supporting evidence. State a section's claim in its first paragraph, open each paragraph as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md) directs, and define each unfamiliar term before its first use. To test the order, cover everything after a sentence: if a covered sentence explains something that the visible text needs, move that explanation earlier. You may reorder sentences within a paragraph and paragraphs within a section, but flag moves between sections. Within a sentence, follow [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md) and [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).

**[Apply the deletion test without changing meaning](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).** Delete a word or clause only when it adds nothing and the sentence remains as easy to understand; brevity alone does not justify an edit. Before keeping any deletion, replacement, or restructuring, check that the revision preserves the original's referents, actors, actions, definitions, logical relations, modality (whether something is possible, required, or actual), scope, and authorial stance, as well as the [structural words](rules/prose/first-reading.md) that the reader needs on the first reading. Preserve distinctions even when a simpler version conveys the gist, and apply the [claim rules](rules/claims/scope.md) to any substantive change. When you are uncertain whether the revision and the original mean the same thing, keep the original and flag the ambiguity.

### Paragraphs

**[Develop one point per paragraph](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md).** Develop one point per paragraph, and state it as a specific claim in the paragraph's first sentence. "Results were mixed" and "We analyze robustness" [announce](rules/prose/signposts.md) a topic; "Yield falls above 80 °C" states a claim. Allow brief setup only when the claim cannot be understood first: one sentence of necessary context from the draft, immediately followed by the claim. A results paragraph may open with the specific question it tests if the next sentence answers it. Put the evidence after the answer. A procedural or descriptive paragraph may begin with its first fact or step. Keep both sides of a comparison in one paragraph when they support one point. Split a paragraph where its point changes, and merge short paragraphs only when they develop the same point. Judge a paragraph by development and coherence, not length.

**[Use lists for genuine enumeration](rules/prose/list-structure.md).** Use a list for items readers must distinguish or follow separately. Turn bullets that make an argument into connected prose when the bullets hide the reasons or the links between items. Remove numbering that implies an unsupported order or ranking; keep numbers the text uses to refer to items. Flag any proposed change to a list of contributions or objectives, such as regrouping, renumbering, or converting it to prose, instead of making it.

**[Use transitions that express a valid logical relation](rules/prose/transitions.md).** Use a connective, such as "therefore" or "however", only for the relation the draft supports. When "as", "since", or "while" could mean time, cause, or contrast, reword it so that one reading remains; "conversely" needs an actual reversal. Remove a redundant connective when the sentence already expresses that relation, but keep a link the reader needs. If "therefore" introduces a conclusion the preceding premises do not support, flag the missing inference. Causal wording must satisfy the [causal-evidence rule](rules/claims/causal-language.md).

**[Finish one topic before starting another](rules/prose/topic-order.md).** Finish one logical topic before switching to another, and repair any unexplained return to an earlier topic. In a comparison organized by named dimensions, each dimension can be the topic: discuss both methods on one dimension before moving to the next. Preserve that structure rather than regrouping the text into one block per method. Reorder sentences and paragraphs within a section when needed; flag changes to section order.

**[Read the topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md).** When each sentence is clear but a paragraph does not cohere, list the opening words of each sentence; this list is the topic string. Check that these openings develop the paragraph's point rather than merely repeating words between sentences. Consecutive sentences may keep the same topic, and a new term at the end of a sentence need not recur unless the text promises to develop it, but if most sentences open with new material, rewrite them so that each opens with [old information](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). Next, identify the logical relation between each pair of adjacent sentences, such as contrast, consequence, or example. When a link between sentences is missing, restore it from elsewhere in the draft or flag the gap; a connective cannot replace missing reasoning. If two adjacent paragraphs could swap places without other edits, add a [summary naming their parallel items](rules/prose/direct-content.md) when they present parallel items; otherwise, state how the second paragraph depends on the first.

### Information flow within a sentence

**[Put old information before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md).** Open each sentence with something the preceding text has given the reader: a term it has used, or a part, property, or alternative the reader can infer from it. A concept is not old information merely because the field knows it. When several elements are old, open with the one the new sentence builds on; if they are equally relevant, choose the most recently mentioned one. When changing voice or clause order, keep what the paragraph is about as the main subject, and preserve a useful passive under [alternative sentence orders](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).

**[Put emphasized information in the stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).** The stress position is the end of a sentence or independent clause, where the clause becomes grammatically complete. Put important new information in that position. When a trailing qualifier such as "in these tests" buries the point, move the qualifier to the front of the sentence rather than deleting it. If several important items compete for one ending, split the sentence so that each receives emphasis. Check whether a paragraph's first sentence ends on the topic that the paragraph goes on to develop. When joining or subordinating clauses, preserve the relative weight of findings and qualifications. Move an essential qualification out of parentheses when the parentheses make it look optional. Keep each scope condition attached to its claim, and follow [pause punctuation](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) when using colons or semicolons.

**[Keep old information first in passives, fronting, and inversions](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).** In a passive with a by-phrase, keep the subject at least as familiar as the agent named in the by-phrase. A fronted phrase (one placed before the subject) or an inversion ("Closest to our work is X") must begin with information the passage has established; otherwise use ordinary subject-verb-object order. A sentence in ordinary subject-verb-object order may deliberately open with new information when doing so is clearer. Preserve a passive, a "there is" opening, or an "it ... that" construction when it introduces new material without making that material an unfamiliar subject.

### Sentence structure

**[Put actors in subjects and actions in verbs](rules/prose/character-action.md).** A nominalization is an action expressed as a noun. When a phrase pairs a weak verb with a nominalization ("perform an evaluation of", "lead to an improvement in"), or when several nominalizations occur in one clause, put the actor in the subject and the action in the verb. Keep a nominalization that names a technique ("quantization") or a concept the text keeps discussing, that refers back to the previous sentence ("This reduction in noise ..."), that replaces an awkward "the fact that" clause, or that appears alone in an otherwise clear sentence. Preserve every action; if the draft never states who performs an action, flag the gap. With an inanimate subject, replace a verb that requires intention, thought, perception, feeling, memory, or eating ("hopes", "forgets", "swallows") with a verb that names the literal event. Keep established technical usage, such as an enzyme recognizing its substrate, a sensor detecting light, or a function expecting input, and keep a component, instrument, or organism as the subject of the operation it performs. A result or figure may be the subject of a [reporting verb](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) ("the results suggest"). Follow [voice](rules/conventions/active-voice.md) and [information flow](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).

**[Make every sentence clear on the first reading](rules/prose/first-reading.md).** Repair what forces rereading; length alone is not a defect. Bring subjects next to verbs and verbs next to complements, and shorten an overloaded opening phrase or clause. Move a long subject clause or mid-sentence interruption later unless it carries old information, while keeping short, familiar asides. Unpack stacks of three or more nouns and long chains of modifiers, counting an established technical compound as one unit. Clarify which noun a trailing relative clause describes and what each participle modifies. Read left to right for false starts, including coordination that first seems to join the wrong items. Keep or restore articles and clause markers such as "that" after "assume", "show", or "find" when omitting them permits a misreading, but do not add optional words to short, predictable clauses or "that" after "we have". Put "only" beside what it restricts. Place conditions before a claim unless a short trailing condition is unambiguous. Give long conditions their own sentence, and enumerate three or more steps or conditions packed into one sentence, preserving their scope when splitting. Use the diagnostics in Details for omitted markers or unclear modifier attachment. If grammatical prose still compresses too many concepts, unpack the explanation under [definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).

**[Express parallel ideas in parallel form without changing meaning](rules/prose/parallel-form.md).** Give ideas with the same logical role the same grammatical form, but preserve meaningful differences and qualifications. Do not rename unlike objects or delete a condition to create symmetry. Check which coordinated items share each modifier and predicate; when that scope is unclear, repeat words or split the sentence. Compare things of the same kind, and restore an omitted metric or predicate when needed to identify both sides of a comparison. When joining words, phrases, or clauses as equals hides a causal or qualifying relation, state the relation the draft supports. Keep clear idiomatic variation. Follow [list structure](rules/prose/list-structure.md) when choosing layout.

**[Do not open a sentence with "And", "But", "So", or "Plus"](rules/prose/sentence-initial-conjunctions.md).** Do not begin a sentence with "And", "But", "So", or "Plus". Join the sentence to the preceding one or replace the opening word, preserving its logical relation: keep a contrast as a contrast, and mark a consequence only when the draft supports it. For the final item of a series, use "Finally", not "In addition", "Moreover", or "Furthermore", which mark further support. For a cited author's objection, follow [prior-work attribution](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md). Keep clear coordination within a sentence.

**[Read the sentence aloud](rules/prose/read-aloud.md).** Read the sentence aloud, or simulate doing so, to locate hesitation, unclear stress, or missing links; then repair the structural cause. A smooth delivery does not prove clarity: the words and syntax must supply the intended reading without imagined pauses, emphasis, or prior knowledge. A punctuation change alone may not repair the structure. Repair the wording itself with literal statements; do not add a [writerly aside](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md) that tells readers how to read the sentence.

### Content instead of promises

**[State the content directly](rules/prose/direct-content.md).** Replace promises of deferred content and generic apologies about complexity with the content the draft supplies; flag missing content. Delete a trailing promise such as "and we discuss each below" when the content is already present. Keep a summary sentence that states the high-level idea, and keep a count whose items follow immediately ("We make four observations. First, ..."). Keep sentences that state a real dispute, an unresolved definition, or a [limitation](rules/claims/limitations.md); delete only a generic warning about complexity, never a statement of real uncertainty. To test a sentence, cover the rest of the paragraph and ask whether the sentence alone says anything readers can understand or check.

**[State the finding instead of a reporting policy](rules/prose/reporting-policies.md).** When a sentence such as "we report both metrics" says how findings will be reported instead of stating them, replace it with the conclusions, measurements, and outcomes it refers to, taken from the draft's results, or flag any missing results. Keep a sentence that describes an actual reporting or analysis procedure when it explains what was done rather than standing in for a result.

**[Combine signposts with the content they introduce](rules/prose/signposts.md).** Replace sentences about the text, such as "The previous section described the apparatus. This section presents the measurements.", with the content they announce and, if needed, a reference to its location. Keep references that locate evidence or a proof, and keep a roadmap of at most two sentences at the end of an introduction or chapter opening. Delete three kinds of signpost: announcements that name no content ("Section 7 concludes"), roadmaps within a section that repeat its subheadings, and signposts that take more effort to understand than they save. Preserve [genuine transitions and summaries](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md). For a retained roadmap, check its count, order, labels, and distinctions against the actual sections or items. Repair a mismatch that the draft resolves unambiguously; flag conflicting categories and any repair that would change the section order or the contributions.

**[State the reasons for a choice](rules/prose/choice-reasons.md).** Unless the omitted items are obvious, replace "for various reasons", or an "etc." that ends a list, with the reasons or items the draft supplies; if the draft omits them, flag the gap. When an unexplained method choice is central to a stated claim, give the rationale the draft states, or flag the missing rationale. Do not request justifications for routine choices whose role is clear. A stated reason for a choice is not evidence that the choice caused a result; claims of cause follow [causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md).

### Concrete quantities and operations

**[Replace empty verbs with the missing change or operation](rules/prose/empty-verbs.md).** When "affects", "varies", or "has implications for" leaves a reported change unspecified, state its direction and magnitude. When "provides", "enables", or "allows" names only an outcome, state the operation that achieves it. If the draft does not supply the needed measurement or operation, flag the gap. Keep wording that already explains the change or capability, and do not demand a measured magnitude for an explicitly untested hypothesis.

**[Name the measured quantity instead of an umbrella noun](rules/prose/umbrella-nouns.md).** When a measurement is hidden behind an umbrella noun such as "performance" or "quality", replace the noun with the measured quantity, such as accuracy, yield, sensitivity, or energy use. Keep a defined collective label when it accurately covers several named quantities.

**[Quantify comparatives](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md).** Replace a vague comparative with the measurement the draft reports, and name what it is compared with. Lead with the [headline number](rules/story/headline-number.md), describe the pattern, and refer to the table for other values. A label such as "large" needs a stated comparison or threshold; it cannot replace the measurement. Do not calculate an unreported ratio, such as a speedup or a fold change, merely to make the prose more concrete.

**[Cut intensifiers](rules/prose/intensifiers.md).** Cut empty intensifiers such as "very" and "extremely", or replace them with a [measurement](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md) when the draft supplies one. Treat "essentially" and "largely" as [softeners](rules/claims/hedges.md), preserving their scope or uncertainty unless the draft supplies a precise replacement. Delete "Note that" and similar prompts unless they prevent a likely misreading. Keep "obviously", "clearly", or "trivially" only for a proof step that is routine for a reader outside the subfield; remove them from empirical claims such as "clearly outperforms", and flag any nonroutine step whose explanation is missing.

### Plain wording

**[Say what literally happens](rules/prose/literal-wording.md).** Name the actual object and operation. Replace inflated "utilize" or "leverage" with "use" when equivalent. Check whether an abstract word such as "approach", "framework", "strategy", or "issue" hides a more specific meaning, but keep any defined term that the paper reuses as shorthand. Name an object before labeling its role. Expand figurative verbs, nouns used as verbs for one occasion ("we spreadsheet the readings"), and unusual word pairs when readers must decode them, following [parts of speech](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md). Preserve established technical terms, verbs, and word combinations; when explaining an unfamiliar term, keep the population, metric, and denominator it covers. Replace an opaque phrasal verb only with an exact equivalent. Keep an existing analogy whose correspondence the draft makes clear, and replace decorative or misleading imagery with literal wording without adding a new analogy. The [aphorism rule](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md) governs slogan formulas. Use the diagnostics in Details for an ambiguous preposition or for a "replace" or "substitute" phrase that leaves unclear which item takes the other's place.

**[Use logical words unambiguously](rules/prose/logical-words.md).** Replace ambiguous "any" with "every" or "at least one" according to the intended meaning; "some" requires only one case and leaves open whether every case qualifies. Use "each" with a singular noun instead of "all" with a plural when the plural could suggest that the objects share one value ("each sample is held at its own temperature"). Rewrite a negative statement as a positive one only when the two statements hold in exactly the same cases, including values equal to a threshold. Keep a deliberate denial as a denial, and do not replace a negated property, including a monotonicity property, with its apparent opposite ("not connected" does not mean "every vertex is isolated"). When "can" or "should" could mean capability, possibility, or obligation, use the meaning the draft establishes, or flag the ambiguity if the draft establishes none; for these words, also follow [hedges](rules/claims/hedges.md) and [performed actions](rules/conventions/performed-actions.md). Replace an ambiguous slash in prose with the words it stands for, such as "or" or "and", while keeping standard notation and abbreviations. Preserve whether a list is complete; "includes" alone does not imply omitted members. Keep "then" after an "if" clause whose conclusion is a formula or technical claim, and when "contains" could mean membership or subset inclusion, state which. Use the diagnostics in Details for nested conditions, negation scope, or a rhetorical "if" mistaken for a condition.

**[Spell out definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).** Explain each metric, rule, and procedure the reader relies on, including how to interpret or reproduce its results. A definition must identify the kind of thing named and what distinguishes it; an acronym expansion is not enough. Accompany formal definitions that the reader needs with a plain-language explanation, and for related concepts, state both their shared category and how they differ. When definitions compete, state which meaning the paper adopts and its justification from the draft; neither invent competing definitions nor drop ones the draft mentions. Do not flag standard steps covered by a precise citation, but keep every modification to them and the explanation readers need at that point. When a procedure's description only lists its steps, state the purpose the draft gives, or flag that the purpose is missing. Check that worked examples match the stated method or definition, and flag mismatches rather than changing either. Use the diagnostics in Details when an explanation is hard to follow or an example lacks a stated purpose. Apply the domain checks in Details (for algorithms, numerical work, experiments, data and samples, or human participants) only during a story or claims check, or when a result depends on a missing setting. Delete or move reproducibility detail only where the [cutting rules](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md) allow it.

**[Make every referent clear](rules/prose/referents.md).** Make each pronoun, demonstrative, comparative, and shorthand noun identify one referent from the text alone. Name an ambiguous baseline or previous run using the draft's identifiers, then reuse that name. Restore context lost during editing or flag the missing connection. The first sentence under a heading, including a run-in paragraph title such as "**Sample preparation.**", must make sense without that heading. When adding a noun after an ambiguous "this", follow the [rule on ambiguous "this"](rules/prose/ambiguous-this.md).

**[Add an accurate noun after an ambiguous "this"](rules/prose/ambiguous-this.md).** When "this", "that", or "it" could refer to several nouns or claims, add a concise noun identifying the supported referent. If the draft does not settle the referent, flag the ambiguity rather than choosing one. The noun must preserve the earlier statement's status and evaluation: a conjecture is not a finding, and a change is not necessarily an improvement. A bare demonstrative may remain in a clear interpretive opening such as "This suggests that" after one unambiguous claim.

**[Replace writerly asides with literal claims and reasons](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md).** Replace conversational idioms such as "Call it a wash" and clipped editorial instructions with complete literal statements of the claim and its reason. Use only reasons the draft supplies; flag a missing explanation rather than adding one. Keep a deliberately short sentence when it is complete and literal. Preserve the author's supported stance under [author voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).

## Conventions

These conventions override generic writing advice.

### Voice and tense

**[Use active voice with a consistent agent](rules/conventions/active-voice.md).** Default to active "we" for the authors' actions and decisions. Do not use "we" for the research community or people in general, though in derivations and proofs "we" may include the reader. Use one agent throughout a method description, either the method or "we", and always use "we" for design choices. Preserve a single author's consistent "I" or "we". Keep passives when the actor is obvious, irrelevant, or unknown, when they keep [old information first](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md), when a long agent belongs at the end, or when a passive modifier ("the samples selected by technicians") is easier to read. Do not assign an action to the authors when the draft does not say who performed it.

**[State performed actions directly](rules/conventions/performed-actions.md).** When the draft shows that an action was performed, replace "can", "aims to", or "seeks to" with a direct verb. Keep those words for a capability that the study did not use and for a goal that the draft says remains unattained; removing that distinction would [change the claim](rules/claims/unsupported-labels.md).

**[Match tense to the time frame](rules/conventions/tense.md).** Use present tense for the paper's contents ("Section 4 shows", not "will show"), results, method, setup, established facts, and continuing prior work. Preserve a draft's consistent past tense for its experiments, and use past tense for events outside the described procedure. Use future tense for uncompleted plans, not for later sections. Keep tense consistent within a time frame, and prefer simple tense unless perfect tense marks current relevance or an event before another past event. Keep past tense in reported findings ("Lee found that the alloy was weaker") and hypotheticals ("if the sample were larger"). Note that "No work had studied X before Lee" says only what held before Lee, not whether the gap remains open. Do not turn a general research recommendation into the authors' own promise. For relative dates, see the page.

### Terminology

**[Use one term per concept](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md).** Use one term per concept throughout the text; do not alternate "device", "sensor", "instrument", and "system" for variety. List the names used for each recurring concept: multiple names are an error unless they denote distinct concepts. Preserve differences in type, scope, or measured quantity. In a parallel or more general case, repeat the earlier wording verbatim, then state what differs. You may refer back with an unambiguous pronoun ("it") or bare demonstrative ("this") in the same or next sentence, but do not replace a specific name with a category noun ("this compound", "the device"); repeat the name instead. Check captions and visual labels as well, and flag inconsistencies that prose edits cannot repair.

**[Use each technical term as one part of speech where possible](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md).** Use each technical term as one part of speech where possible. Words such as "sample", "estimate", and "model" can be either nouns or verbs, and using both roles in one sentence can force rereading. When that repetition hinders reading, replace the verb with an ordinary verb or separate the clauses.

**[Spell out acronyms and coin them sparingly](rules/conventions/acronyms.md).** Spell out an acronym where repeated use begins, and use the full term for isolated earlier mentions. Define the acronym separately in the abstract, in the body, and in each major section where it returns. Prefer the full term unless the acronym is standard or replaces a long, recurring name. Spell out any acronym that appears only once or twice, shares its letters with another common term in the field ("LR"), or saves no syllables when read aloud ("TS" for time step). Flag an unfamiliar acronym in the title and suggest a spelled-out alternative, but leave the title unchanged and define the acronym in the body. Flag opaque coined labels rather than renaming them. If an acronym you would expand also appears in tables, figures, or code, flag the change rather than making the prose inconsistent with them.

**[Spell each word one way throughout](rules/conventions/spelling.md).** Use US or UK spelling consistently throughout the text. When a word has two accepted forms, such as "dataset" and "data set", follow the venue's style or, if the venue specifies neither, use the draft's more frequent form.

**[Refresh long-lived shorthand](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md).** When shorthand returns after a long gap or section boundary, remind the reader of its established name and either its definition or where it was defined. Phrase the reminder as a recall rather than a new definition. When a generic label such as "the rate" could fit several quantities, replace it with the original full name. For a bare display number far from its display, add the draft's name for that display, as in "the energy balance (9)".

**[Clarify suggestive, overloaded, and suitcase terms](rules/conventions/suggestive-terms.md).** Clarify suggestive coinages (an "intelligent" coating), overloaded terms ("exponential" for merely rapid growth), and suitcase words with several meanings in the field ("stability") by stating which meaning applies. Flag an undefined coinage and suggest a literal alternative. Keep established technical usage ("the enzyme recognizes its substrate") and explain the operation at first use. Replace intentions or mental states attributed to a system ("the particle wants to minimize its energy") with the literal operation or the authors' design goal; if the draft states neither, flag the missing information. When an ordinary word collides with a nearby technical meaning ("a normal sample" in a paper about normal distributions), replace the ordinary word and keep the technical term. Follow [definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) for unfamiliar categories and competing definitions.

### Mathematics

**[Define each symbol by its first use and give it one meaning](rules/conventions/symbol-definitions.md).** Define every symbol, at least informally, at or before its first use, and [refresh the definition after a gap](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md). Give each symbol one meaning and each object one symbol. Flag conflicting meanings, unused definitions, easily confused names, or dense notation; do not rename symbols yourself. Distinguish a function from its value: if $V$ is a potential, flag "$V = 0.31$" when "$V(x_0) = 0.31$" is meant. Keep local definitions near where they are used. When global definitions are scattered, move them into an existing notation section only if the request [allows relocation](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md); otherwise flag them, and never create a new notation section. For unnecessary indices, repeated expressions, and verbal alternatives, see the page.

**[Write mathematics as grammatical prose](rules/conventions/math-prose.md).** Read each formula as part of the sentence so that the prose and mathematics together are grammatical. Begin sentences and clauses with words, not bare symbols, and spell out sentence-initial numbers. Separate adjacent formulas with words, including across sentence boundaries. In prose, write quantifiers and relations as words ("for all", not ∀; "is at most 0.1", not "is ≤ 0.1"), except in work on logic. Punctuate a displayed formula with a period when it ends a sentence and a comma when the sentence continues; use a colon before a display only after a complete clause. A trailing "where" clause may define symbols, but any definition needed to understand a claim must precede the claim. For LaTeX paragraph breaks, see the page.

**[Explain why formal material is needed](rules/conventions/formal-material-purpose.md).** Flag a central equation or definition whose purpose the text never states; do not infer a purpose that the draft does not give. If the explanation appears later in the same paragraph or section, move it before the equation or definition; if the explanation appears only in another section, flag the move instead. Follow [formal support](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md) when the claimed application depends on unstated conditions.

**[Set operators, subscripts, and signs in the correct type](rules/conventions/math-typography.md).** Set multi-letter operators and word subscripts upright, while keeping variable subscripts italic. Use × for multiplication and multiplicative factors ("3× the yield"), not x or *, and use a minus sign for negative values. Fix LaTeX typography when the mathematical meaning stays unchanged. Flag, rather than make, changes to symbols, grouping, delimiters, or operator meaning, and preserve code syntax. For ambiguous slash fractions, tiny exponent fractions, oversized delimiters, and LaTeX commands, see the page.

**[Number referenced and central displays](rules/conventions/display-numbering.md).** Number every display referred to in the text and every central display, such as a governing equation, the objective, or the main bound. Leave intermediate steps unnumbered.

**[Make theorem statements self-contained](rules/conventions/theorem-statements.md).** Introduce a theorem, lemma, or algorithm with a complete sentence, using a colon only after a complete clause. Make the formal statement self-contained: state the type of each object ("the linear operator A") and define every symbol before use. Keep motivation and informal commentary outside the statement. When the hypotheses run for several lines, flag the statement and propose extracting them into named conditions defined before it ("Assumption 1 (smoothness)").

**[Open each proof with its strategy and mark its progress](rules/conventions/proof-strategy.md).** Open each proof with its strategy and mark subgoals as they are completed. Make clear whether each statement is assumed, defined, established, or deferred to a later proof. Add a strategy or progress statement only when the draft makes it identifiable. For "clearly" and "it is easy to see", follow [intensifiers](rules/prose/intensifiers.md). For matching case order and separating a construction from its use, see the page.

### Citations, figures, and tables

**[Keep citation markers out of subject and object positions](rules/conventions/citation-markers.md).** A bracketed or parenthetical citation marker is not a noun. Replace "[8] introduces" with a textual citation (`\citet{key}` in LaTeX), keeping the key. For an established result that the paper asserts, shorten "In a recent study, ... showed that X" to "X (citation)"; keep the cited authors as the subject when the sentence reports, characterizes, or contrasts a claim that the paper does not itself assert, following [prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md). When the draft shows which source supports which clause, place each citation beside its clause; otherwise flag a trailing citation cluster that leaves unclear which source supports which claim. Flag any passage where the draft leaves unclear how far a source's material extends. For citation commands, author-name formatting, and attribution across sentences, see the page.

**[Flag missing or unresolvable citations](rules/conventions/missing-citations.md).** Flag a technical claim presented as prior knowledge ("It is well known that") without an identifiable source, or a citation key missing from the supplied references. Common knowledge needs no citation, and you should not delete an existing citation merely because it seems unnecessary. Preserve explicit secondhand wording such as "as cited in" rather than implying that the authors consulted the original source directly. Follow [vague attribution](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md) for "Studies show" and [reference markers](rules/conventions/figure-table-references.md) for "??".

**[Write captions that stand alone and state the finding](rules/conventions/captions.md).** A caption that only names its figure or table is incomplete. Complete it from the paragraph that cites it, and flag needed facts that appear nowhere in the draft. Identify quantities, units, axes, conditions, visual encodings such as colors and line styles, and [error bars](rules/claims/variability.md); state whether higher or lower is better when that is not obvious. State the supported finding and check that it agrees with the body text. Name compared methods or conditions instead of identifying them only by color. In tables, flag unexplained blank or dash cells and columns with mixed or missing units. When the plot source or caption gives axis ranges, a second vertical axis, or the range of a fitted curve, flag any claim read from visual distances or slopes that those scales do not support, and flag any extrapolated value described as measured. An axis zoomed to part of its range is not by itself an error.

**[Cite each figure and table where its claim is stated](rules/conventions/figure-table-references.md).** Cite each figure or table in the sentence stating its claim, rather than writing "In Figure 6 we show the results". Point to the specific panel or comparison that the draft identifies, and use numbered references instead of relative pointers such as "below". If the claim requires an exact value that a reader could only estimate from a plot, state the value from the draft or flag that it is missing. Flag unresolved citation or reference markers such as "??". For LaTeX float commands and capitalization, see the page.

### Typography

**[Hyphenate compound modifiers before nouns](rules/conventions/hyphens.md).** Hyphenate compound modifiers before nouns ("real-time monitoring", "three-layer coating"). Leave the same phrase unhyphenated when it follows the noun ("monitoring runs in real time"). Preserve an established field term without hyphens when the draft consistently writes it that way ("a high energy physics experiment").

**[Spell out small counts and use numerals for measurements](rules/conventions/number-forms.md).** Spell out small counts used as adjectives in prose ("two passes"). Keep numerals for measurements, parameter settings even when small ("5 cycles", "3 iterations"), quantities with units, mathematical values, and numbered names. Follow the draft's consistent cutoff for spelling out counts unless the venue sets one. Write compared magnitudes in the same form ("1.5M and 0.2M", not "1.5M and 200,000"); flag any change that requires rounding or unit conversion.

**[Use "i.e.", "e.g.", and "cf." for their meanings](rules/conventions/latin-abbreviations.md).** Use "i.e." to mean "that is" and "e.g." to mean "for example"; never interchange them. In US style, follow both with a comma, and do not italicize either. Use "cf." to mean "compare", not "see".

**[Set off non-restrictive clauses with commas](rules/conventions/nonrestrictive-commas.md).** Use commas around a non-restrictive clause, which adds information rather than identifying which objects are meant. Preserve the meaning of a restrictive clause: do not add commas merely because it begins with "which", and change restrictive "which" to "that" only when the venue requires it. Do not put commas around a symbol that names the preceding noun ("a directed graph G with source s").

**[Use punctuation and type only for their formal functions](rules/conventions/punctuation-typography.md).** Avoid contractions, exclamation marks, and emoji. Use title-case headings only when the venue requires them. Use italics for definitions, not for decorative emphasis. When italics mark what a negation denies ("we do not heat the *lid*"), express that contrast in words if the intended focus is clear; otherwise keep the italics and flag the ambiguity. Use quotation marks for quotations, mentioned words, and unendorsed terms. Keep quotation marks on unendorsed terms (the cell's "decision"), and flag the term if the draft never states what it refers to. Do not add scare quotes to ordinary words. Keep sentence punctuation outside exact literals, such as commands and outputs, unless it belongs to the literal, and preserve literal characters.

**[Give units, separated from numbers by a non-breaking space](rules/conventions/units.md).** Give units for all measurements and axes, separated from the number by a non-breaking space. When a unit or its basis is ambiguous ("ppm" by mass or by volume; "Mb" as megabits or megabytes), state the intended unit and basis from the draft, or flag the ambiguity. For ambiguous binary or decimal prefixes and time formats, see the page; never change a value to normalize units.

**[Preserve LaTeX boundaries when reflowing prose](rules/conventions/latex-boundaries.md).** When reflowing LaTeX source, compare it with the original: keep paragraph breaks (blank lines) where they were, and keep each `%` comment hiding the same text as before. Joining a prose line onto the end of a comment line hides that prose; breaking a comment line can expose the rest of the comment as text. Undo any boundary changes introduced by reflow.

## Deslopping

Divide recognizable patterns of AI-written prose into two tiers:

1. **Blacklisted patterns.** Delete every instance; the threshold for tolerable patterns below does not apply.
2. **Tolerable patterns.** Treat all other patterns in this section as tolerable. Edit a single instance when doing so improves the sentence itself, and rewrite a whole passage only when it contains three or more instances of tolerable patterns.

**[Preserve the author's voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).** Keep the draft's [varied rhythm](rules/deslopping/repetitive-syntax.md) and the stance that its authors genuinely express. Never add stance, emphasis, sentence fragments, opposing positions, or details that the draft lacks; after rewriting for style, [verify that the meaning is unchanged](rules/prose/deletion-test.md). Follow the conventions on [voice](rules/conventions/active-voice.md), [tense](rules/conventions/tense.md), and [terminology](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md): first-person "we", present tense for the text and prior work, and repeated technical terms are not defects merely because generated prose also uses them. Finally, [match confidence to evidence](rules/claims/scope.md) without adding stance.

### Blacklisted patterns

**[Delete em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md).** Delete every em dash, including `---` in LaTeX, and every en dash used as a pause. Replace the dash with a colon or semicolon only when the [pause-punctuation test](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) allows it, and use parentheses only when they do not make an essential qualification look optional under [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md); otherwise, rewrite the sentence without the pause while preserving its emphasis. Keep hyphens in compound words and en dashes in numeric ranges or two-person names ("Sections 3–5", "Kullback–Leibler"). For `--` in LaTeX source, see Details.

**[Delete chatbot artifacts](rules/deslopping/chatbot-phrases.md).** Delete conversational wording left by an assistant, such as "Certainly!", "Great question", "I hope this helps", or "As of my last update".

**[Delete generic openings and closings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md).** Delete opening or closing filler that could fit any paper in the field ("In the ever-evolving field of materials science", "The future looks bright"), including popularity claims that sound factual ("There has been much recent interest in X"). Merely naming the topic does not make a sentence necessary. If the draft gives a specific reason the topic matters, use that reason instead; if readers need prerequisite context first, follow the rule on [brief setup](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md).

**[Remove leaked generation artifacts and flag the gaps](rules/deslopping/generation-artifacts.md).** Remove citation tokens leaked from chat interfaces, such as `citeturn0search0` and `contentReference[oaicite:...]`, along with unfilled placeholders such as "[Insert citation]". Keep the surrounding claim and flag the missing information. When a URL contains the AI-referrer parameter `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, leave the URL unchanged in the text and flag the parameter for removal.

### Tolerable patterns

**[Apply prose rules to ornate words, nominalizations, counts, and lists](rules/deslopping/prose-rule-patterns.md).** Apply the prose rules to [ornate wording](rules/prose/literal-wording.md) (such as "delve", "underscore", "showcase", and "pivotal"), clauses with [several nominalizations](rules/prose/character-action.md), and [counts that defer their items](rules/prose/direct-content.md). For arguments formatted as bullet lists, apply [list structure](rules/prose/list-structure.md). Follow each linked rule's exceptions, and keep a precise technical word, a useful nominalization, or a count whose items follow immediately.

**[Expand compressed constructions that hinder the first reading](rules/deslopping/compressed-constructions.md).** Check for [inanimate subjects given human actions ("the model hopes")](rules/prose/character-action.md), [nouns used as verbs and unusual word pairs](rules/prose/literal-wording.md), and [noun stacks, omitted words such as "that" or an article, and long gaps between subject and verb](rules/prose/first-reading.md), following the diagnostics and exceptions on each linked page. Expand only constructions that hinder the first reading; preserve clear short forms and established technical terms.

**[Repair pause punctuation between a claim and its evidence](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md).** Check every colon or semicolon, not only one that replaced an em dash, for a vague claim followed by its evidence ("Annealing helps: conductivity rises by 40%"). Read the first clause alone: if it states no specific fact, either connect the claim to its evidence in words ("as shown by ...") or delete the vague clause when the evidence already states the whole claim. Simply replacing the colon or semicolon with a period does not fix the vague clause. When the first clause states a specific fact, keep a semicolon before a related fact or a colon before an explanation or example. A colon may also introduce a list, display (following [mathematical punctuation](rules/conventions/math-prose.md)), quoted definition, or label without a prior factual assertion, as long as it does not pair a vague claim with its evidence. No colon may imply a cause or an exhaustive list that the draft does not support. Also restore a missing verb in a trailing comma phrase ("adds 3 ms, below the limit" becomes "adds 3 ms, which is below the limit"), while keeping appositives, clear participial phrases, and parallel phrases that share an earlier verb.

**[Replace reasoning-chain leakage with conclusions and evidence](rules/deslopping/reasoning-leakage.md).** Replace "Let us think step by step", "Breaking this down", and numbered internal monologues with the conclusion and evidence. Preserve mathematical setup such as "Let X be", proof steps, and substantive reasoning that justifies the result.

**[Describe the work without narrating manuscript revisions](rules/deslopping/revision-narration.md).** In the paper, replace accounts of manuscript revisions (diff-anchored prose), such as "In this revised version, we clarify" or "As suggested by the reviewer", with the resulting scientific content; revision history belongs in the response letter or change log. Preserve the explanation, evidence, and justification, including material added during review, and keep a substantive comparison between versions of a method when that comparison is part of the study.

**[Name the source of a vague attribution](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md).** Replace a vague attribution such as "studies show", "experts believe", or "X has been shown to" with its named source only when the draft cites one for that claim, usually in the same or an adjacent sentence; otherwise, flag the missing attribution. An explicitly hypothetical objection that the paper poses and answers needs no citation merely to identify an objector, though "Some may argue" alone does not mark an objection as explicitly hypothetical. Follow the [prior-work rules](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md) to keep clear who holds each position.

**[Replace negative parallelism with the positive claim](rules/deslopping/negative-parallelism.md).** Replace constructions such as "not only … but also" and "It is not just X, it is Y", including those that span two sentences ("The headline is not the speedup. The real story is ..."), with the positive claim when the alternative that they reject is invented for emphasis. Keep contrasts that exclude a real alternative ("we measure X, not Y"). State the supported result and its measurements directly.

**[Name both sides of a false concession](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md).** When a concession such as "While X has advanced, Y remains a challenge" makes no concrete comparison, replace each vague side with the specific finding reported in the draft, and flag any side that the draft leaves unspecified. Keep concessions in which both sides are specific and supported.

**[Revise reflexive rule-of-three triads](rules/deslopping/rule-of-three.md).** Revise a three-part list such as "faster, cheaper, and more robust" when it is assembled for rhythm rather than to state three distinct, supported items. Keep a list whose subject calls for three items. Do not drop a substantive item merely to change the count; remove only empty or redundant wording, and flag unsupported attributes.

**[Replace false ranges with the settings tested](rules/deslopping/false-ranges.md).** When the endpoints of "from X to Y" do not lie on an ordered scale, the phrase suggests breadth without defining a range; replace it with the tested settings that the draft names. Keep true ranges along an ordered scale, and do not shorten a longer stated list to its endpoints.

**[Delete transition chains that announce more text](rules/deslopping/transition-chains.md).** Delete repeated openings such as "Moreover", "Furthermore", and "Additionally" when they merely announce more text. Keep transitions that express a [valid logical relation](rules/prose/transitions.md), and do not replace an additive sequence with an unsupported causal, temporal, or contrastive connection.

**[Replace copula avoidance with "is", "has", or action verbs](rules/deslopping/copula-avoidance.md).** Replace phrases such as "serves as", "acts as", "represents", or "boasts" with "is" or "has" when the meaning stays the same, or turn the following noun into a verb when it names an action ("serves as a temperature monitor" becomes "monitors temperature"). Keep these verbs when they express a role distinct from identity, such as a compound acting as a catalyst, or when they have a precise technical meaning.

**[Delete or replace trailing "-ing" pseudo-analysis](rules/deslopping/trailing-ing.md).** Delete trailing participial commentary such as "highlighting its importance" or "reflecting broader trends" when it adds no checkable claim, or replace it with a specific claim the draft supports. Keep substantive participial clauses, and clarify them under the [first-reading rule](rules/prose/first-reading.md) when their attachment or meaning is ambiguous.

**[Make importance clear without self-labeling significance](rules/deslopping/self-labeling.md).** Delete a sentence that merely labels an earlier result important or surprising ("This is the key result"). When the draft explains why the result matters, use that explanation to organize the passage; otherwise, delete the empty label without adding an explanation. Keep a word of genuine authorial stance that introduces a claim ("Interestingly,").

**[Replace rhetorical questions used as transitions with their answers](rules/deslopping/rhetorical-questions.md).** Replace a question used as a transition ("How well does this scale?") with its answer as the topic sentence; a question that only connects paragraphs is not a permitted opening, even if the next sentence answers it. Keep a research question that states what the text tests, and place its answer as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md) directs. Keep a mid-paragraph question only when it reframes details already given and the next sentence answers it. For a leading question that presumes agreement, apply the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) to its implied claim.

**[Vary repetitive syntax through the connections between ideas](rules/deslopping/repetitive-syntax.md).** Look for successive sentences with the same opening, clause pattern, or length, and for paragraphs built the same way. Following the [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md), connect sentences through relations already stated in the draft: a shared object, condition, time, or consequence. Let those relations determine sentence structure instead of changing lengths at random, and do not invent causal, temporal, or contrastive links. Preserve [parallel wording for parallel ideas](rules/prose/parallel-form.md) and [consistent technical terms](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md). Keep sentences complete and literal, without adding [writerly asides](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md), contrasts, or emphasis, and do not vary a passage in ways that reduce clarity.

**[Replace significance inflation with the supported implication](rules/deslopping/significance-inflation.md).** Delete grand-trend commentary such as "marking a pivotal moment" or "paving the way" when it adds no substantive claim, or replace it with the draft's specific implication and scope. If the passage asserts a substantive but unsupported implication, keep that claim and flag the missing support.

**[Replace aphorism formulas with the mechanism](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md).** Replace a slogan-like analogy ("X is the Y of Z") with the mechanism that the draft states, and delete an empty slogan that makes no substantive claim. If the analogy asserts a substantive mechanism that the draft does not explain, keep the analogy and flag the missing explanation.

**[Present successes and failures with comparable prominence](rules/deslopping/relentless-positivity.md).** When the text frames every result as a success (relentless positivity or sycophantic framing), give reported failures and counterevidence comparable prominence and [calibrate each claim to its evidence](rules/claims/scope.md). Do not add a failure or balancing caveat, or hedge a supported finding, merely to make the tone seem cautious.
