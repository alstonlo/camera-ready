# Writing guide

This guide is generated from a collection of writing rules. Each rule has its own page in the `rules/` folder next to this guide and follows this format:

```markdown
# {{ Name of the rule }}

{{ A summary of the rule: what to look for, the edit to make or problem to flag, and the exceptions that change the edit. }}

## Details

{{ Explanations, further cases, and procedures left out of the summary, or the sentence "This rule has no further details." }}

## Examples

**Edit 1.** {{ Optional context for the example. Then what the edit changes and why, in one or two sentences. }}

> **Before.** {{ A passage that breaks the rule. }}
>
> **After.** {{ The same passage, corrected under this rule alone. }}
```

The guide below reproduces each rule's heading and summary, and each rule page adds `Details` and at least one example. Read the full page when a rule applies to your text but the summary does not show which edit to make, or when the summary refers to a case on the page. Each example shows one edit that its rule calls for. No example permits a change beyond what the user asked for.

## Table of contents

The guide has six sections of rules:

- [Story](#story): the argument from problem to implications, titles and headings, section order and openings, contributions, related work, results by question, and numbers that support claims.
- [Claims](#claims): reporting verbs matched to evidence, scope, overclaiming and underclaiming, consistency across the text, causal language, what an efficiency claim counts, statistical significance, hedges, speculation, and limitations.
- [Cutting](#cutting): what you may delete or move without asking the author, the triage order under a length limit, sentences to protect, and how to repair text around a cut.
- [Prose](#prose): explanation order, paragraphs, information flow from familiar to new, sentence structure, direct content instead of promises, concrete quantities, and plain wording.
- [Conventions](#conventions): voice, tense, terminology, mathematics, citations and credit, figures and tables, typography, and LaTeX source.
- [Deslopping](#deslopping): blacklisted and tolerable patterns common in AI-written prose.

For a request narrower than a full polish, apply the sections it names and every rule they link to. Check each chunk of text against every rule in each applicable section. When two rules call for different edits to a sentence and one links to the other, follow the linked rule. Otherwise, choose the clearer sentence, or use your judgment as an expert editor if both are equally clear.

## Story

Organize the text around one five-part argument: a problem the reader cares about; a gap in what is known or possible, with the cost of leaving it open; an idea that fills the gap; evidence that the idea works; and what the findings imply.

Check this argument across the whole text and within each section before editing individual sentences, because sentence edits cannot fix a missing or illogical step. Use the [rules on claims](#claims) to match each claim to its evidence, the [rules on cutting](#cutting) to decide what stays, and the [rules on prose](#prose) to edit paragraphs and sentences.

Without a separate request from the author, you may:

- Reorder sentences in a paragraph or paragraphs in a section to put the claim first.
- Rewrite a section's opening using content already in that section.
- Remove numbers from running text when a table already gives them.
- Rewrite a caption to state a conclusion already drawn in the body.

Flag any change to the claims, section order, or list of contributions instead of making it yourself. Never add a cost, explanation, or number absent from the draft.

### The whole argument

**[Summarize the argument in five lines](rules/story/five-line-summary.md).** Before editing, summarize the draft in five lines: the problem, the gap and its cost, the idea, the evidence, and the implication. Combine the idea and evidence lines into the core claims for the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md). Flag any part that the draft omits.

**[Summarize the argument in one sentence](rules/story/one-sentence-summary.md).** Combine the five-line summary into one sentence: "Because the problem matters and existing work leaves a gap, we propose an idea that our result supports." If it names no gap, flag the missing problem. If it requires unrelated problems, flag competing focuses without choosing one or inventing a link.

**[Match each section to its part of the argument](rules/story/section-functions.md).** Check that each section does its part of the argument. The introduction states the problem, gap, idea, and contributions. Background gives only the context needed for the gap and comparisons. Methods explains the idea, Results gives the evidence, and Discussion interprets the evidence, limitations, and alternatives. The conclusion states the implications. Keep combined or repeated sections if the argument stays clear, and follow venue rules. Each result must follow the method description it needs (when Methods comes last, see Details). State the contributions in the first quarter of the text. If a central result first appears later or sections need reordering, flag the problem instead of moving sections.

The abstract states all five parts of the argument within the venue's word limit, using recognizable terms for the problem, system, and technique. Readers must understand it without the body or bibliography. Remove a reference to a section or figure only when the abstract already states that result. Flag a missing part or result, or needed content that exceeds the limit. For proposals, surveys, and abstract citations, see Details.

**[Keep the argument linear](rules/story/linear-argument.md).** Order material by the argument's logic, not by when each part was done or how much effort it took. Apply the [rule against alternation](rules/prose/topic-order.md) across sections: do not return to an earlier topic after leaving it (A, B, A). The core idea may appear in every section. Keep a second round of methods and results that depends on an earlier result ([section functions](rules/story/section-functions.md)), reminders of needed definitions, and protected restatements of contributions. Give each component and counterargument space proportional to its role in the core claims. Follow the [rule on comparing with prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md) when explaining each side of a comparison. Under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md), keep a rejected approach in the main text only as a negative result, and state its flaw before its details. Flag moves of material between sections or changes in section order.

**[Test the argument by skimming](rules/story/skim-test.md).** Check that the abstract, headings, first paragraph of each section, and [captions](rules/conventions/captions.md) alone state all five parts of the argument. When the request allows, restore any missing part or connection from the draft. Flag missing content and any repair that changes a claim or the section order.

**[Flag titles that are unclear or overstate the contribution](rules/story/titles.md).** Flag a title that hides the subject, has two reasonable readings, overstates the contribution, or uses an acronym that readers outside the subfield would not know ([acronym rule](rules/conventions/acronyms.md)). Suggest a revision that keeps the body's claim strength and conditions, leave the title unchanged, and follow venue requirements.

### Problem, gap, and contribution

**[Open with the problem](rules/story/open-with-problem.md).** Open by naming the problem the paper addresses. If readers need context first, apply the [brief-setup rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md). Delete any [generic opening](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md). If the draft never says why the problem matters, flag the missing motivation instead of inventing an example. For a mismatched opening, see Details.

**[State the gap's cost](rules/story/gap-cost.md).** State the specific gap in what is known or can be done, and state its cost: what readers lose, pay, cannot do, or cannot understand because of it. Replace "little is known" with the specific unknown when the draft supplies it. Flag a missing gap or cost. If stating the gap requires rewriting the objectives or contributions, flag the proposed rewrite instead of making it. Do not invent a flaw in prior work or a hypothesis to serve as the cost (see Details). Distinguish established consequences from [speculative applications](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Explain why the problem is hard](rules/story/why-hard.md).** Explain why the obvious way to solve the problem fails, so readers can see why the authors chose their approach. Flag a missing explanation.

**[State each contribution as a claim and name its supporting section](rules/story/contribution-claims.md).** For each contribution, state a checkable claim and name its supporting section. If a contribution states only an activity, replace it with a claim under the [underclaiming rule](rules/claims/underclaiming.md) and match its strength to the evidence with the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md). Flag contributions without evidence. Keep a major result that supports no contribution, and apply the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md) to it (see Details). When contributions name their sections, delete any roadmap that states no content under the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md).

### Section openings and connections

**[Open every section with its question and answer](rules/story/section-openings.md).** State each section's question and finding in its first paragraph, using only content from that section. Openings that only name the topic are [signposts](rules/prose/signposts.md).

**[Connect each section to the preceding text](rules/story/section-connections.md).** Connect each section's question to the preceding text, following [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). Make the introduction understandable without the abstract. Keep short reminders for readers who start at Results, Discussion, or Conclusion. Open a methods section with the key idea before the steps. Put an existing [explanatory example](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md) before the general case.

**[Use headings to show how the argument is organized](rules/story/headings.md).** Make each parent heading describe all its subsections, and give headings under the same parent [parallel form](rules/prose/parallel-form.md). When it makes the argument hard to follow, flag a parent with only one subheading, a fourth-level heading, or a very short section. When a heading could state its section's finding, flag it and propose one that keeps the body's claim strength and conditions. Also flag and propose any change to how sections are grouped or nested. Do not make either change yourself. Keep distinctions that help readers, and follow venue requirements.

### Related work

**[Compare prior work fairly and state specific differences](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md).** End each paragraph or group of paragraphs about prior work with a checkable difference in an assumption, condition, scope, metric, measured result, cost, or guarantee. A comparison that is vague on both sides is a [false concession](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md). Use only the draft's account of each source. Flag missing context or differences instead of supplying them. Explain both sides enough for readers to judge the comparison. State what each source contributes and assumes before criticizing it, and preserve its [credit](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md). Do not write "ignores" or "fails to" for a difference in scope unless the draft shows that the source failed. Keep a source's tentative verbs. Attribute each claim, concession, and reported objection to whoever holds it, not to the paper. For surveys, see Details.

**[State what is new and what comes from earlier work](rules/story/novelty.md).** Distinguish what the authors contribute from standard components, borrowed ideas, and their earlier results. If the work extends a preliminary publication, state what the authors added. Keep wording that identifies their earlier work, or flag it if the venue requires anonymity. Do not present their earlier work as independent confirmation. Flag any unclear contribution or credit instead of assigning it.

**[Flag comparisons with prior work that readers cannot yet understand](rules/story/prior-work-placement.md).** Detailed comparisons with prior work may appear in any section. Leave each comparison in place, and flag it only if it appears before material that readers need to understand it. Do not flag background that readers need to understand the problem. For what to say in a comparison, follow the [rule on comparing with prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md).

### Results

**[Organize results around research questions](rules/story/results-by-question.md).** State which questions the Results section answers and connect each to a contribution. If a heading names only a dataset, sample, site, or setup, flag it and propose one that states a question or finding. If one result motivates the next experiment, keep that sequence and keep the interpretation beside the result. Distinguish [measurement from inference](rules/claims/hedges.md). Flag an experiment for cutting if it answers no identifiable question, and flag any proposed section move or reordering. Also flag a result with no described procedure or an evaluation procedure with no reported use, and delete neither.

**[Answer one question per results paragraph](rules/story/one-question-per-paragraph.md).** Give each results paragraph one question and its answer as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md) directs, then give the evidence. Split a paragraph that answers two independent questions, and flag one that answers none.

**[Explain why the evidence supports the claim](rules/story/explain-evidence.md).** When readers outside the subfield may not see why a measurement or reason supports a claim, explain the reasoning that connects them. Check that the assumptions behind that reasoning hold in this study. Use only support from the draft. If the draft omits the connection, flag the gap instead of inventing a reason. Follow [scope](rules/claims/scope.md) for claims that exceed what the measurement establishes, and [formal support](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md) for conclusions from a theorem.

**[Identify which part produces the effect](rules/story/component-effects.md).** When a method, treatment, intervention, material, or design combines several parts or changes, the results must show which part produces the reported effect. If no evidence separates one part's effect, flag the missing evidence and credit only the combination under [causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md). Treat an untested explanation as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md). Flag any obvious alternative explanation that the draft does not address. If the draft raises a counterexample, conflicting result, or alternative explanation, check its reply. Flag the reply if it gives neither evidence nor a reason why the objection does not apply.

### Numbers

The most common flaw in the argument of AI-generated text is listing results before stating what they show, or never stating the point. Reorder the text so that every number supports a claim already stated in words.

**[State the claim before the number](rules/story/claim-before-number.md).** State the finding in words before its supporting number, and refer to the table for the remaining values. Check which claim each number supports. Flag any number without an identifiable claim. Move it only if the request allows relocation and a suitable place exists. When a table gives a number, remove it from the text only under the [headline-number rule](rules/story/headline-number.md), keeping needed comparisons and uncertainty.

**[Choose a headline number for each claim](rules/story/headline-number.md).** Choose a headline number for each claim, not an unrepresentative best case. Keep its baseline and any interval or range needed to interpret it. Describe the pattern the results support, including where it fails. Refer to an existing table for the remaining values. Remove numbers that repeat the table only if the text keeps the comparison, [uncertainty](rules/claims/variability.md), and [statistical conclusion](rules/claims/statistical-significance.md).

**[Match precision to the claim](rules/story/number-precision.md).** Report only the digits that the measurement and comparison support. Keep words that mark estimates or approximations. Flag unsupported precision or comparable quantities reported with different precision. Never add digits, round values, or replace numbers without the author's authorization and the source data.

### Discussion and conclusion

**[Explain how the results address the gap](rules/story/return-to-gap.md).** In the discussion's first paragraph, explain how the results address the introduction's gap. Then state the limitations under the [rule on limitations](rules/claims/limitations.md). If the introduction promises more than the results establish, flag the mismatch under the [rule on scope](rules/claims/scope.md).

**[Use the conclusion to explain what the findings mean](rules/story/conclusion-perspective.md).** In the conclusion, keep one statement of each contribution under the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md). Connect these statements, then state what the established findings let readers understand or do. A conclusion may be understandable without reading the paper, as long as it explains what the findings mean. Flag a conclusion that instead repeats general statements, merely repeats the abstract, gives new supporting evidence, or introduces a new subject. Future work may state a specific question that the findings raise and what answering it would establish, but do not invent a research plan. Preserve the findings' scope and [limitations](rules/claims/limitations.md), and label [speculative implications](rules/claims/speculation.md). When shortening restated contributions, repair the connections between them. Do not join copied sentences that depend on omitted context.

## Claims

Every claim must match its evidence. Claiming either more or less than the evidence supports is an error. An unreached goal may motivate the work if the text states that the goal remains unreached.

### Strength and scope

**[Choose a reporting verb from the verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md).** Choose each reporting verb from the verb ladder to match the evidence, regardless of enthusiasm for the result.

| Verb | Required support |
|---|---|
| **prove** | only a theorem with stated assumptions |
| **show / demonstrate / establish** | direct evidence that rules out plausible alternatives |
| **find / observe / report** | a directly measured empirical result |
| **suggest / indicate**; **hypothesize / conjecture** | an inference or interpretation that could reasonably be wrong |

An empirical test does not prove a mechanism. A figure or table shows only what it directly displays. When the text interprets a figure or table, match the verb to the evidence. Replace *reveal* or *confirm* with a weaker verb unless the evidence establishes the conclusion. Flag a claimed disproof supported only by an objection or an inconclusive test. When reporting cited work, preserve whether the source treats a result as central or incidental and whether it accepts or doubts the result (see Details and [prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md)).

Keep any Results hedge, such as "suggests", in the abstract, introduction, conclusion, and other summaries. Trace each summary claim to its supporting result, and flag any unsupported claim. When the body supports a summary claim, add any missing section, table, or figure reference. In the abstract, state the result itself instead of citing the body.

**[Match scope and confidence to the evidence](rules/claims/scope.md).** Make each claim as broad and as confident as the evidence supports, without [underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md). Keep every claim, especially a headline claim, within the conditions, datasets, and settings that its evidence covers. When support is uncertain, leave the claim unchanged and flag it. An unqualified plural noun, as in "Solar cells degrade in humid air", makes a claim about the whole class. If the evidence covers only the tested cases, name them, as in "the four cell designs we test". Check whether words such as "always" or "unique" extend a claim to untested cases.

A single example, such as a microscope image, case report, or generated sample, shows only that a case occurs, not how often. Flag a broader claim from a single example or from sampled data unless the draft explains how the example or sample was selected and, for sampled data, justifies the target population (see Details). Keep any explicit label marking an example as hypothetical or constructed, and never add one by inference. Check [independent confirmation](rules/claims/variability.md) under its own rule.

Unless the draft supports the interpretation, flag a research question or conclusion that treats a score as the broader property it measures indirectly, or a ratio of scores as a proportional change in that property. Do not rewrite the question or add a caveat to fix either mismatch. Flag a superiority claim that lacks a relevant baseline. Flag a claim such as "ready for clinical use" or "ready for production" without evidence on costs and operating conditions beyond test gains. Keep applications that the draft explicitly marks as tentative.

See Details for tendencies and counterexamples, trends over time, probabilities from small samples, baseline choices, score differences, and words that assume an unstated fact, such as "the solution" or "restores accuracy".

**[Remove or flag unsupported labels on empirical claims](rules/claims/unsupported-labels.md).** On empirical claims, remove or flag labels such as *solves*, *optimal*, *guarantees*, and *first to* unless the draft's evidence supports the full claim. Reserve *proves* for theorems, as the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) requires. Flag unsupported superlatives, and flag any *state-of-the-art*, *record*, or *human-level* claim that omits the task or quantity, the compared methods or results, or the metric. Remove *novel* when it adds only praise. Beyond removing a label, rewrite the claim only when the draft's evidence determines its [scope](rules/claims/scope.md); otherwise, flag it. For uniqueness claims, see Details.

**[State supported contributions without underclaiming](rules/claims/underclaiming.md).** State supported contributions directly, without unneeded hedges (see Details). Each item in a list of contributions should state a claim that readers can check and cite its supporting section or result when the draft provides one. If an item says only what the authors *study*, *explore*, or *investigate*, replace it with the finding from the corresponding section, or flag the missing finding.

**[Flag conflicting claims and numbers](rules/claims/internal-consistency.md).** Compare claims about the same object across sections and lists, and check that totals, bounds, and trends obey the draft's numerical constraints. If a stated difference in setting or definition explains an apparent conflict, flag both passages and propose a revision that states the difference. Otherwise, flag both passages and the missing explanation. Flag conflicting numbers instead of choosing a value. Flag any claim that two options are the only possibilities when the draft names another option in that scope, and name that option.

### Causation and formal support

**[Reserve causal language for causal evidence](rules/claims/causal-language.md).** Use causal wording such as "causes", "leads to", or "improves because" only when the evidence isolates a factor's effect. Describe a correlation with "is associated with" or "correlates with". Keep cause and effect in the order the evidence supports, and do not call one of several causes the only cause. When the draft tests several changes only together, attribute the effect to their combination, not to one component, and do not call each component necessary. Flag unsupported causal claims, and mark an untested mechanism as [speculation](rules/claims/speculation.md).

**[Identify the kind of evidence supporting each claim](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md).** If a sentence reads like a theorem but gives no formal result or conditions, link it to the proved result and its assumptions, restate it as the observed or intuitive claim the draft supports, or flag it. If the draft applies a theorem to observations or experiments, flag the claim when the theorem's assumptions do not hold. An unmet assumption makes a theorem inapplicable, not false. Never drop a condition to simplify a formal statement. Instead, flag any condition that seems unnecessary (see Details). Distinguish proofs, calculations from a model, simulations, and measurements of the real system. Flag any claim that a model result holds in the real system without evidence. A valid claim limited to the model needs no experiment or field study.

**[Specify what an efficiency claim counts](rules/claims/efficiency-claims.md).** A claim about efficiency, speed, yield, cost, samples needed, or scaling must state what it counts and per what unit, its conditions and assumptions, and which costs it includes or excludes. A comparison must name what it compares against, and a ratio must define its numerator and denominator. An analytical bound must define each size variable and the case analyzed (see Details). Add any missing detail the draft states elsewhere; otherwise, flag it. A better ratio or asymptotic bound alone does not show lower total cost under the claimed conditions. Flag suspected errors in the analysis without changing the mathematics.

### Numerical comparisons

**[Report variability and compare like with like](rules/claims/variability.md).** For each numerical comparison, check that the draft states the number of runs, repetitions, or samples, how they were combined, what ± means, and what varied across runs. These details may appear beside the comparison or in a general statement; flag any that is missing. A best run is not comparable with a mean. Flag a comparison, including one with results from other papers, unless the draft establishes that both sides use equivalent tasks, outputs, quality requirements, aggregation, and procedures. When one measure improves and another worsens, state both changes. If the draft does not establish how the other measure compares, flag the missing comparison.

Check that the draft states what each percentage is of and whether a percentage change is relative or in percentage points. Edit the wording when the draft makes the meaning clear; otherwise, flag the ambiguity without guessing. Name the measure behind each ranking, and flag selective reporting.

Data used to select or tune a method cannot independently test it. Flag a claim of independent confirmation from reused data, related datasets, or correlated measures unless the draft establishes a separate test. For misleading averages, systematic measurement errors (including biased timings), and convergence error, see Details.

**[Draw only the conclusions a statistical test supports](rules/claims/statistical-significance.md).** Use *significant* only for a named statistical test, reported with its p-value or significance level and any effect size in the draft; flag any that is missing. When *significant* means large or important, replace it with the reported magnitude. Keep available effect and uncertainty estimates even when the result is not significant. A p-value is the probability of observing results at least as extreme as those measured if the null hypothesis and test assumptions hold, not the probability that a hypothesis is true. Preserve the tested hypothesis and the test's direction. Failing to reject the null hypothesis does not establish equality, and statistical significance does not establish practical importance. Interval overlap alone does not show whether one result is better; flag any such claim made without a test. When the draft tests significance across several datasets, outcomes, or measures, flag a missing multiple-comparison correction. For p-value thresholds and statistical power, see Details.

### Qualification and limits

**[Hedge interpretations, not measurements or procedures](rules/claims/hedges.md).** Hedge tentative interpretations, including proposed mechanisms. State measurements directly and keep their reported uncertainty. State performed procedures directly, and hedge only the reason for performing them. Make a claim more or less confident only when the evidence supports the change. In every summary of a claim, keep the claim's hedge and its [verb-ladder strength](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md). Use at most one hedge per uncertain claim, in a standard form such as "may", "likely", or "suggests". When you remove a redundant hedge, keep the hedges on other uncertain claims and keep every [scope condition](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md). Replace vague softeners such as *somewhat* or *relatively* with the conditions or magnitudes that the draft supplies. If the draft supplies neither, keep the softener and flag the missing information.

**[Never state speculation as fact](rules/claims/speculation.md).** Do not state a proposed explanation or mechanism as fact without a supporting test or argument. Mark it as a conjecture ("We conjecture that") or put it in a passage explicitly labeled speculative. If an explanation relies on an undefined term or an unstated measurement, flag it instead of marking it as a conjecture. A claim that one property does not imply another requires a counterexample, a supporting result, or a reference. When the draft says a question is open, keep it open. Failure under the tested conditions does not show that success is impossible, so flag any unsupported negative conclusion.

**[State limitations precisely and explain their effects](rules/claims/limitations.md).** State each limitation fully, precisely, and without apology where it first affects a method or inference, and keep brief reminders that limit later claims. A limitation of an interpretation introduced in the Discussion may appear there first. Next to each limitation, keep the draft's explanation of how it affects the results and which conclusions still hold, or flag the missing explanation. Flag unexplained unexpected results, unjustified data exclusions, and decoy limitations (see Details). Every future-work statement must name the current limitation it addresses. Move a limitation between sections only under the [relocation rules](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md). Delete a repeated full statement only when a request to shorten the text reaches it in the [triage order](rules/cutting/triage-order.md).

## Cutting

Use the [spine test](rules/cutting/spine-test.md) to find the core claims and the paragraphs readers need to understand or evaluate them. Then check the request to see what you may delete or move.

### Deciding what to cut

**[Delete empty wording without asking](rules/cutting/bloat.md).** Delete [chatbot artifacts](rules/deslopping/chatbot-phrases.md), [generic openings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md), and [leaked artifacts](rules/deslopping/generation-artifacts.md), and repair [em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md), following each rule and its exceptions. Delete content announcements and signposts only when the [signpost rule](rules/prose/signposts.md) allows. Shorten remaining sentences without changing their meaning. These edits require no separate request, unlike [idea-level cuts](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md).

**[Cut or relocate ideas only on request](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md).** Delete or relocate substantive content only when the author asks to condense the text or sets a length limit. Otherwise, keep it and flag it for cutting or relocation. Substantive content is anything the author would miss: ideas, results, caveats, evidence, examples, reasons, and necessary transitions. A request to polish or tighten authorizes removing empty wording, not idea-level cuts. Reordering material within a section to [build understanding](rules/prose/explanation-order.md) is a prose edit; moving material between sections or out of the main text is relocation.

**[Apply the spine test before cutting sentences](rules/cutting/spine-test.md).** To apply the spine test, state the core claims in one to three sentences and summarize each paragraph's role in one line. Classify each paragraph as **central**, **supporting**, **ancillary**, or **off-spine** (see Details). Treat a paragraph that contains counterevidence or other [protected content](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md) as supporting if it is not central. If no short, specific title summarizes a section, flag its competing points before cutting. When [the request](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md) permits cutting, cut or relocate off-spine paragraphs, shorten ancillary ones, and then edit [sentence by sentence](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md). Keep a cut only if the argument remains at least as clear.

**[Follow the triage order to meet a length limit](rules/cutting/triage-order.md).** To meet a length limit, cut material in this triage order, preserving [protected content](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md) and following [relocation safeguards](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md):

1. **Repeated points.** Cut points repeated across the introduction, section openings, and conclusion, keeping the most informative statement (usually the one that explains the point). Preserve one statement of each contribution in each of the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, along with [shorthand reminders](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md). Keep a closing restatement when readers need the intervening explanation to understand the opening claim. If a restatement copies another passage word for word, as when a conclusion repeats the abstract, revise it to state established results or flag it. Apply the [deletion test](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).
2. **Remaining signposts.** Cut signposts left after removing empty wording, including the introduction's closing [roadmap](rules/prose/signposts.md). Keep references to evidence and proofs.
3. **Unrelated background and related work.** Cut background that does not motivate the research question and citation lists unrelated to the gap. Keep the few works and comparisons that define the gap.
4. **Minor results, secondary analyses, and exploratory findings.** Remove results from the main text if they do not support a central claim, and relocate sensitivity analyses and robustness checks needed for rigor. In the main text, keep any failure or counterevidence that alters a central claim, and state a failed approach as a negative result rather than narrating attempts.
5. **Full parameter lists and procedural details.** Keep [explicit procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md), essential steps, and conditions in the main text so readers can reproduce the method in outline. Relocate complete parameter lists, full parameter sweeps, and subsidiary details.

**[Protect sentences the argument depends on](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md).** Preserve the material below, moving it only when the [sentence-by-sentence steps](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md) allow:

- Keep hedges, qualifiers, scope conditions, limitations, supporting evidence, and counterevidence. Cut a repeated limitation only when the triage order reaches it and a full statement remains elsewhere. Because removing a caveat changes a [claim's scope](rules/claims/scope.md), [recalibrate the claim](rules/cutting/post-cut-check.md) after an authorized cut only as remaining evidence requires.
- Keep the reasons for choices in the method and the details needed to reproduce the work (see Details).
- Keep definitions, topic sentences, necessary framing, [high-level summaries](rules/prose/direct-content.md), [logical transitions](rules/prose/transitions.md), and references back to earlier sections that later text relies on. Keep [standalone caption content](rules/conventions/captions.md) even when it repeats the body (see Details).
- Keep fair credit to prior work and accurate descriptions of it. Preserve acknowledgments, funding statements, disclosures of assistance (including help from AI tools), and disclosures of financial or other relationships that could bias the work.

### Making the cut

**[Repair text that depends on a sentence you cut or move](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md).** For each sentence within [the request's scope](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md), follow four steps:

1. **Check whether it is needed.** Apply "Protect sentences the argument depends on" before deleting a sentence that a core claim or the reader needs.
2. **Check what depends on it.** Check for a later reference such as "this" or "as shown above", a term defined or source cited only here, a hedge or evidence for another claim, or a break in the paragraph's topic string. Repair every dependent passage in the same edit, or keep the sentence.
3. **Choose whether to delete or move it.** Delete only content that no reader needs. Move content that only some readers or a skeptical reviewer will need, and never delete details needed to reproduce the work. Move material to an appendix or supplement only if the draft already has one. Otherwise, keep it in place and flag what to move. Make moved material understandable on its own, and point to it from the main text. Keep enough of the method and the reasons for choosing it in the main text for readers to judge the results. Keep essential steps and definitions used later in the main explanation (see Details for footnotes and parentheses).
4. **Read across the gap.** Check that the sentence after the cut begins with something the remaining text has already introduced. Restore any missing connection from the draft, or keep the original sentence.

**[Revise cuts when stop rules apply](rules/cutting/stop-rules.md).** Revise a cut if it leaves a paragraph without needed explanation or support, or breaks the paragraph's [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md). Merge the paragraph with an adjacent one that makes the same point, or restore the missing material and connections. Before cutting a confusing passage, check for a missing step or [referent](rules/prose/referents.md). Restore it only from elsewhere in the draft, or flag it if the draft lacks it. If a reader outside the subfield cannot follow the shortened passage, restore the needed context and steps instead of cutting further. These stop rules override the length target. Never meet a target by adding negative `\vspace`, reducing font sizes, or narrowing figures. Report the remaining excess instead.

**[Recheck claims, the argument, and references after cutting](rules/cutting/post-cut-check.md).** After cutting, check that each remaining claim keeps its evidence, scope, and hedges under the [verb ladder](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md). If you moved supporting evidence, refer precisely to its new location. Weaken a claim only when the remaining evidence supports less, not merely because the evidence moved. Reread the problem, gap, approach, findings, and implications, and restore any reasoning steps the cut removed. Finally, repair references to cut text or nonexistent appendix sections, cite every remaining figure and table, and define each term before its first use.

## Prose

**[Explain ideas in the order readers need](rules/prose/explanation-order.md).** State the main idea before explaining how it works. State a finding and its interpretation before supporting evidence. Put a section's claim in its first paragraph, and open each paragraph as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md) directs. Define unfamiliar terms before using them. If readers need a later explanation to understand an earlier sentence, move that explanation earlier. You may reorder sentences within a paragraph and paragraphs within a section, but flag moves between sections. Within a sentence, follow [old before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md) and [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).

**[Apply the deletion test without changing meaning](rules/prose/deletion-test.md).** Apply the deletion test: delete a word or clause only if it adds nothing and the sentence remains as easy to understand. Any deletion, replacement, or restructuring must keep the [structural words](rules/prose/first-reading.md) readers need on the first reading. Preserve who acts, what each actor does, what each word refers to, and whether something is possible, required, or actual. Also keep definitions, logical relations, distinctions, the cases covered, and the author's stance. Follow the [claim rules](rules/claims/scope.md) to any substantive change. If you are unsure whether an edit changes the meaning, keep the original and flag the ambiguity.

### Paragraphs

**[Develop one point per paragraph](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md).** Develop one point per paragraph, and state it as a specific claim in the first sentence instead of [announcing](rules/prose/signposts.md) the topic. Allow brief setup only when readers cannot understand the claim without context: give at most one sentence of context from the draft, then state the claim. A results paragraph may open with the specific question it tests if the next sentence answers it, followed by the evidence. A descriptive or procedural paragraph may open with its first fact or step. Keep both sides of a comparison in one paragraph when they support one point. Split a paragraph where its point changes, and merge short paragraphs only when they develop the same point.

**[Use lists when readers need separate items](rules/prose/list-structure.md).** Use a list for items readers must distinguish or follow one by one. If bullets make an argument but hide reasons or connections between claims, rewrite them as connected prose. Remove item numbers that imply an unsupported order or ranking, unless the text refers to them. Flag any change to a list of contributions or objectives instead of making it.

**[Use connecting words only for relations the draft supports](rules/prose/transitions.md).** Use a connecting word such as "therefore" or "however" only for a relation the draft supports. Replace "as", "since", or "while" when it leaves time, cause, or contrast ambiguous. Use "conversely" only when the second statement reverses the first. Remove a redundant connecting word, but keep one that readers need. Flag "therefore" if the preceding statements do not support the conclusion. Follow the [causal-evidence rule](rules/claims/causal-language.md) for causal words.

**[Finish one topic before starting another](rules/prose/topic-order.md).** Finish discussing one topic before starting another, and repair any unexplained return to an earlier topic. In a comparison organized by named properties, each property can be the topic; keep that structure instead of regrouping the text by method (see Details). Reorder sentences and paragraphs within a section when needed, but flag changes to section order.

**[Check sentence openings with the topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md).** When individual sentences are clear but readers cannot follow the paragraph, list each sentence's opening words. That list is the topic string. Check that these openings advance the paragraph's point rather than merely repeating words. Consecutive sentences may keep the same topic, and a new term at a sentence's end need not recur unless the text promises to explain it. If most sentences start with new material, rewrite them to start with [old information](rules/prose/old-before-new.md). Next, check the logical relation between adjacent sentences, such as contrast, consequence, or example. If a relation is missing, supply it from the draft or flag the gap. If two adjacent paragraphs could swap places without other edits, add a [summary naming their parallel items](rules/prose/direct-content.md) when they present parallel items, or state how the second depends on the first.

### Information flow within a sentence

**[Put old information before new](rules/prose/old-before-new.md).** Open each sentence with old information: a term from the preceding text, or a part, property, or alternative that readers can infer from it. A concept known in the field is not old information until the text introduces it. Among several old items, open with the one that the sentence discusses, or the most recent one if they are equally relevant. When changing voice or clause order, keep the paragraph's topic as the main subject. Keep useful passives under [alternative sentence orders](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).

**[Put important new information in the stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md).** Put important new information in the stress position, the end of a sentence or independent clause. If a qualifier such as "in these tests" follows the main point, move the qualifier to the front instead of deleting it. If several important items share one ending, split the sentence so that each gets its own ending. Check whether a paragraph's first sentence ends with the topic that the paragraph develops. When joining or subordinating clauses, keep the relative emphasis of findings and qualifications. Move an essential qualification out of parentheses if they make it look optional. Keep each scope condition with the claim it limits. Follow [pause punctuation](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) when using colons or semicolons.

**[Keep old information first in passives, fronting, and inversions](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).** In a passive with a by-phrase, keep the subject at least as familiar as the actor in that phrase. A fronted phrase before the subject, or an inversion that puts the verb before the subject ("Closest to our work is X"), must begin with old information. Otherwise, use ordinary subject-verb-object order, which may open with new information when doing so is clearer. Keep a passive, "there is", or "it ... that" construction when it introduces new information without making that information the subject.

### Sentence structure

**[Put actors in subjects and actions in verbs](rules/prose/character-action.md).** When a phrase pairs a weak verb with a nominalization ("perform an evaluation of", "lead to an improvement in"), or when a clause contains several nominalizations, make the actor the subject and the action the verb. Preserve every action. If the draft never states who performs an action, flag the missing actor. Keep a nominalization that names a technique ("quantization") or a recurring concept, refers to the previous sentence ("This reduction in noise ..."), replaces an awkward "the fact that" clause, or appears alone in a clear sentence. When an inanimate subject takes a verb of intending, thinking, perceiving, feeling, remembering, or eating ("hopes", "forgets", "swallows"), replace the verb with what literally happens. Keep established technical usage (see Details), and keep a component, instrument, or organism as the subject of an operation it performs. A result or figure may be the subject of a [reporting verb](rules/claims/verb-ladder.md) ("the results suggest"). Follow [voice](rules/conventions/active-voice.md) and [information flow](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md).

**[Make every sentence clear on the first reading](rules/prose/first-reading.md).** Rewrite any sentence that makes readers reread it. Length alone is not a defect. Put subjects next to verbs and verbs next to their objects or complements. Shorten a long opening phrase or clause. Move a long subject clause or interruption toward the end unless it states old information, and keep short, familiar asides. Rewrite strings of three or more nouns and long modifier chains, counting an established technical name as one noun. Read from left to right for words that mislead at first, such as "and" or "or" joining the wrong items. Make clear which noun a relative clause or verb-form phrase describes. Keep or restore articles and "that" when needed to prevent misreading, except in short, predictable clauses or after "we have" (see Details). Put "only" next to what it restricts. Put a condition before its claim unless it is short and clear at the end. Give a long condition its own sentence, and list three or more steps or conditions instead of packing them into one sentence. If a sentence still packs in too many concepts, follow [definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).

**[Give parallel ideas the same grammatical form without changing their meaning](rules/prose/parallel-form.md).** Give ideas with the same logical role the same grammatical form, without renaming unlike objects or dropping conditions merely to force symmetry. When "and" or "or" joins items and it is unclear which modifiers and verbs apply to each item, repeat words or split the sentence (see Details). Compare things of the same kind, restoring an omitted quantity or verb when needed. When joining items as equals hides a causal or qualifying relation, state the relation the draft supports. Keep clear natural variation, and follow [list structure](rules/prose/list-structure.md).

**[Do not open a sentence with "And", "But", "So", or "Plus"](rules/prose/sentence-initial-conjunctions.md).** Join a sentence beginning with "And", "But", "So", or "Plus" to the previous sentence, or replace the opening word. Keep a contrast as a contrast, and mark a consequence only when the draft supports it. Use "Finally" for the last item of a series, not "In addition", "Moreover", or "Furthermore", which add support. For a cited objection, follow [prior-work attribution](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md). Keep clear uses of these conjunctions within a sentence.

**[Read the sentence aloud](rules/prose/read-aloud.md).** Read the sentence aloud, or imagine doing so, to find where you hesitate, cannot tell what to stress, or miss a connection. Fix the structure and state the meaning literally, because punctuation alone may not be enough. A sentence read smoothly can still be unclear if it relies on pauses, stress, or background knowledge. Do not add a [writerly aside](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md) telling readers how to read it.

### Content instead of promises

**[State the content directly](rules/prose/direct-content.md).** When a sentence only promises later content or calls a topic complex, replace it with the draft's content, or flag the gap. Delete trailing promises such as "and we discuss each below" when the content is already present. Keep a summary of the main idea and any count whose items follow immediately ("We make four observations. First, ..."). Keep statements of real uncertainty, a real dispute, an unresolved definition, or a [limitation](rules/claims/limitations.md). To test a sentence, cover the rest of the paragraph and ask whether the sentence alone says anything readers can understand or check.

**[State findings instead of how they are reported](rules/prose/reporting-policies.md).** When a sentence only states a reporting policy, such as "we report both metrics", replace it with the draft's conclusions, measurements, and outcomes. Flag any missing results. Keep a sentence that describes an actual reporting or analysis procedure when it explains what the authors did rather than replacing a result.

**[State the content a signpost announces](rules/prose/signposts.md).** A signpost announces what the text discusses or where to find it. Replace it with the content it announces and, if needed, a location reference. Keep references to evidence or proofs, [genuine transitions and summaries](rules/cutting/protected-sentences.md), and a roadmap of at most two sentences at the end of an introduction or chapter opening. Delete announcements that state no content ("Section 7 concludes"), roadmaps within a section that repeat its subheadings, and signposts that take more effort to understand than they save. In a kept roadmap, correct mismatches with the actual sections or items when the draft resolves them unambiguously (see Details). Flag conflicting categories and any revision that would change the section order or contributions.

**[State the reasons for a choice](rules/prose/choice-reasons.md).** Unless the omitted items are obvious, replace "for various reasons" or a list's final "etc." with the draft's reasons or items, or flag the gap. When a stated claim depends on an unexplained method choice, give the draft's reason or flag the missing reason. Do not ask authors to justify routine choices whose role is clear. A reason for a choice is not evidence that the choice caused a result. For causal claims, follow [causal language](rules/claims/causal-language.md).

### Concrete quantities and operations

**[Replace empty verbs with specific changes or operations](rules/prose/empty-verbs.md).** When "affects", "varies", or "has implications for" reports an unspecified change, state its direction and size. When "provides", "enables", or "allows" states only an outcome, state the operation that produces it. Flag missing measurements or operations. Keep wording that already explains the change or capability. For an explicitly untested hypothesis, state the hypothesized direction without requiring a measured size.

**[Name the measured quantity instead of an umbrella noun](rules/prose/umbrella-nouns.md).** Replace an umbrella noun such as "performance" or "quality" with the specific measured quantity, such as accuracy, yield, sensitivity, or energy use. Keep a defined collective label when the statement applies to all quantities it covers.

**[Replace vague comparisons with measurements](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md).** Replace a vague comparative such as "more" or "faster" with the reported measurement, and name what it is compared with. Lead with the [headline number](rules/story/headline-number.md), describe the overall pattern, and refer to the table for remaining values. A label such as "large" needs a stated comparison or threshold and cannot replace the measurement. Do not calculate unreported ratios (see Details).

**[Cut words that add only emphasis](rules/prose/intensifiers.md).** Cut words that add only emphasis, such as "very" and "extremely", or replace them with a [measurement](rules/prose/quantified-comparatives.md) from the draft. Treat "essentially" and "largely" as [softeners](rules/claims/hedges.md); preserve their scope or uncertainty unless the draft supplies a precise replacement. Delete "Note that" and similar prompts unless they prevent a likely misreading. Keep "obviously", "clearly", or "trivially" only before a proof step routine for readers outside the subfield. Remove them from empirical claims, and flag any unexplained nonroutine step.

### Plain wording

**[Say what literally happens](rules/prose/literal-wording.md).** Name the actual object and the actual operation performed on it or by it. Replace "utilize" or "leverage" with "use" when the meaning is the same. When an abstract word such as "approach", "framework", "strategy", or "issue" stands for something more specific, name that specific thing; keep it when defined and reused as shorthand. Name an object before describing its role. Rewrite figurative verbs, nouns used as temporary verbs ("we spreadsheet the readings"), and unusual word pairs that readers must decode, following the [parts-of-speech rule](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md). Keep established technical terms, verbs, and word combinations. Replace an opaque phrasal verb, such as "dial back", only with an exact equivalent. Keep an analogy whose correspondence the draft makes clear, replace decorative or misleading imagery with literal wording, and never add a new analogy. For slogan-like sentences, follow the [aphorism rule](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md). For unfamiliar terms, ambiguous prepositions, and "replace" or "substitute", see Details.

**[Use logical words unambiguously](rules/prose/logical-words.md).** Replace ambiguous "any" with "every" or "at least one", whichever the draft means; "some" requires only one case and leaves open whether every case qualifies. If "all" with a plural noun could suggest that objects share one value, use "each" with a singular noun instead ("each sample is held at its own temperature"). Rewrite a negative statement as a positive one only when both statements hold in exactly the same cases, including values equal to a threshold. Keep a deliberate denial as a denial. Do not replace a negated property, including monotonicity, with its apparent opposite ("not connected" does not mean "every vertex is isolated"). When "can" or "should" could mean capability, possibility, or obligation, use the meaning the draft establishes, or flag the ambiguity; also follow [hedges](rules/claims/hedges.md) and [performed actions](rules/conventions/performed-actions.md). Replace an ambiguous slash in prose with the words it stands for, such as "or" or "and", while keeping standard abbreviations and notation. Preserve whether a list is complete; "includes" alone does not imply omitted members. Keep "then" after an "if" clause whose conclusion is a formula or technical claim. When "contains" could mean membership or subset inclusion, state which. Use the checks in Details for nested conditions, negation scope, or an "if" that frames a comparison.

**[Spell out definitions and procedures](rules/prose/definitions-procedures.md).** Explain each metric, rule, and procedure readers rely on, including how to interpret or reproduce its results. A definition must name the kind of thing and its distinguishing traits; expanding an acronym is not enough. Accompany formal definitions readers need with plain explanations; for related concepts, state both their shared category and their differences. When definitions compete, state which one the paper adopts and the draft's reason; neither invent competing definitions nor drop ones the draft mentions. Do not flag standard steps covered by a precise citation, but keep every modification and the explanation readers need at that point. When a procedure only lists steps, state its purpose from the draft, or flag the missing purpose. Check that worked examples match the stated method or definition, and flag mismatches rather than changing either. Apply the domain checks in Details (for algorithms, numerical work, experiments, data and samples, or human participants) only during a story check or an overclaiming check, or when a result depends on an unstated setting. Delete or move details needed for reproduction only where the [cutting rules](rules/cutting/sentence-dependencies.md) allow it. For an unclear explanation or an example without a stated purpose, see Details.

**[Make clear what each word refers to](rules/prose/referents.md).** Each pronoun, demonstrative, comparative, and shorthand noun phrase must point to one referent from the text alone. If a baseline or previous run is ambiguous, name it with the draft's label and reuse that name. Restore context removed by an edit, or flag the missing connection. For openings under headings, see Details. When adding a noun after an ambiguous "this", follow the [rule on ambiguous "this"](rules/prose/ambiguous-this.md).

**[Add an accurate noun after an ambiguous "this"](rules/prose/ambiguous-this.md).** When "this", "that", or "it" could refer to several nouns or claims, add a noun naming the supported referent. If the draft leaves the referent unclear, flag the ambiguity instead of choosing one. The noun must preserve the statement's status and evaluation: a conjecture is not a finding, and a change is not necessarily an improvement. A bare "This" may open an interpretation like "This suggests that" after one unambiguous claim.

**[State claims and reasons in complete, literal sentences](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md).** Replace conversational idioms such as "Call it a wash" and editorial commands with complete, literal statements of the claim and its reason. Use only reasons the draft supplies; flag a missing reason. Keep a deliberately short sentence when it is complete and literal. Preserve the author's supported stance under [author voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).

## Conventions

When a convention in this section conflicts with general writing advice, follow the convention.

### Voice and tense

**[Use active voice and keep the actor consistent](rules/conventions/active-voice.md).** By default, use active "we" for authors' actions and decisions. Do not use "we" for the research community or people in general; "we" may include the reader in derivations and proofs. In method descriptions, use either the method or "we" throughout; always use "we" for design choices. Keep a single author's consistent "I" or "we". Keep passives when the actor is obvious, irrelevant, or unknown; when they put [old information first](rules/prose/passives-fronting-inversions.md); when a long actor belongs at the end; or for clear passive modifiers ("the samples selected by technicians"). Do not assign an action to the authors when the draft omits the actor.

**[State performed actions directly](rules/conventions/performed-actions.md).** When the draft shows an action was performed, replace "can", "aims to", or "seeks to" with a direct verb. Keep these words for a capability the study did not use and a goal the authors have not reached; removing them would [change the claim](rules/claims/unsupported-labels.md).

**[Match tense to the time frame](rules/conventions/tense.md).** Use present tense for the paper's contents ("Section 4 shows"), results, method, setup, established facts, and prior work that still applies. Preserve a draft's consistent past tense for experiments, and use past tense for events outside the described procedure. Use future tense only for uncompleted plans, never for later sections. Keep tense consistent within each time frame. Prefer simple forms unless perfect tense marks current relevance ("has tested") or an event before another past event ("had tested"). Keep past tense for reported findings ("Lee found that the alloy was weaker") and hypothetical situations ("if the sample were larger"). Do not turn a general research recommendation into the authors' own promise. For relative dates and past gaps, see Details.

### Terminology

**[Use one term per concept](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md).** Use one term for each concept throughout the text, including captions and figure labels. Do not alternate terms such as "device", "sensor", and "system" for variety. When the draft uses multiple names for one concept, replace them with one term. Keep different names only when they mark distinct concepts, such as different types, scopes, or measured quantities. In the same or the next sentence, a clear pronoun ("it") or bare demonstrative ("this") may refer back to the term. Flag any inconsistency that prose edits cannot fix, such as a mismatched figure label. For parallel cases and category nouns, see Details.

**[Avoid using one technical term as both a noun and a verb](rules/conventions/parts-of-speech.md).** Where possible, use each technical term as only one part of speech. When a sentence uses a term such as "sample" or "test" as both a noun and a verb, forcing rereading, replace the verb with an ordinary verb or split the sentence (see Details).

**[Spell out acronyms and introduce few new ones](rules/conventions/acronyms.md).** Spell out an acronym where repeated use begins, and use the full term for isolated earlier mentions. Define it separately in the abstract, the body, and each major section where it returns. Prefer the full term unless the acronym is standard or shortens a long, recurring name. Spell out an acronym that appears once or twice, shares letters with another common term in the field ("LR"), or saves no syllables read aloud ("TS" for time step). Flag an unfamiliar title acronym (see Details) and propose a spelled-out title, but leave the title unchanged and define the acronym in the body. Flag opaque coined labels rather than renaming them. If an acronym you would expand also appears in tables, figures, or code, flag the expansion instead.

**[Spell each word the same way throughout](rules/conventions/spelling.md).** Use US or UK spelling consistently. For terms with two accepted forms, such as "dataset" and "data set", follow venue requirements; if the venue specifies neither form, use the draft's more frequent variant.

**[Remind readers what shorthand means after a gap](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md).** When shorthand or a symbol returns after a long gap or section boundary, remind readers of its name and either its definition or where it was defined. Phrase this as a reminder ("Recall that $r$ denotes...") rather than a new definition. Replace ambiguous labels such as "the rate" with the original full name. When referring to a distant display by number alone, add its name, as in "the energy balance (9)".

**[State what ambiguous or suggestive terms mean](rules/conventions/suggestive-terms.md).** State what a term means when the draft invents a suggestive name (an "intelligent" coating), uses a technical word loosely ("exponential" for rapid growth), or uses a word with several meanings in the field ("stability"). Flag an undefined invented term and suggest a literal alternative. Keep established technical wording such as "the enzyme recognizes its substrate", but explain the literal operation at first use. When an ordinary word collides with a nearby technical term ("a normal sample" near "normal distribution"), replace the ordinary word and keep the technical term. For intentions attributed to systems and unfamiliar categories, see Details.

### Mathematics

**[Define each symbol by its first use and give it one meaning](rules/conventions/symbol-definitions.md).** Define every symbol, at least informally, at or before its first use, and [refresh the definition after a gap](rules/conventions/shorthand-reminders.md). Give each symbol one meaning and each object one symbol. Flag conflicting meanings, unused definitions, easily confused symbols, dense notation, and functions equated with values; do not rename symbols yourself. Keep local definitions near the passages that use them. When definitions of symbols used throughout the paper are scattered, move them into an existing notation section only if the request [allows relocation](rules/cutting/idea-cuts.md); otherwise, flag them. Never create a new notation section. See Details for notation improvements and distinguishing functions from values.

**[Write mathematics as grammatical prose](rules/conventions/math-prose.md).** Read each formula as part of its sentence so that words and mathematics form a grammatical sentence. Begin sentences and clauses with words rather than bare symbols, and spell out sentence-initial numbers. Separate adjacent formulas with words, including across sentence boundaries. Except in work on logic, write quantifiers and relations in prose as words. End a displayed formula with a period when the sentence ends, or a comma when it continues. Put a colon before a display only after a complete clause. A trailing "where" clause may define symbols, but put any definition needed to understand a claim before that claim. See Details for examples and LaTeX paragraph breaks.

**[Explain why an equation or definition is needed](rules/conventions/formal-material-purpose.md).** Flag any central equation or definition whose purpose the draft never states; do not infer one. If the explanation appears later in the same paragraph or section, move it before the equation or definition. If it appears only in another section, flag the proposed move. Follow [formal support](rules/claims/evidence-kind.md) when an application depends on unstated conditions.

**[Use the correct type for operators, subscripts, and signs](rules/conventions/math-typography.md).** Use upright type for multi-letter operators and word subscripts, but keep variable subscripts in italic type. Use × for multiplication and multiplicative factors ("3× the yield"), and use minus signs for negative values. Fix LaTeX typography when mathematical meaning stays unchanged. Flag, rather than make, any change to symbols, grouping, delimiters, or operator meaning, and preserve code syntax. See Details for LaTeX commands and ambiguous notation.

**[Number referenced and central displays](rules/conventions/display-numbering.md).** Number every display that the text refers to and every central display; leave intermediate steps unnumbered. See Details.

**[Make theorem statements self-contained](rules/conventions/theorem-statements.md).** Introduce a theorem, lemma, or algorithm with a complete sentence, and use a colon only after a complete clause. Make the formal statement self-contained: state each object's type and define every symbol before use. Keep motivation and informal comments outside the statement. If hypotheses run for several lines, flag the statement and suggest extracting them into named conditions defined before it (see Details).

**[Open each proof with its strategy and state when each goal is met](rules/conventions/proof-strategy.md).** Open each proof with its strategy and mark subgoals as they are completed. Make clear whether each statement is assumed, defined, established, or deferred to a later proof. Add strategy or progress statements only when identifiable in the draft; otherwise, flag the omission. Follow [intensifiers](rules/prose/intensifiers.md) for "clearly" and "it is easy to see". See Details for cases and constructions.

### Citations, figures, and tables

**[Keep citation markers out of subject and object positions](rules/conventions/citation-markers.md).** Do not use a bracketed or parenthetical citation marker as a subject or object. Replace "[8] introduces" with a textual citation such as `\citet{key}`, keeping the citation key. When asserting an established result, shorten "In a recent study, ... showed that X" to "X (citation)". Keep cited authors as the subject when reporting, characterizing, or contrasting a claim that the paper does not assert, following [prior work](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md). Place each citation beside its clause when the draft identifies that link; otherwise, flag a trailing cluster that obscures which source supports which claim. Flag any passage that leaves unclear where a source's material begins or ends. For package commands, author formatting, and multi-sentence attribution, see Details.

**[Flag missing citations and citation keys absent from the references](rules/conventions/missing-citations.md).** Flag a technical claim presented as prior knowledge without an identifiable source, as in "It is well known that". Also flag any citation key missing from the supplied references. Common knowledge needs no citation. Do not delete an existing citation merely because it seems unnecessary. Preserve secondhand wording such as "as cited in". Follow [vague attribution](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md) for "Studies show" and [reference markers](rules/conventions/figure-table-references.md) for "??".

**[Write captions that stand alone and state the finding](rules/conventions/captions.md).** A caption that only names its figure or table is incomplete. Complete it using facts from the citing paragraph, and flag needed details absent from the draft. Identify quantities, units, axes, conditions, visual symbols like colors and line styles, and [error bars](rules/claims/variability.md). State whether higher or lower values are better when that is not obvious. State the supported finding, verify that it matches the body text, and name compared methods or conditions directly instead of by color alone. In tables, flag unexplained blanks, dashes, and columns with mixed or missing units. When a plot specifies axis ranges, dual vertical axes, or a fitted curve's range, flag claims drawn from visual slopes or distances that the scales do not support, and flag any extrapolated value described as measured. An axis displaying only part of its range is not by itself an error.

**[Cite each figure and table in the sentence that states its finding](rules/conventions/figure-table-references.md).** Cite each figure or table where its claim is stated, replacing announcements like "In Figure 6 we show the results". Point to the specific panel or comparison that the draft identifies, using numbered references instead of relative pointers such as "below". When a claim requires an exact value readers can only estimate from a plot, state the draft's value or flag the missing number. Flag unresolved citation or reference markers like "??". For LaTeX commands, capitalization, and float placement, see Details.

### Typography

**[Hyphenate compound modifiers before nouns](rules/conventions/hyphens.md).** Hyphenate a compound modifier before its noun, as in "real-time monitoring". Leave the modifier unhyphenated after the noun ("monitoring runs in real time"). Keep an established field term unhyphenated if the draft consistently writes it without hyphens.

**[Spell out small counts and use numerals for measurements](rules/conventions/number-forms.md).** In prose, spell out a small count before a noun ("two passes"). Use numerals for measurements, quantities with units, mathematical values, numbered names ("Experiment 4"), and parameter settings ("3 cycles"). Follow the venue cutoff if one exists, otherwise the draft's consistent cutoff. Match forms when comparing quantities ("1.5M and 0.2M"); flag any edit that requires rounding or unit conversion.

**[Use "i.e.", "e.g.", and "cf." only for their meanings](rules/conventions/latin-abbreviations.md).** Use "i.e." for "that is" and "e.g." for "for example"; never interchange them. In US style, follow both with a comma; never italicize either. Use "cf." to mean "compare", not "see".

**[Set off non-restrictive clauses with commas](rules/conventions/nonrestrictive-commas.md).** Put commas around a non-restrictive clause, which adds information without restricting the noun. Do not add commas to a restrictive clause just because it starts with "which". Change restrictive "which" to "that" only when the venue requires it. Do not place commas around a symbol naming the preceding noun ("a directed graph G with source s").

**[Use punctuation and type styles only for their standard purposes](rules/conventions/punctuation-typography.md).** Avoid contractions, exclamation marks, and emoji. Use title-case headings only when the venue requires them. Use italics for definitions, not decorative emphasis. When italics mark what a negation denies ("we do not heat the *lid*"), state the contrast in words if clear, or keep the italics and flag the ambiguity. Use quotation marks for quotations, words discussed as words, and unendorsed terms (the cell's "decision"); flag an unendorsed term if the draft never explains it (see Details). Do not quote ordinary words. In exact literals, such as commands and outputs, preserve every character; place sentence punctuation outside unless it belongs to the literal.

**[Give units and separate them from numbers with a non-breaking space](rules/conventions/units.md).** Give units for every measurement and axis, separated from numbers by a non-breaking space. If a unit or its basis is ambiguous ("ppm" by mass or volume; "Mb" as megabits or megabytes), state the intended meaning from the draft or flag the ambiguity. For binary prefixes and durations, see Details. Never change a value to normalize units.

**[Preserve LaTeX paragraphs and comments when changing line breaks](rules/conventions/latex-boundaries.md).** When changing line breaks in LaTeX source, compare the result with the original. Keep paragraph breaks (blank lines) where they were, and keep each `%` comment covering the same text as before (see Details). Undo any paragraph or comment boundary changes caused by reflowing.

## Deslopping

Patterns common in AI-written prose fall into two groups:

1. **Blacklisted patterns.** Delete every instance, even a single one. The three-instance threshold below does not apply.
2. **Tolerable patterns.** Edit a single instance when the edit makes the sentence clearer. Rewrite a whole passage only when it contains three or more instances of tolerable patterns.

**[Preserve the author's voice](rules/deslopping/author-voice.md).** Keep the draft's [varied rhythm](rules/deslopping/repetitive-syntax.md) and the authors' genuine opinions and reactions. Never add opinions, reactions, emphasis, sentence fragments, opposing positions, or unstated details. After rewriting, [verify that the meaning is unchanged](rules/prose/deletion-test.md). Follow conventions on [voice](rules/conventions/active-voice.md), [tense](rules/conventions/tense.md), and [terminology](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md). Never treat first-person "we", present tense for the text or prior work, or repeated technical terms as defects merely because generated prose uses them. [Match confidence to evidence](rules/claims/scope.md) without adding hedges.

### Blacklisted patterns

**[Delete em dashes](rules/deslopping/em-dashes.md).** Delete every em dash, including `---` in LaTeX, and every en dash used as a pause. Replace the dash with a colon or semicolon only when the [pause punctuation](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md) rule allows it. Use parentheses only when they do not make an essential qualification look optional under [stress position](rules/prose/stress-position.md). Otherwise, rewrite the sentence without the pause and keep its emphasis. Keep hyphens in compound words and en dashes in numeric ranges or two-person names (see Details).

**[Delete conversational replies left by a chatbot](rules/deslopping/chatbot-phrases.md).** Delete conversational phrases left by an AI assistant, such as "Certainly!", except in analyzed text (see Details).

**[Delete generic openings and closings](rules/deslopping/generic-openings-closings.md).** Delete opening or closing filler that could fit any paper in the field (such as "The future looks bright"), along with popularity claims that sound factual ("There has been much recent interest in X"). Merely naming the topic does not make a sentence necessary. If the draft gives a specific reason the topic matters, use that reason instead. If readers need background first, follow [brief setup](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md).

**[Remove stray citation codes and placeholders, and flag missing information](rules/deslopping/generation-artifacts.md).** Remove chat citation codes, such as `citeturn0search0` and `contentReference[oaicite:...]`, and unfilled placeholders, such as "[Insert citation]". Keep the surrounding claim and flag the missing information. For URLs with `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, leave the URL unchanged and flag the parameter for removal.

### Tolerable patterns

**[Apply prose rules to ornate words, actions written as nouns, counts, and lists](rules/deslopping/prose-rule-patterns.md).** Apply the prose rules to [ornate words](rules/prose/literal-wording.md) such as "delve", "underscore", "showcase", and "pivotal", clauses that [express several actions as nouns](rules/prose/character-action.md), [deferred counts](rules/prose/direct-content.md), and bulleted arguments under [list structure](rules/prose/list-structure.md). Follow each linked page's exceptions. Keep precise technical terms, useful actions written as nouns, and counts whose items follow immediately.

**[Expand compressed wording that slows the first reading](rules/deslopping/compressed-constructions.md).** Check for [inanimate subjects given human actions](rules/prose/character-action.md) ("the model hopes"), [nouns used as verbs and unusual word pairs](rules/prose/literal-wording.md), and [noun strings, omitted "that" or articles, and long gaps between subject and verb](rules/prose/first-reading.md). Expand compressed wording only when it slows the first reading, following each linked page's exceptions. Keep clear short forms and established technical terms.

**[Repair pause punctuation between a claim and its evidence](rules/deslopping/pause-punctuation.md).** Check every colon and semicolon for a vague claim followed by its evidence, as in "Annealing helps: conductivity rises by 40%". Check all marks, not only those that replaced an em dash. Read the clause before the mark on its own. If it states no specific fact, join the claim to its evidence in words (such as "as shown by") or delete the vague clause when the evidence states the whole claim; replacing the mark with a period does not fix the vague clause. If the first clause states a specific fact, keep a semicolon before a related fact or a colon before an explanation or example. For colons that introduce lists, displayed formulas under [mathematical punctuation](rules/conventions/math-prose.md), quoted definitions, or labels, see Details. In a phrase after a comma, restore any missing verb, changing "adds 3 ms, below the limit" to "adds 3 ms, which is below the limit". Keep appositives, clear participial phrases, and parallel phrases (see Details).

**[Replace narration of the thinking process with conclusions and evidence](rules/deslopping/reasoning-leakage.md).** Replace narration of the thinking process, such as "Let us think step by step", with the conclusion and evidence. Keep mathematical setup, proof steps, and substantive reasoning (see Details).

**[Describe the work without narrating manuscript revisions](rules/deslopping/revision-narration.md).** In the paper, replace sentences that describe manuscript revisions (diff-anchored prose), such as "In this revised version, we clarify" or "As suggested by the reviewer", with the scientific content directly. Revision history belongs in the response letter or change log. Keep all added explanations, evidence, and justifications, and keep substantive comparisons between method versions when part of the study.

**[Name the cited source when an attribution is vague](rules/deslopping/vague-attribution.md).** Replace vague attributions such as "studies show", "experts believe", or "X has been shown to" with the named source only when the draft cites a source for that claim; otherwise, flag the missing attribution. An explicitly hypothetical objection that the paper poses and answers needs no citation to identify an objector (see Details). Follow the [prior-work rules](rules/story/prior-work-comparisons.md) to make clear who holds each position.

**[Replace negative parallelism with the positive claim](rules/deslopping/negative-parallelism.md).** Replace negative parallelism, such as "not only … but also", "It is not just X, it is Y", or paired sentences ("The headline is not X. The real story is Y"), with the positive claim when the rejected alternative was invented for emphasis. State supported results and measurements directly. Keep contrasts that exclude real alternatives ("we measure X, not Y").

**[Make both sides of a false concession specific](rules/deslopping/false-concession.md).** When a concession such as "While X has advanced, Y remains a challenge" makes no concrete comparison, replace each vague side with the draft's specific finding. Flag any side the draft leaves unspecified. Keep concessions in which both sides are specific and supported.

**[Revise rule-of-three lists written for rhythm](rules/deslopping/rule-of-three.md).** Revise a three-part list such as "faster, cheaper, and more robust" when written for rhythm rather than to state three distinct, supported items. Remove empty or redundant words, and flag unsupported attributes. Never drop a substantive item just to change the count. Keep three items when the subject requires them.

**[Replace false ranges with the settings tested](rules/deslopping/false-ranges.md).** When the endpoints of "from X to Y" do not lie on an ordered scale, the phrase implies breadth without defining a range. Replace it with the draft's tested settings. Keep true ranges along an ordered scale. Never shorten a longer list to its endpoints.

**[Delete transition chains that announce more text](rules/deslopping/transition-chains.md).** Delete repeated openings such as "Moreover", "Furthermore", and "Additionally" that merely announce more text. Keep transitions that show a [valid logical relation](rules/prose/transitions.md). Do not replace additive sentences with unsupported causal, temporal, or contrastive connections.

**[Replace needless substitutes for "is", "has", or action verbs](rules/deslopping/copula-avoidance.md).** Replace phrases such as "serves as", "acts as", "represents", or "boasts" with "is" or "has" when the meaning stays the same, or turn the following action noun into a verb ("serves as a temperature monitor" becomes "monitors temperature"). Keep these verbs when they express a role distinct from identity, such as a compound acting as a catalyst, or have a precise technical meaning.

**[Delete or replace empty commentary in trailing "-ing" phrases](rules/deslopping/trailing-ing.md).** Delete trailing "-ing" phrases, such as "highlighting its importance" or "reflecting broader trends", that add no checkable claim, or replace them with specific claims the draft supports. Keep substantive phrases, but clarify their attachment or meaning under the [first-reading rule](rules/prose/first-reading.md) when ambiguous.

**[Delete sentences that only label results important or surprising](rules/deslopping/self-labeling.md).** Delete sentences that merely label an earlier result important or surprising ("This is the key result"). If the draft explains why the result matters, organize the passage around that reason. Otherwise, delete the label without adding an explanation. Keep a word of authorial stance that introduces a claim ("Interestingly,").

**[Replace rhetorical questions used as transitions with their answers](rules/deslopping/rhetorical-questions.md).** When a question only introduces the next topic, delete it and make its answer the paragraph's first sentence. Never open a paragraph with a question that merely links paragraphs, even if the next sentence answers it. Keep a research question that states what the text tests, placing its answer as the [paragraph rule](rules/prose/paragraph-development.md) directs. Keep a mid-paragraph question only if it turns preceding details into a puzzle and the next sentence answers it. For questions that presume agreement, see Details.

**[Vary sentence structure to show how ideas connect](rules/deslopping/repetitive-syntax.md).** Look for consecutive sentences or paragraphs that share an opening, clause pattern, or length. Using the [topic string](rules/prose/topic-string.md), join sentences through relations stated in the draft: a shared object, condition, time, or consequence. Let those relations decide sentence structure instead of varying lengths at random. Do not invent causes, sequences, or contrasts. Preserve [parallel wording for parallel ideas](rules/prose/parallel-form.md) and [consistent technical terms](rules/conventions/term-consistency.md). Keep sentences complete and literal without adding [writerly asides](rules/prose/writerly-asides.md), contrasts, or emphasis. Keep the original structure whenever varying it reduces clarity.

**[State what a result implies without exaggerating its importance](rules/deslopping/significance-inflation.md).** Delete empty grand-trend phrases such as "marking a pivotal moment" or "paving the way", or replace them with the supported implication and its conditions. If an implication lacks evidence, keep the claim and flag the missing support.

**[Replace slogan-like analogies with literal explanations](rules/deslopping/aphorisms.md).** Replace a slogan-like analogy ("X is the Y of Z") with the mechanism stated in the draft. Delete any slogan that makes no substantive claim. If an analogy asserts an unexplained mechanism, keep the analogy and flag the missing explanation.

**[Make reported failures as noticeable as successes](rules/deslopping/relentless-positivity.md).** When a draft presents every result as a success, give reported failures and counterevidence comparable prominence, and [match each claim to its evidence](rules/claims/scope.md). Never invent a failure, add a balancing caveat, or hedge a supported finding to make the tone seem cautious.
